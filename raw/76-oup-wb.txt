 Abstract
 Numerous large language models (LLMs) are marketed for use as creativity support tools, despite several studies showing that using an LLM as a creative partner narrows creative outputs. However, these studies only consider the effects of interacting with a single LLM on specific creativity tasks, begging the question of whether narrowed creativity stems from using a particular LLM—with an arguably limited range of outputs—or from using LLMs in general . To test this, we elicit creative responses from many humans and LLMs using standardized creativity tasks and compare population-level response diversity. We find that LLM responses mirror other LLM responses far more than humans do other humans, even after controlling for key confounding variables. This finding adds a new dimension to the ongoing discussion about creativity and LLMs. If today’s LLMs behave similarly, using them as creative partners—regardless of the model used—may drive users toward similar “creative” outputs.
 AI , creativity , homogeneity 
 Significance Statement 
 Motivated by prior work showing that particular language models (e.g., ChatGPT) can produce homogeneous creative outputs, we elicit creative responses from humans and a broad set of large language models (LLMs) using multiple standardized creativity tasks and compare the population-level diversity of responses. We find that LLM responses are much more similar to other LLM responses than human responses are to each other, even after experimentally controlling for response structure and other key variables. This finding of significant homogeneity in creative outputs across LLMs enriches the ongoing discussion about creativity and LLMs. Not only do particular LLMs produce homogeneous creative outputs—all LLMs produce similar , homogeneous creative outputs. This study highlights a potential danger of relying on LLMs as creative partners.
 Introduction
 Large language models (LLMs) have moved out of research labs and into our everyday lives. Given their advanced abilities to generate text and respond to prompts, LLMs are often marketed as creativity support tools that allow users to write drafts, edit documents, and generate new ideas with ease ( 1–4 ). Consumers have responded eagerly to these suggestions. According to a 2024 survey by Adobe, over half of Americans have used generative AI tools like LLMs as creative partners for brainstorming, drafting written content, creating images, or writing code. An overwhelming majority of LLM users surveyed believe these models will help them be more creative ( 5 ). Already, many studies have shown how AI models, such as ChatGPT, outperform humans in standard lab-based assessments of creativity ( 6–12 ), lending credibility to this view.
 Creativity is typically defined as the ability to generate ideas that are both novel and useful ( 13 , 14 ). Extensive cognitive research on human creativity over the past few decades has focused on the cognitive capacities that facilitate creativity, such as attention, memory, intelligence, cognitive control, and associative thinking ( 15–20 ). A majority of such cognitive and neuroscientific research focuses on divergent thinking, the ability to generate multiple solutions to a given problem ( 16 , 21 , 22 ). Divergent thinking is commonly measured via verbal creativity tasks such as the alternative uses task (AUT; ( 23 )), where participants are given common objects and asked to list possible alternative uses. Traditionally, performance in such tasks was assessed via subjective ratings of external judges ( 24 ) or self-report questionnaires ( 25 ), but the introduction of computational tools to creativity assessment have significantly advanced creativity assessment in and outside of the lab ( 26–30 ). Parallel research on creativity as a process emphasizes the importance of associative thinking, the ability to search through one’s memory and combine concepts into novel and useful ideas ( 15 , 31–34 ).
 Regardless of the definition, outsourcing human creativity to LLMs could have unintended consequences and demands further scrutiny ( 35–38 ). One major concern in the study of AI and creativity is whether using AI to aid creative tasks homogenizes creative outputs. Numerous studies have shown evidence of this. For example, Doshi and Hauser ( 39 ) found that writers who used GPT-4 as a creativity support tool produced more creative stories than humans working alone, but that the stories from writers who collaborated with GPT-4 were more similar to each other than were stories from human writers, thus more homogeneous. Similarly, Moon et al. ( 40 ) compared college essays written by humans and GPT models and found that LLM-authored essays contributed fewer new ideas and were more homogeneous than human-authored essays. This phenomenon of LLM-driven content homogenization appears across domains—in research idea generation ( 41 ), essay writing ( 40 ), survey responses ( 42 ), creative ideation ( 43 ), and art ( 44 )—although recent work argues such homogenization may be context-dependent ( 45 ).
 Recent work also showed that when GPT models are evaluated multiple times in associative thinking tasks, their responses overlap, even if each individual response has a higher quantitative-based originality score ( 46 ). Cropley ( 46 ) examined how different LLMs performed on an associative thinking task, the divergent association task (DAT; ( 47 )), compared to humans. In the DAT, participants are required to generate words that are unrelated to each other, assessing disassociative thinking abilities that facilitate creativity ( 31 ). This study revealed that while the LLMs generate more original output than humans in this task, the variability of their responses was lower. Given the significance of variability in human creativity ( 48 , 49 ), such a less variable, homogenized output by the LLMs raises potential concerns regarding the discussion of LLM creativity.
 Most current works comparing human–AI creativity examine a single model (usually an LLM like ChatGPT) and its effect on downstream creative content. However, this begs the question: does AI-driven homogeneity observed in prior studies occur because only a single AI model is studied? It could be reasonably argued that a single LLM must have a limited range of outputs, causing the homogeneity. Perhaps, if researchers employed numerous different AI models, the homogenization effects would vanish. Thus, a critical open question is whether it is the use of specific models in these studies—often GPT variants—that causes observed homogeneity, or if such homogeneity would be observed regardless of the model used .
 Recent work studying feature space alignment in large-scale AI models suggests that using multiple models will not solve the homogenization problem. Numerous studies have proposed ways to measure similarity between AI model feature representations (e.g., their internal way of representing model inputs) ( 50–54 ), since such similarity is believed to indicate overall model similarity. Nascent work has applied similar methods to LLMs and found evidence of “feature universality” across LLMs ( 55 , 56 ). We postulate that such feature space alignment in LLMs may result in homogeneous creative outputs across these models. This would imply that using LLMs as creative partners in general causes output homogeneity, because all LLMs would have limited and similar output ranges.
 The consequences of cross-LLM creative output homogeneity would be significant in the creative space and beyond. Humans who rely on LLMs as creative partners would find their creative outputs remarkably similar to those of other LLM users regardless of the model used, resulting in a collective narrowing of creativity. This is especially critical given a recent study showing that students over-rely on LLM-generated suggestions when generating creative ideas, rather than engaging in more complex cognitive strategies ( 57 ). More broadly, homogeneity among popular LLMs could lead to bias propagation, widespread security vulnerabilities, or other problems ( 58 , 59 ).
 In the current study, we build on prior work showing that using specific LLMs as creative partners in creative thinking tasks narrows the range of creative outputs, to study creative output variability across tasks and across LLMs (Fig. 1 ). We evaluate the diversity of responses to creative prompts across many LLMs using standard creativity tasks, such as the AUT, DAT, and forward flow (FF; measuring associative thinking ( 60 )) and compare this to the diversity of human responses. We specifically focus on verbal creativity tasks that are commonly used in empirical and AI creativity research ( 9 , 31 , 46 ), to generalize across these tasks and provide a comprehensive examination of potential LLM creative homogenization. Our key finding is that LLMs responses are much more similar to each other than are human responses, indicating significant homogeneity across the LLMs we evaluate. This study enriches the current debate surrounding LLMs and creativity, clarifying that it is likely the use of LLMs in general, rather than the use of a specific LLM, that homogenizes creative outputs.

 Results
 Our goal is to measure whether LLMs produce more, less, or equally diverse creative outputs as a group of human participants. We measure this diversity (or variability) in responses by computing the semantic similarity among responses of 102 human participants and 22 LLMs to three standard creativity tasks: the AUT ( 61 ), FF ( 60 ), and DAT ( 47 ).
 To score responses in these tasks, we leverage recent work enabling quantitative assessment of creativity performance ( 26 , 28 , 62 ). We score two aspects of task responses: individual originality and population-level variability . The originality metric O t ( ⁠ t = AUT, DAT, FF) measures the originality of an individual’s (LLM or human participant) response via its semantic distance from the task prompt. Intuitively, this metric captures the divergence of thought between the prompt and response, a commonly recognized measure of creativity ( 26 , 27 ). The variability metric V t measures the breadth of creative responses from a population (e.g., LLMs or human participants), by computing the semantic distances between embedded representations of population responses. Following prior work and common practice and, in particular, AI creativity research, originality, and variability are computed in aggregate across start words for the AUT and FF tasks ( 47 , 60 , 63 , 64 ). Fig. 1 presents our workflow.
 Individual originality of human participants vs. LLMs
 As a baseline, we first compute originality scores across the three tasks for all human participants and LLMs, then compare them. LLMs score were slightly higher than human participants on the AUT and DAT tasks, mirroring prior work ( 9 ) but were worse on FF. Fig. 2 and Table 1 show the distributions of originality scores for human participants and LLMs on these tasks and provides statistics comparing population means for the two groups. LLMs and human participants exhibit roughly equal levels of measured originality on these tests on average, removing this as a possible confounding variable in our study of response variability.

 Population-level response variability in human participants vs. LLMs
 Next, we explore the main question: whether human participants and LLMs exhibit different population-level variability in creative outputs. Our key finding is that LLM responses exhibit much less variability, as measured by semantic distance between pairs of embedded responses, than do human responses. Fig. 3 and Table 2 show the distributions of V t ( LLM ) and V t ( Human ) ⁠ , e.g., cosine distances between embedded representations of responses from these populations. Both these views of the data confirm that LLM responses are much more similar to each other than are human participant responses. From this, we conclude that a population of LLMs produces more homogeneous outputs in response to creative prompts than a population of human participants.

 Exploring causes and mitigations for LLM output homogeneity
 Having established that LLMs produce more homogeneous creative outputs than human participants, we next explore several additional dimensions of this key finding. We find that the low response variability of LLMs can be partially explained through analysis of lexical patterns in LLM and human responses. We remove stopwords from responses, then count the number of word overlaps between sets of responses from LLMs and human participants—all AUT uses from a human/LLM, all words in a FF response, etc. As Fig. 5 shows, LLM responses tend to have many more words in common than human responses, across all tasks. This overlap at least partially accounts for the high semantic similarity between LLM responses, as the embedding process maps responses with overlapping words to similar feature vectors. Further exploration of lexical pattern differences between LLMs and humans participants is needed in future work.

 Next, we demonstrate that LLM response homogeneity remains even after strictly controlling for structural differences in human and LLM responses. For both the DAT and FF tests, the response structure is fixed, making comparison of population-level variability straightforward. However, the AUT is more open-ended, so confounding variables such as differences in response structure (e.g., number of words, tense, etc.) between LLMs and human participants may impact measurements of response variability. For example, if every LLM AUT response is four words long and uses a gerund (e.g., “making” and “writing”), the measured similarity between LLM responses may be artificially inflated. Since we want to measure variability in the substance rather than the structure of responses, we must ensure that structural similarity does not impact our findings. Through carefully designed experiments, we demonstrate that the observed population-level difference in LLM and human response variability remains even after controlling for AUT response structure. For space reasons, this analysis can be found in the Supplementary material .
 Next, we inspect whether models in the same “family” produce more homogenous responses than a baseline set of otherwise unrelated models. To do this, we measure the population-level variability of AUT responses from Llama model family: Meta Llama 3 70B Instruct , Meta Llama 3 8B Instruct , Meta Llama 3.1 405B Instruct , Meta Llama 31 70B Instruct , and Meta Llama 3.1 8B Instruct . Given the small number of models we are comparing, we add more AUT startwords to increase dataset size. These start words, modeled on prior AUT studies ( 62 ), are: book, bottle, brick, fork, hammer, pants, shoe, shovel, table, and tire. Fig. 6 shows population-level AUT originality distributions for unrelated LLMs vs. Llama models, and statistics comparing these distributions. This analysis reveals that models in the same “family” exhibit slightly lower response diversity than models from different “families.” Although the differences of means is not statistically significant (see Table 3 ), from visual inspection of histograms in Fig. 6 , we see that the sentence embedding distribution for Llama models is skewed right. The presence of these outliers drives up the overall Llama population mean, making it appear more similar to that of the regular LLMs. Future work is needed to examine other model families and explore other dimensions of family-specific similarity.

 Finally, we consider ways to make LLMs produce more variable creative outputs as a possible mitigation for the creative homogeneity we observe. We experiment with varying the temperature of the LLM response and the system prompt provided to the model. The temperature t is a variable ranging from 0 to 2 that shifts the probability distribution of output tokens that the model uses to form its response. At low t , models are more likely to choose high probability tokens, potentially making responses less creative and random. All prior experiments in the current study were run with T = 1.0 ⁠ , but here, we experiment with T = { 0.5 , 1.0 , 1.5 , 2.0 } ⁠ . As Fig. 7 and Table 4 show, increasing t does improve the population-level variability of LLM responses. However, this statistical result is somewhat misleading. We observed empirically that responses at T = 2.0 were largely gibberish. This is important as a creative idea is one that is both original and appropriate ( 14 ). Models would often start their response with somewhat sensible English responses to the prompt, then quickly devolve into nonsense. To create a set of data usable in our analysis, we culled LLM responses at T = 2.0 via two criteria: (i) removing non-English words and (ii) removing repeated outputs. Examples of nonedited LLM responses at T = 2.0 can be found in the Supplementary material .

 Finally, we consider whether modifying the LLM system prompt to strictly requesting creative outputs will induce higher variability without the gibberish observed at high temperatures. We experiment with prompts designed to elicit different levels of creativity:
 Baseline : “You are a helpful assistant.”
 More creative : “You are a creative assistant that always provides answers that demonstrate imaginative, outside-the-box thinking.”
 Very creative : “You are a creative assistant that always provides answers that demonstrate imaginative, outside-the-box thinking. You are about to take a creativity assessment, and your answers should be as novel, original, and bold as possible. If you receive the highest score on this creativity assessment, you will receive $200.”
 Not creative : “You are a robot assistant that always provides answers that are unoriginal, bland, and soulless. You are about to take a creativity assessment, and your answers should be as generic and unoriginal as possible.”

 Discussion
 Our study explores possible convergence in the creative outputs of large-scale LLMs across multiple creativity tasks. While prior studies have found that using LLMs can facilitate creative tasks ( 66 ), likely by facilitating a cocreative act ( 67 ), other studies have highlighted homogenization effects incurred by LLMs during creativity tasks ( 39 , 46 , 68 , 69 ). We test this by soliciting creative outputs from LLMs and human participants using standardized and commonly used creativity tasks—the AUT ( 61 ), FF ( 60 ), and the DAT ( 47 )—and measuring the population-level variability of responses. While caution should be used in extrapolating human-centric psychological tests to nonhuman entities ( 70 ), these tests are now widely used in assessing LLM creativity ( 71 ).
 Mirroring prior work ( 9 ), we show that LLMs match or outperform humans on standard tasks of creativity. Yet, this finding of individual creativity is misleading because LLM responses to creative prompts are much more similar to each other than are human responses, even after controlling for LLM “family” overlap and differences in human/LLM response structure. Altering the LLM system prompt to encourage higher creativity slightly increases inter-LLM response variability , but human responses are still more variable. This result replicates, supports, and extends prior observations of LLM-induced homogeneity ( 39 , 40 , 46 , 72 ). Importantly, such prior work only considered the effect of specific LLMs on creative outputs. Thus, our current study strongly suggests that the use of LLMs in general may homogenize creative outputs.
 Importantly, recent work highlights how individuals vary in their susceptibility to be impacted by potential homogenization effects by LLMs during performance in creative tasks ( 73–75 ). For example, Distefano et al. ( 73 ) demonstrate how individual differences, domain expertise, and creative thinking ability impact the success of human–AI creativity; Urban et al. ( 74 ) find that more creative individuals use more variable and complex prompts then less creative individuals when solving creative problems; and Mermert et al. ( 75 ) show how less creative individuals tend to over-rely on an LLM brainstorming tool compared to more creative individuals, which led to a reduction in ideas contributed by the less creative individuals. Conversely, Orwig et al. ( 76 ) examined how individual variability in creativity impacted prompts used to create AI-generated images, finding that more creative individuals generated richer prompts that led more creative AI-generated images. This further suggests that individual creativity differences influence the creativity of AI outputs in human–AI teaming. As people become more dependent on LLMs, their ideas and products are likely to become increasingly homogenized ( 39 , 43 , 68 , 69 ), regardless of the LLM they use . Such diminished variability of LLMs output could harm future thinking, especially when human creativity, flexibility, and adaptability are needed most.
 Our results also have interesting implications for those who develop and deploy AI models. Although nascent work has observed similarity in feature representations among today’s large-scale LLMs ( 56 , 77 ), limited work has considered downstream consequences of LLM similarity. One work ( 78 ) examines question-answering bias of 10 LLMs across 4 “families” but finds little evidence of bias similarity among models. Another demonstrates jailbreak attacks that transfer between LLMs ( 79 ) but does not specifically leverage LLM similarity in attack development. Huh et al. ( 77 ) also postulated that large models will become more similar over time due to their design and objectives, suggesting the homogenization we observe may worsen over time. Those developing AI models can hopefully use this work as a springboard to motivate study of other homogeneous properties of AI models and develop appropriate mitigations.
 Our work has several limitations. First, while we demonstrate LLM homogeneity in response to certain creativity tests, this does not prove that LLMs in general produce homogeneous outputs when asked to behave creatively. It merely indicates that future work should explore this subject. Additionally, we measure a single metric of divergent thinking or creativity—originality, as measured by semantic distance—and find that LLMs are homogeneous along this dimension. However, there are other well-known metrics of divergent thinking, such as flexibility, fluency, and elaboration, and LLMs may demonstrate more or less homogeneity along these dimensions. Future work should consider these alternatives.
 Another limitation of our study is that we consider only LLMs served behind public APIs, rather than locally hosted models. Publicly hosted models typically undergo extensive alignment training to ensure compliance with company guidelines. Prior work has shown that aligned models perform worse on tests of creativity and randomness than baseline (e.g., unaligned) models ( 80 ). However, given the widespread use of hosted, aligned LLMs, we believe the results of our study remain relevant, though future work is needed to examine response diversity from unaligned LLMs.
 Another issue is whether we can truly compare human and LLM performance on creativity tasks. Given our aim, it seems reasonable to test humans and LLMs using the AUT, FF, and DAT and then compare the population-level variability of their responses. However, it is an active area of debate as to whether psychological tests—including those of creativity—designed for humans are applicable to LLMs. Some argue that works probing LLM performance on these tasks is misguided due to fundamental differences between LLM and humans ( 46 , 70 , 81 ). Despite this disagreement, we believe creativity tasks administered to LLMs can be useful for our purposes, because we are not trying to measure inherent LLM creativity. Instead, we would like to understand the variability of LLM and human responses to creative prompts, which is an empirical rather than psychological trait. Well-trained LLMs should respond similarly to factual questions but not necessarily to creative prompts, so if there is indeed homogeneity in LLM outputs, creativity is the appropriate lens through which to evaluate this question.
 Our results highlight a danger of relying on generative AI models as creative partners, particularly if LLMs are widely adopted as creativity support tools for writing, idea generation, or similar tasks ( 39 , 72 ). If all LLMs respond similarly to specific creative requests, then the population of users leveraging to LLMs to aid creativity will converge toward a limited set of creative outputs. In other words, LLM users may be self-limited from being exhibiting the divergent creativity that defined well-recognized artistic geniuses like Tolkein, Mozart, and Picasso because their LLM “creative” partners may collectively drive them toward a mean. Creativity thrives on heterogeneity of ideas, but today’s LLMs seem unable to provide these.
 Given the rapidly evolving landscape of AI, a critical question based on our findings and past work is whether AI creativity is an engineering challenge that can be solved in the future. As empirical creativity research progresses in elucidating the cognitive building blocks of creativity—i.e., divergent thinking, associative thinking, memory, attention, cognitive control, analogical reasoning, etc.—can these building blocks be implemented in new LLMs to achieve a true creative artificial intelligence? Increasing understanding of the complexity of the creative process at the cognitive and neural levels ( 6 , 82 , 83 ), is advancing a mechanistic understanding of creativity that may one day be successfully implemented in AI ( 84 ). However, similar to the pursuit of artificial general intelligence ( 85 ), many challenges remain in reaching such an engineering implementation. Critically, whether such a goal can be achieved remains in question, given critical dimensions of creativity that center around authenticity, intentionality, context, embodiment, and individuality ( 86–89 ), dimensions that are not currently realistically possible in the current LLM landscape. Overall, while several cognitive mechanisms of creativity can be implemented in future LLMs (i.e., increased response variability), it remains unclear whether creativity in its comprehensive sense can ever be.
 Materials and methods
 Participants and procedures
Human participants
 We conducted an IRB-approved user study to collect human responses to creativity tests. a Study participants were recruited from the Prolific platform and were asked to complete the DAT, FF, and AUT creativity tasks. Exact wording of test questions can be found in the Supplementary information . It took participants 19 min on average to complete the survey, and they were compensated at a rate of $15/h. Participants completed the three tests in order: DAT, then FF, then AUT. Participant demographics are described in Table S1 . All participants completed a signed consent form before starting the study. We recruited 114 participants from the Prolific platform, screening for English fluency and an approval rating > 95 ⁠ . Of these, 12 were excluded on suspicion of being bots due to unusually short response times ( ⁠ < 5 min) or failed attention checks. Thus, the final dataset contains 102 human participants. Authors manually inspected responses to correct obvious misspellings.
 The risk in relying on responses from online crowd workers is that they may themselves be bots or may leverage LLMs in crafting their responses, resulting in reduced response diversity ( 42 ). Prolific runs strict tests to ensure human responses, and we also used safeguards to prevent this, including attention tests and post hoc data inspection. However, the risk remains. Therefore, we confirm the validity of our survey responses by comparing them to public datasets of human responses to the AUT b , FF c , and DAT d from prior studies as a secondary validation dataset. These data are from creativity tests run in person before the rise of LLMs (around 2022), so they are unlikely to contain LLM responses.
 We test both the individual originality and population-level variability of our human respondents versus prior study respondents and find that while respondents in prior studies score better individually on the tasks, respondents to our study exhibit equal or greater population-level variability (the more important metric for our study) on the more-informative AUT and FF tests. Our results roughly mirror those of prior studies, making them a reasonable baseline for our analysis. Details of our analysis are below.
 Large language models
 As a baseline, we test 22 LLMs with public APIs: https://docs.github.com/en/github-models/prototyping-with-ai-models and https://ai.google.dev/ : AI21-Jamba-Instruct , Cohere Command R, Cohere Command R Plus , Meta Llama 3 70B Instruct , Meta Llama 3 8B Instruct , Meta Llama 3.1 405B Instruct , Meta Llama 31 70B Instruct , Meta Llama 3.1 8B Instruct , Mistral large , Mistral large 2407 , Mistral Nemo, Mistral small , Google Gemini 1.5 , gpt 4o , gpt 4o mini , Phi 3 medium 128k instruct , Phi 3 medium 4k instruct , Phi 3 mini 128k instruct , Phi 3 mini 4k instruct , Phi 3 small 128k instruct , Phi 3 small 8k instruct, and Phi 3.5 mini instruct . Models in the same “family” (e.g., all Llamas, all GPTs, etc.) may generate unusually similar responses due to similarities in architecture, training data, or optimization techniques. To control for this, we restrict ourselves following subset of models when conducting statistical tests, which contains only one model from each “family”: AI21 Jamba 1.5 Large ( 90 ), Google Gemini 1.5 ( 91 ), Cohere Command R Plus ( 92 ), Meta Llama 3 70B Instruct ( 93 ), Mistral Large ( 94 ), gpt 4o ( 95 ), and Phi 3 medium 128k Instruct ( 96 ). All these models were trained by distinct entities, providing a reasonable independent baseline. When we explore how models within the same “family” behave, we use the Llama model family ( 93 ): Meta Llama 3 70B Instruct , Meta Llama 3 8B Instruct, Meta Llama 3.1 405B Instruct , Meta Llama 31 70B Instruct, Meta Llama 3.1 8B Instruct . We evaluate all models with the default system prompt of “You are a helpful assistant,” a default temperature of 1.0, and a default top-p of 1.0 unless otherwise indicated. Since human test respondents respond to one instance of the task with a particular startword at a given time, we ask LLMs to do the same. For example, we prompt an LLM to respond to the AUT with startword “book,” then we reprompt them to get their AUT response with the next startword “hammer.” We chose to elicit responses from LLMs this way, rather than eliciting multiple responses at once (e.g., prompting for AUT responses with all startwords) to most closely mimic human responses to these tests. Exact LLM prompt wording can be found in the Supplementary material .
 After obtaining all model responses to all tests with all startwords in this one-by-one manner, we remove unnecessary punctuation and characters (e.g., numbered DAT outputs) by manual inspection to enable standardized, automated processing of the data. Less than 2 % of data were removed from LLM responses in this process.
 Creativity assessment
 We assess LLM and human performance on three creativity tasks. These tasks are commonly used in human creativity research and measure main cognitive capacities that realize creativity, namely divergent thinking and associative thinking ( 15 , 18 ).
 Alternative uses task ( 61 ) is one of the most widely used in-lab creativity assessment tasks. It measures divergent thinking—a critical aspect of creativity ( 13 , 22 )—by presenting participants with an object and asking them to generate creative uses for it. Following established best practices ( 97 , 98 ), we test users with five common objects—book, fork, table, hammer, and pants. Using multiple starting objects reduces the effect of a particular object (e.g., book) on participant responses, ensuring results generalize ( 62 ). It also allows us to collect more data, given the limited number of LLMs we can evaluate relative to the number of possible human subjects.
 Forward flow ( 60 ) measures the divergence of a user’s chain of thought from a fixed starting point based on their associative abilities ( 99 , 100 ), a general mechanism of creative thinking ( 15 ). It provides a starting prompt word and asks people to write down the next word that follows in their mind from the previous word for up to 20 words. We follow the original FF approach and run our study using five different start prompt words: candle, table, bear, snow, and toaster. As in the AUT, providing multiple creative stimuli ensures results generalize and gives us more data.
 The divergent association task ( 47 ) asks participants to list 10 words that are as unrelated as possible. Higher semantic variety in this list is positively correlated with traditional metrics for creative thinking ( 31 , 47 ). These are subject to certain constraints: only nouns, no proper nouns, only single words in English, and the task must be completed in <4 min. The DAT provides a limited amount of information compared to the other tests, since the creative stimulus is not varied.
 Evaluation metrics
 The primary goal of this study is to evaluate the variability of LLM responses to creative prompts relative to that of humans. To do this, we compute the semantic similarity of responses in different populations (LLMs vs. humans) and compute distributional differences in similarity scores between populations. As a baseline, we also compare the originality of individual LLM responses to the tests relative to that of humans.
 Traditionally, performance in the verbal creativity tasks we use was assessed via subjective ratings of external judges ( 24 ), or more general creative ability was assessed via self-report questionnaires ( 25 ). However, the introduction of computational tools to creativity assessment have significantly advanced creativity assessment in and outside of the lab ( 26–30 ). Such methods are based on using various computational models to quantify idea originality in open-ended creativity divergent thinking tasks, such as the AUT ( 26 , 28 ), or in associative thinking tasks ( 60 , 100 ). We leverage these computational methods in our work.
 Scoring individual originality
 Although divergent thinking tasks can be measured using multiple metrics ( 23 ), it has long been argued the originality of responses is the strongest indicator of creativity ( 34 ). Originality—how novel responses are relative to the given prompt(s)—can also easily be measured in an automated fashion by embedding prompts and responses in a mathematical feature space and measuring the cosine distance between the feature vectors ( 101 ). Prior work confirms that such automated analysis closely matches originality rankings of human scorers ( 62 ).
 Our metrics for individual originality follow the guidelines of prior studies but use the automated evaluation methods of ( 62 ) and leverage the GloVe 840B model ( 102 ) to compute word embeddings. The format of each test necessitates different originality scoring procedures, described in detail below. Originality scores are denoted as O t ( P ) ⁠ , where t = AUT, FF, or DAT and P is a population, either humans or LLMs.
 After computing originality scores, we then compare the distributions of O t ( LLM ) and O t ( Humans ) to measure differences in originality between the two groups. We do this by testing for statistically significant differences in μ ( O t ( LLM ) ) and μ ( O t ( Human ) ) using Welch’s t -test to compare differences in means, since the populations typically do not exhibit equal variance. We use a statistical significance threshold of ρ = 0.01 ⁠ . For all tests, the null hypothesis is that μ ( O t ( LLM ) ) = μ ( O t ( Human ) ) ⁠ , and the alternative is that μ ( O t ( LLM ) ) > μ ( O t ( Human ) ) ⁠ .
 Scoring population-level variability
 We measure the variability in responses to the creativity tasks from a given population by computing the semantic distances between sets of responses from individuals in the population (e.g., comparing the set of AUT uses produced by an LLM to that of another LLM). If many population members generate semantically similar sets of answers, this indicates that the response variability of the population is low, and vice versa if it is high. We denote the variability of a population P on test t as V t ( P ) ⁠ , the set of all similarity scores between all responses from all population members. As before, P refers to either LLMs or humans.
 We use a sentence embedding model S to measure semantic similarity between responses. Sentence embedding models map sentences or short paragraphs to feature vectors and, similar to the word embedding model, map similar content to similar feature vectors. We compute elements of V t ( P ) by representing an individual’s responses to a certain test condition (e.g., all their AUT responses to a certain prompt) as a single, space-separated word string R and embedding this into a mathematical space via S ⁠ , producing S ( R ) ⁠ . We then take the cosine similarity between this vector and those of other population members to form V t ( P ) ⁠ :
 V t ( P ) = { 1 − cos ( S ( R i p ) , S ( R i + 1 p ) ) , ∀ ( R i p , p ) ∈ P } , 
 (1) 
 where R i p , R j p denote the responses of two different population members to prompt p . Computing distances only between successive population members ensures the resulting distance dataset is independent and identically distributed (IID), a necessary condition to perform t -tests. In our experiments, we use all-MiniLM-L6-v2 from the sentence_transformers Python library ( 103 ), a high-performing and widely used model, to compute sentence embeddings. We remove punctuation and stopwords before computing embeddings.
 V t ( P ) is composed of cosine distance scores, so if it skews toward 0, responses in the population are similar to each other. If it skews toward 1, they are more different, and therefore the population exhibits higher variability. Note that V t ( P ) only contains similarity scores of responses from different LLM/human subjects. We can then compare the statistical distributions of V t ( LLM ) and V t ( Humans ) to measure the relative response variability between these groups. We do this using the same statistical tests as before. For all tests, the null hypothesis is that μ ( V t ( LLM ) ) = μ ( V t ( Human ) ) ⁠ , and the alternative is that μ ( V t ( LLM ) ) > μ ( V t ( Human ) ) ⁠ .
 For our statistical tests, we compare the means of two independent samples without assuming equal variance between the two populations, since computed statistics for the human and LLM response populations showed different variances for the two populations. When running a two-sample t -test of independent samples without the assumption of equal variance, one must use the Satterwaithe approximation to compute the effective degrees of freedom, which is given as:
 ν = ( s 1 2 n 1 + s 2 2 n 2 ) 2 s 1 4 n 1 2 ( n 1 − 1 ) + s 2 4 n 2 2 ( n 2 − 1 ) , 
 (2) 
 where s 1 2 , s 2 2 are the sample variances of each population and n 1 , n 2 are the population sample sizes.
 When reporting results of statistical t -tests, we use the standard APA format, reporting the degrees of freedom (DOF), test statistic X , and significance level y : t ( DOF ) = X , p = y ⁠ . For context, we also report the effect size, which is the difference between the means of the two populations divided by their pooled standard deviation. Cohen ( 104 ) defines small, medium, and large effect sizes as 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8, respectively. Finally, we report test power, the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis.
