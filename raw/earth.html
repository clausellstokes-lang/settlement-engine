Readers rated AI stories higher than human fiction in study of 2,587 adults

ChatGPT-generated stories received higher reader ratings than human fiction, and most readers could not tell them apart.

ByEric Ralls
Founder
Follow Earth on Google

A handful of short stories were put in front of readers with one simple question: Which ones were best? The top-rated stories were generated by ChatGPT, beating fiction written by human authors published in a literary journal.

People are increasingly encountering writing created by both humans and AI, often without knowing which is which. Readers in this test could not reliably identify the author, and they gave stories higher ratings when they believed a person had written them.

The research comes from Villanova University and appears in Judgment and Decision Making, a Cambridge University Press journal published for SJDM. The findings add to a growing body of evidence that people judge writing differently once they believe it was created by AI.

Readers compared AI and human stories

Sydney Sears and Deena Skolnick Weisberg built the study around six stories. Three were fiction published by human authors in a reputable literary journal or short story collection.

The other three were ChatGPT versions of the same stories. Every participant was an adult, ranging in age from 18 to 81.

In the first experiment, 1,682 readers each read a single story. Half were told the truth about who wrote it. Half were told the opposite.

They then rated the story’s quality and how absorbed they felt while reading it.

Absorption was measured with an edited Story World Absorption Scale, covering attention, transportation, emotional engagement, and mental imagery.

AI stories earned higher ratings

The AI stories were rated higher on both measures. Readers called them better written, and said they were pulled in further.

The label mattered too. The very highest ratings went to AI-generated stories that participants had been told a human wrote.

Deena Weisberg is the senior author and a researcher in the Department of Psychological and Brain Sciences at Villanova. She reads the result as a bias rather than a verdict on the writing.

“In our study, participants gave the highest ratings to stories that they had been told were written by humans but which were actually written by AI,” says Weisberg.

“I’d argue that this reveals a bias toward narratives written by real people. We assume creative writing requires uniquely human qualities, such as emotional understanding and lived experience, which leads people to underestimate AI’s capabilities.”

“I’d say that public assumptions about AI’s capabilities are increasingly out of date,” she says.

Can people spot AI writing?

The second and third experiments handed each reader one human story and one AI-generated story, with no hint about origins. The task was to say which was which.

In the second experiment, readers picked correctly 39.93% of the time. That is worse than a coin flip, meaning they were steered wrong rather than merely confused.

In the third, accuracy reached 51.97%, statistically no different from chance. Between them, 905 adults took the test.

Weisberg expected those errors to run in one direction. They did not.

“I was most surprised by the mistakes that participants made when they were asked to guess which story was human-written and which was AI-generated. I had expected that the mistakes would be in one direction, specifically that people would tend to say that AI-generated writing was human-written. But that’s not what we found,” Weisberg told Earth.com.

Readers relied on the wrong cues

“Participants were equally likely to get the question wrong by saying that an AI-written story was written by a human and by saying that a human-written story was written by AI. What that suggests to me is that our participants were using the wrong metrics to judge which story was which.”

“Participants may have come into the study with some ideas about what characterizes human writing and AI writing, but those ideas seemed to be wrong,” she says.

Experience with literature offered no edge. Readers who described themselves as well-read in fiction were no better at spotting the machine.

AI familiarity improved detection

One thing did predict success, and it was familiarity with AI itself. In the second experiment, each one-point rise in self-reported AI expertise raised the odds of a correct guess by 14%.

The third experiment used the Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale, a previously validated measure. There, a one-point gain raised the odds by 33%.

That advantage appears to come from pattern recognition rather than literary taste. The tells are small, stylistic, and learnable.

“One thing to note was that familiarity with AI systems appeared to help people recognize the patterns typical of AI-generated writing, such as em dashes and sentence structures such as ‘it’s not just X, it’s Y,'” says Weisberg.

“That suggests that improving AI literacy would be one way to help people to navigate the new AI-enabled world that we’re living in.”

Fiction gives readers less practice

Asked what a reader could practically train themselves to notice, Weisberg points to plain exposure.

“The main thing that people can do is familiarize themselves with what AI-generated output looks like… In general, we found that participants with higher self-reported levels of experience with AI systems were better at telling the difference between human-written and AI-generated stories. The more you get to know the style of AI writing, the easier it is to spot,” she says.

There is a catch in that advice. Most people meet machine writing in work reports and student essays, not in fiction.

“…my impression is that AI systems are mostly being asked to generate nonfiction or expository writing, like reports for work or student essays. If that’s true, that means that people generally have less familiarity with AI-generated creative writing, which may make it harder to spot,” says Weisberg.

Clear writing influenced ratings

Weisberg’s explanation for the preference is simple. AI writing tends to be clean, and clean prose is easy to read.

“AI writing tends to be clearer, more direct and easier to process. By contrast, human-written stories are often more subtle and complex,” says Weisberg.

“For example, the AI versions of our stories usually stated their themes explicitly, rather than allowing readers to infer meaning from the characters’ words and actions.”

Large language models absorb the statistical patterns of the text they are trained on and reproduce them. Explicit, tidy prose is one of those patterns.

“We hear a lot about the crisis in literacy, shrinking attention spans and the influence of platforms such as TikTok. But I don’t think we can say that our findings in this study can be entirely explained by contemporary media consumption,” says Weisberg.

“It’s more that these technologies amplify existing tendencies, rather than creating them. Perhaps people generally prefer predictability, because difficult or subtle material requires more brain power.”

Where AI writing goes next

The study rests on three story pairs and one AI system. Other models, other genres, and longer work could all behave differently.

The experiments also ran on an earlier version of ChatGPT. Weisberg does not expect a newer one to swing the result back toward human authors.

“The models have gotten more advanced in what they’re able to do, so if anything, they now should be able to generate short stories that seem even more human-like,” she says.

Readers also judged the stories once, in a single sitting. Nothing here says a machine can write a novel that people return to for years.

Broader impacts of AI writing

What the results do show is more limited, but no less relevant. Readers cannot reliably tell the difference, and the label alone moves their judgment.

That gap matters beyond fiction. AI writing assistants already shift opinions in social media posts, and chatbots draw more empathy than doctors in patient conversations.

The same tools have pushed invented citations into peer-reviewed papers. In each case, readers judged the text before knowing where it came from.

The study is published in the journal Judgment and Decision Making.
