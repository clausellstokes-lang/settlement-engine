  In his article "Toward a Psychosociology of Contemporary Food
Consumption," Roland Barthes points out that "an entire
'world' (social environment) is present in and signified by
food" (23). The signifying power of food in a world, however
fantastic, becomes apparent in George R. R. Martin's (yet to be
completed) cycle titled The Song of Ice and Fire. (1) Not only do his
sensuous descriptions of food enhance the medieval aura of the world in
his novels, but they also underpin the ideologies of the parties
involved in the conflict. The social structure of this world is complex,
but among the many differences at play in its evolution, the dominant
opposition between those in power and the disinherited emerges through,
among other things, their attitudes toward food. Whereas the privileged
indulge the pleasure of eating and prove their power through extravagant
food displays, the dispossessed are forced to resort to violence or risk
starvation. Both the hedonism of one faction and the cruelty of the
other are symptoms of an imbalance at once social and spiritual.
<BR><BR>  Food sharing scenes in Martin's novels have a special appeal.
His readers' enthusiasm for the subject surfaces in blogs too
numerous to mention, letters to the author, (2) and not one but two
cookbooks (3) inspired by the recipes in his novels. In his preface to
the "official" cookbook, he spells out his reasons for
describing food: "It grounds the scenes, gives them texture, makes
them vivid and visceral and memorable. Sense impressions reach us on
much deeper and more primal levels than intellectual discourse can ever
hope to" (Monroe-Cassel and Lehrer X). Yet, food descriptions are
not simply a means to achieve a visceral effect. They contribute to the
fashioning of a world fringed by magic from fragments of historical or
literary representations. In her work on medieval romance, Geraldine
Heng remarks that "Romance, of course, freely cannibalizes from
extant cultural material--from bodies of older texts, floating metaphors
and motifs, bits of tales, legends, superstitions, mere hints and
guesses--and organizes its hybrid matter into a pattern and a structure
that it instantiates at a particular historical moment" (46). What
is true about medieval romance is also true of historical fantasy. The
fantasy writer "cannibalizes" the medieval recipes and
organizes them in a pattern, where the missing pieces play as crucial a
role as those included. For whereas the eating habits of the aristocracy
reflect historically documented attitudes, two important ideologies
surrounding food in the Middle Ages are left out: the theory of humours
and Christian food prohibitions. By the same gesture, the violence
attending the procurement and preparation of food is purged from the
descriptions of the meals of the privileged and relegated to the means
by which the disinherited and hungry get their food. The contrast
between eating for pleasure and being driven by hunger to commit
unspeakable acts underscores the conflict between those in power and the
dispossessed.
<BR><div class="xz" id="xz8"></div><BR>  Studies about food provide insights in many aspects of culture and
can be used as a framework for analyzing Martin's use of food
descriptions. In the introduction to the second edition of her book,
Much Depends on Dinner, Margaret Visser looks back on the developments
in food studies since its first edition (1986): "Since the book
first appeared there has been an explosion of writing about food"
(1). She also comments that "Food used to be thought too
'low' a subject for intellectual rumination, a merely animal
pleasure, a necessity that, once we have secured sufficient supplies of
it, was too obvious and too crude for discussion in public" (1-2).
No longer considered "low," the subject has become popular
both with the general public and in academia. Before Visser, it was
structuralism that drew the attention of academic research to food, when
Claude Levi-Strauss (4) offered a way to analyze culture through
cuisine. Like Levi-Strauss, Roland Barthes approaches food as cultural
element, which acquires meaning, or significance, in relation to other
areas of human experience: "[Food] is not only a collection of
products that can be used for statistical or nutritional studies. It is
also, and at the same time, a system of communication, a body of images,
a protocol of usages, situations, and behavior" (21).
<BR><BR>  The idea that food communicates and functions, therefore, as a
language, opens food-related behavior to cultural interpretation. Mary
Douglas sums up the usefulness of the concept of food-as-sign, or rather
system of signs: "If food is treated as a code, the messages it
encodes will be found in the pattern of social relations being
expressed. The message is about different degrees of hierarchy,
inclusion and exclusion, boundaries and transactions across the
boundaries" (252). Analysis of food becomes, in Douglas's
view, a way to look into and understand the workings of a society. Many
other disciplines joined anthropology in using food as a lens through
which to view their object of study. (5)
<BR><BR>  Literary criticism has also made the subject of food a focus of
attention in its exploration of various writers' works. Literary
representations of food are not new. Authors from Homer, Chaucer,
Rabelais, Shakespeare, to Fielding, Dickens, and Laura Esquivel use food
descriptions with various purposes and effects, and critics remark on
food's importance in creating atmosphere and enhancing
characterization. Attention to food, however, can do more than reveal
character or background. Using principles developed in structuralism and
post-structuralism, Timothy J. Tomasik, for instance, argues that
"Rather than satirise excess, Rabelais employs the rhetorical
figure of copia in order to engage other discourses on food in the
Renaissance" (25). His appraisal of Rabelais's lengthy food
catalogues uses the poststructuralist notion of "discourse"
developed by Michel Foucault (6) and highlights the relation between
food and language. The discourse on food is enmeshed in a network of
discourses extending to all aspects of life. In his study of Renaissance
references to food, Robert Appelbaum argues that statements about food
"conveyed a system for communicating with and by way of food, a
system for establishing a chain of communication between the subject,
the foodstuff, the subject's body, and the social world to which
subject, food, and body alike belonged" (35). Focusing on food as
discourse enriches the interpretation of a literary work by drawing upon
its cultural context. For a discourse carries ideas and beliefs, which
emanate from material practices, and it is part of a network of
knowledge specific to a culture. An approach foregrounding the cultural
significance of food is appropriate for Martin's novels, even
though the cultures he refers to are invented, because his invention
extrapolates from actual historical evidence.
<BR><div class="xz" id="xz9"></div><BR>  In his desire to create a medieval atmosphere for the world of his
novels, Martin did historical research, as he confesses in his interview
with Kristen Browning-Blas: "I had books about medieval feasts,
general histories where feasts were described. My research method is one
of immersion. I read all I can beforehand because when I'm writing
a scene, I'm in the scene. I don't want to have to stop and
look something up. Occasionally I put 'and then he ate xxxx'
but that's rare" (sec. 5). Historical descriptions of feasts
reflect the food consumption of the upper classes. Massimo Montanari
notes that "only the cuisine of the powerful has remained available
to us, albeit in a fragmentary form, handed down from documentary and
literary sources" (37). Martin uses the recipes handed down in such
documents, which carry in them not only the tastes but also the ideology
of the upper class. He thus captures the age's general outlook on
food, succinctly described by Melitta Weiss Adamson:
<Pre>
   At the medieval banquet the political power structure was
   transferred directly to the table. Status determined where one was
   seated, how new the table cloth was and how far away the salt was,
   how many people one had to share one's food with, how many dishes
   one partook in, how big one's overall food intake was, and whether
   one was allowed to taste the most luxurious food creations or just
   marvel at them. (233)
</Pre><BR><BR>  A scene that illustrates this historical account is the banquet
Eddard Stark gives in honor of King Robert. As a bastard, Jon Snow is
seated "below the salt" and weighs the pleasures of being able
to drink and hear "tales of battle, of bedding and the hunt"
against the honor of seating near the king (GOT 50). The scene goes
beyond a textbook description of medieval banquets when Jon feeds a
whole roasted chicken to his wolf, in a gesture that normally a lord
would perform when giving gifts of food to his vassals (GOT 51-52). This
instance is, however, lackluster by comparison to Martin's other
uses of food-centered scenes to explore the ideologies of the various
social strata and to set up the conflicts of the cycle.
<BR><BR>  Most noticeably, it is the ideology of the upper classes that
emerges in the ways they consume food. At the cycle's beginning,
Robert Baratheon urges Eddard Stark to come south where "the
markets are bursting with food, the summerwines are so cheap and so good
that you can get drunk just by breathing the air. Everyone is fat and
drunk and rich" (GOT 41). The kingdom's prosperity and safety
is signified by the availability of food. As befits a medieval context,
the king's body stands for the kingdom: Robert is fat and drunk and
rich. Felipe Fernandez-Armesto develops a view of consumption, which
puts gourmands like Robert in a good light:
<Pre>
   Big appetites stimulate production and generate surplus--leftovers
   on which lesser feeders can eat. So, in normal circumstances, as
   long as the food supply is unthreatened, eating a lot is an act of
   heroism and justice, similar in effect to other such acts, such as
   fighting off enemies and propitiating the gods: it is usual to find
   the same sort of people engaged in all three tasks. (102)
</Pre><BR><BR>  Robert combines, indeed, a prodigious appetite and the ability to
fight off enemies, and it is not by chance that the two qualities are
related. Both fighting and procuring food imply direct contact--with the
enemy, the animal, or the land--and violence.
<BR><BR>  The way nobility came in direct contact with the source of food
during the Middle Ages was through hunting. Naomi J. Sykes explains:
"Later medieval hunting manuals describe the chase par force as a
wide-ranging hunt of day-long duration in which a single deer was
stalked, killed, and excoriated (skinned, disemboweled, and butchered)
in a ritualized and formulaic manner" (170). The ritual mediates
between the brutality of the killing and the enjoyment of the food. A
scene involving Lord Tarly, who teaches his son a lesson about life,
"his long knife laying bare the carcass [of a deer] as he
spoke," demonstrates the aristocratic behavior in the hunt (GOT
268). Like the medieval lords, Tarly is skilled not only in killing but
also in skinning and preparing the animal for consumption. King Robert
is also fond of hunting, which stimulates his battle instincts in times
of peace. However, both he and Tarly represent a social order based on
brute power, where heroes earn their titles, as well as their food,
through their prowess and skill. Robert's death--which ironically
pits him against his food, when he is fatally wounded by a boar that
becomes dinner for his funeral--signals the end of an era and the
beginning of social change (GOT 507). Those who succeed Robert are
engaged in power struggles, but their fighting is of another nature.
Power is brokered in the corridors of palaces and castles, at a distance
from the battlefield. The new nobility distances itself not only from
the battles but also from the sources of food.
<BR><BR>  Many of the detailed food descriptions reveal the power struggles
in individual relationships. For instance, the tension between Tyrion
and Tywin Lannister becomes evident before a battle, around the table
improvised on the field. In spite of the improvisation, the food display
fits Tywin's rank: "five suckling pigs, skin seared and
crackling, a different fruit in every mouth" (GOT 675). Tyrion is
excited about eating, not only because he is hungry but also because he
has just come back from the Eyrie, where he was imprisoned and fed
beans. Nevertheless, when his father informs him that he will not have
command of the vanguard, he gives up the food. His self-denial is a way
to resist his father's power and assert his independence. The
description of the roast, which trickles throughout the conversation,
insists on visual, auditory, and gustatory details--"The crisp skin
crackled under his knife, and hot juice ran from the meat" (GOT
676)--showing how difficult it is for Tyrion to leave without eating.
<BR><BR>  Among the privileged, food is a sign of social standing, and is
often used to display and exert power. After becoming the King's
Hand, Tyrion demonstrates his power through lavish meals. He invites
Janos Slynt to his tower and treats him to a princely dinner:
<BR><BR>  The first thing he had done after taking residence in the Tower of
the Hand was inquire after the finest cook in the city and take her into
his services. This evening they had supped on oxtail soup, summer greens
tossed with pecans, grapes, red fennel and crumbled cheese, hot crab
pie, spiced squash, and quails drowned in butter. Each dish had come
with its own wine. (COK 123)
<BR><BR>  The description relies on the names of the dishes, summing up the
ingredients as well as giving some hints of their taste: hot, spiced,
butter(y). The gustatory enticements trap Janos Slynt into accepting the
authority of Tyrion, who banishes him to the Night's Watch. Tyrion
gives Slynt the news strategically, after entertaining him and getting
him drunk. This preparation ensures that Slynt would not refuse the food
in the way that Tyrion did his father's.
<BR><BR>  In contemplating the dishes, Tyrion is especially proud of his
newly acquired cook. Applebaum gives historical evidence showing that,
in the late Middle Ages, having a good cook became a sign of privilege,
and he attributes the appearance of more and more cookbooks to the
improvement of the profession's status at this time: "The
sudden appearance of a number of cookbooks like the Libellus in the late
thirteenth and early fourteenth century (nothing in Europe appears
before then in a vernacular, going back to the fall of the Roman Empire)
bespeaks a change in the relation of cooks to both their craft and their
patrons" (71). Like his counterparts in the thirteenth century,
Tyrion takes pride in his cook and often judges others by the quality of
their cooks, as when he decides that "Cersei set a tasty
table" (COK 773), or when he is positively seduced by
Illyrio's food: "The cooks might be old and fat, but they knew
their business. He had never eaten so well even at court" (DWD 26).
He also despises those who do not have accomplished cooks, as is the
case with Maester Pycelle, in a scene where Tyrion wields his newly
acquired power by showing disdain for the food. He finds the eggs
"wanted salt," the plums were "overcooked and watery to
his taste," and "The porridge was too thick ... and wanted
butter and honey" (262-263). These disparaging comments hint,
beyond the inferior food quality, to the ineptitude of Pycelle's
"girl," who is obviously not a professional cook.
<BR><BR>  Tyrion is not the only lord who enjoys showing his power by
displaying food. Upon Lady Stark's arrival to his camp, Renly
Baratheon delights his guests with a fabulous banquet:
<BR><BR>  Of food there was plenty. The war had not touched the fabled bounty
of Highgarden. While singers sang and tumblers tumbled, they began with
pears poached in wine, and went on to tiny savory fish rolled in salt
and cooked crisp, and capons stuffed with onions and mushrooms. There
were great loaves of brown bread, mounds of turnips and sweetcorn and
pease, immense hams and roast geese and trenchers dripping full of
venison stewed with beer and barley. For the sweet, Lord Caswell's
servants brought down trays of pastries from his castle kitchens, cream
swans and spun-sugar unicorns, lemon cakes in the shape of roses, spiced
honey biscuits and blackberry tarts, apple crisps and wheels of buttery
cheese. (COK 348)
<BR><BR>  Martin enumerates the usual roasts, vegetables, and breads of
lavish meals, but in this case, they seem to form the background for the
delicate desserts. The cream swans and spun-sugar unicorns have not been
mentioned before and attract most of the attention. The food not only
showcases the talents of the cooks but proves the devotion of
Renly's supporters as well. Normally, feasts show the power of the
king or lord who offers food for his subjects. The situation is reversed
in this instance, since it is lord Caswell who provides the food.
Nevertheless, by demonstrating support from such wealthy and
sophisticated subjects, Renly gathers power as favorite pretender.
Catelyn perceives this indulgence as frivolous and a sign of the
would-be king's immaturity. In addition to revealing Renly's
character to Catelyn, the meal is an example of conspicuous consumption,
which Phyllis Pray Bober identifies as specific to the late Middle Ages.
In Bober's account, the excess signals the changing fate of the
aristocracy: "They were not aware, or, at least, refused to believe
that their way of life was in its final days. It was as if outrageous
consumption and display, the accoutrements of power, could fend off an
assault upon their citadel of privilege by a growing class of wealthy
merchants and craftsmen able and ready to ape their standards of
living" (221). The attempt to sustain power and control by evincing
the signs of an authority that is no longer materially supported
indicates the end of privilege. Just so, the extravagant meal Renly
enjoys, before Stannis arrives, is his last. Renly's power is as
fragile as the spun-sugar unicorns and melts as quickly as the cream
swans. It seems fitting that he is killed by a shadow, since his power
is as insubstantial (COK 502). Renly's lack of experience with
politics and war is also metaphorically expressed by his ignorance about
the production and preparation of food, and about the cost of the
extravagance flaunted on his behalf.
<BR><BR>  Renly is not the only representative of a feudal class to be losing
his grip on power. So is Queen Cersei, who also clings to privilege
through displays of lavish meals, in spite of diminishing food supplies:
"Only a trickle of food was coming into King's Landing, most
of it earmarked for castle and garrison. Prices had risen sickeningly
high on greens, roots, flour, and fruit, and Tyrion did not want to
think about what sorts of flesh might be going into the kettles of the
pot-shops down in Flea Bottom" (COK 266). The shortages are seen
from Tyrion's point of view. Unlike Cersei, he is both aware of
prices and acquainted with the pot-shops of Flea Bottom. Supplies resume
after Renly's death, when the Tyrells negotiate their
daughter's marriage to Joffrey and win the love of the city with
gifts of food:
<BR><BR>  The mob loved Margaery so much they were even willing to love
Joffrey again. She had belonged to Renly, the handsome young prince who
had loved them so well he had come back from beyond the grave to save
them. And the bounty of Highgarden had come with her, flowing up the
roseroad from the south. The fools didn't seem to remember that it
had been Mace Tyrell who closed the roseroad to begin with, and made the
bloody famine. (SOS 812)
<BR><BR>  The Tyrells bring their bounty to win Margaery a crown, and the
adulation of the mob gives them a sense of power. To substantiate her
own power, for King Joffrey's wedding, Cersei plans to serve not
seven--which would make sense as honoring the seven gods--but seventy
seven dishes. Tyrion protests against spending money that the crown does
not have, but Tywin Lannister explains:
<BR><BR>  "Extravagance has its uses. We must demonstrate the power and
wealth of Casterly Rock for all the realm to see" (SOS 436). The
wedding banquet is indeed extravagant, and Martin works in the most
unusual recipes preserved in medieval cookbooks. A pie with live doves
under its crust stirs conversations long before the wedding. No doves
are mentioned in medieval recipes, but "four-and-twenty blackbirds
baked in a pie" appear in the nursery rhyme called "A Song of
Sixpence." (7) Like many dishes presented to the guests, the pie
tests the limits of the possible. Mentioned casually within the list of
dishes are also "peacocks served in their plumage" (SOS 821).
Nicola McDonald brings up the same dishes, in her discussion of medieval
romance:
<Pre>
   One recipe demands that a roasted peacock be returned to its plumed
   skin (skillfully removed before cooking) and served "as if it were
   alive"; a second provides instructions for a mercury concoction
   that causes a cooked fowl to jump animatedly in its dish; a third
   recommends hiding live birds inside a pie shell so that they fly
   out when the pie is cut open. These dishes and others like them
   force the diner to acknowledge that the host, even if apparently
   only in jest, has the power to conjure life itself and, by
   implication, death. (137)
</Pre><BR><BR>  By borrowing the recipes, Martin also evokes their intent. In a
less obviously violent way, the extravagant dishes aim for the same
effect as the surprising decapitation of Eddard Stark at Joffrey's
order: they force spectators to recognize the king's power.
However, this king's power has no material support. Like
Renly's banquet, the wedding feast proves to be a last meal, this
time for Joffrey. The cause of his death is uncertain: some suspect
there was poison in the wine, and it is hinted later that the poison had
been hidden in Sansa's hairnet. Yet, for the onlookers at the
wedding, Joffrey meets his death choking on the pie. Unsupported by
physical prowess or wealth, Joffrey's power ends before it begins.
He is a transitional figure between the brutal authority of
Robert's warhammer and the rising economic power of the mercantile
class, best represented by Illyrio Moppat from Pentos, whom Tyrion
enjoys calling the cheesemonger.
<BR><BR>  Faithful to the recipes, Martin's descriptions of meals give
insight into social relations because particular foods and their
combinations carry in them the ideology of the upper class, for which
the enjoyment of food is an assertion of wealth, power, and distinction.
Yet some other ideologies related to food are omitted. Medieval cuisine
followed the principles of the humoural theory developed by Galen (8) in
antiquity. Terence Scully explains that "in order to understand a
human being, we must begin, along with medieval thinkers, by weighing
the relative influence upon him of each of the four elements: earth,
air, water, fire" (41). Foods have qualities related to the
elements. They can be cold and moist, like water, or warm and dry like
fire, etc. Renly's feast above can be analyzed along these lines.
It starts with pears poached in wine. Medieval meals started with fruit
because fruit was considered hard to digest after other foods. Raw pears
were considered almost poisonous for being cold and moist and had to be
cooked in wine, which tempered their coldness. Fish was fried, because
it was cool and moist, and frying dried and warmed it. Capons--castrated
roosters--made good roasts because the process of drying, which started
with the maturity of the male bird, was stopped by castration. Roasting
dried, while boiling tempered dryness. Turnips were first boiled to
combat dryness, and then sliced and fried. Venison was considered warm
but too dry, and stewing helped moisten it.
<BR><BR>  The theory of humours could also explain the large number of dishes
served at banquets and at Joffrey's wedding. As Tomasik remarks,
the long menus of medieval meals can appear as signs of excess, but they
have their rationales:
<Pre>
   The number of dishes served can give the impression of excess.
   However, it is important to put the plethora of dishes into a
   medical and physical context. A great variety of prepared foods are
   necessary to accommodate the different kinds of humours that the
   attending diners might have. Moreover, each diner would be able to
   choose only from those dishes in his or her general vicinity at the
   table. (30)
</Pre><BR><BR>  Without such rationales the meals in the novels can only be seen as
signs of excess. As Martin does not elaborate on medical principles
valid in the Middle Ages, the order of serving the dishes, the
composition of the meals, and the mixtures of various ingredients appear
arbitrary, adding to the impression of hedonism and decadence at court.
<BR><BR>  Another significant exclusion of an ideology related to food is
that of prohibitions established by religion. The omission of
Christianity is relevant, especially when the object of reflection is
food, since in the Middle Ages, eating was regulated by the Christian
Church. As Bridget Ann Henisch explains, "Over the centuries,
church and society learned to live together in fair comfort, but there
was a price to be paid for the alliance. Each was deeply penetrated by
the other's influence. The Church was flawed and tarnished by the
relationship, while society found itself caught in a network of rules
and regulations spun around it by the Church" (6). These rules and
regulations had mainly to do with food and sex. Bober speaks about a
"fundamental dichotomy that affects cooking and eating during the
so-called Middle Ages. Body and soul; feasting and fasting; relishing
God's bounty yet evading the sins of the flesh like gluttony"
(193). While eating was sanctioned as a way to partake of the divine
bounty, asceticism was cultivated to purify the soul, and there was
considerable tension between the two. The dichotomy feast/fast accounts
for "meat days" and "fish or meatless days" in
cookbooks, from the early Middle Ages to the Renaissance.
<BR><BR>  Not only does Martin eliminate Christianity, but he also leaves out
any rules regarding eating from the religions he invents. None of the
religions in the novels--the religion of the seven gods, the quasi pagan
belief in the old gods, the monotheistic belief in R'hllor, or the
adoration of the God of the many faces--designates asceticism as a
virtue or gluttony as a sin to the extent Christianity does. There is a
mention of Baelor the Blessed, who fasted for so long he died of
starvation (SOS 806), and the remark of a street prophet that "Even
the high septon has forgotten the gods! He bathes in scented waters and
grows fat on lark and lamprey" (COK 317). The implication of the
outcry is that asceticism is valued by the church of the seven. However,
no limitations on food for the believers are ever mentioned.
Restrictions on sex, as Barristan Selmy explains, extend to "The
masters of the citadel, the septons and septas, the silent sisters of
the dead, the Kingsguard, and the Night's Watch" (SOS 317).
However, there are not any food restrictions. The food of the
Night's Watch is not as elaborate as the food of the castles, and
it is similar to the types of food eaten in medieval monasteries, but
without any prescribed "lean days."
<BR><BR>  Abandoning the idea of regulated eating, Martin creates conditions
that further emphasize indulgence among the privileged. Fish recipes are
frequent in medieval cookbooks because fish substitutes for meat on lean
days. In Martin's novels, meals including fish abound without any
rationale. Lamprey and eel dishes stand out, as they are not frequently
consumed in American culture today. Richard Schweid relates the
popularity of the eel to the religious calendar: "With meat
prohibited on numerous days of the years in the Church calendar, flesh
of the flavourful fishes was an ordinary substitute on many a table. And
why not? Eels were plentiful and cheap, if not free, for the easy
fishing, and they are a rich and flavourful fish" (78). As Schweid
also intimates, availability was another reason why the medieval
kitchens used eel and lamprey. According to Giovanni Rebora, from
antiquity to the Middle Ages, "Eel was a most important resource
since it could be transported live and on arrival kept alive in the
ponds.... For this reason cookbooks are full of recipes for eel and for
lamprey (which also could be transported live)" (62). Martin,
perhaps deliberately, makes the eel and lamprey coveted, but religiously
insignificant foods. Littlefinger is fond of lamprey pie, but he
mentions the suckling pig on the same menu (GOT 357). Imprisoned by
Stannis for having thought about killing Melisandre, Davos realizes he
is not truly punished because he is daily served lamprey, an elegant
food (SOS 345). Traveling north and facing daily hunger, Bran imagines
that his brother Rickon and Osha, his companion, have reached lord
Manderly's castle and are eating eels (SOS 331). In such
descriptions, the pleasurable aspect of food surfaces at the expense of
its spiritual dimension, which was all important to Christian believers.
<BR><BR>  The role of prohibitions, both religious and medical, was to temper
the consumption of food, in recognition of the effort and the violence
involved in its production. Christian as well as other religious rituals
and cultural customs regard food as sacrifice and thereby sanctify it. A
ritualized meal, where food is eaten in full awareness of what is
involved in its production, is the Harvest Feast, presided over by Bran.
Although no food restrictions are mentioned in relation to the religion
of the old gods, the Starks have respect for nature and reverence for
its bounty:
<BR><BR>  There were great joints of aurochs roasted with leeks, venison pies
chunky with carrots, bacon and mushrooms, mutton chops sauced in honey
and cloves, savory duck, peppered boar, goose, skewers of pigeon and
capon, beef-and-barley stew, cold fruit soup. Lord Wyman had brought
twenty casks of fish from White Harbor packed in salt and seaweed;
whitefish and winkles, crabs and mussels, clams, herring, cod, salmon,
lobster and lampreys. There was black bread and honeycakes and oaten
biscuits, there were turnips and pease and beets, beans and squash and
huge red onions, there were baked apples and berry tarts and pears
poached in strongwine. (COK 324-25)
<BR><BR>  The meal is perhaps richer than the meals at King's Landing
but has a much earthier flavor. The foods are not drowned in butter or
hidden in pies, for even the pie ingredients are evident. Moreover,
everyone knows where the food comes from, for all the guests have
brought some of their harvest to the table. The style of the description
is different as well. Sensory details, obscured by repetition and
alliteration, sound like a ritual chant. The name of the Harvest Feast
itself implies repeatability and suggests ritual. Before the food is
brought in, Bran asks his guests "to thank the gods old and new for
Robb's victories and the bounty of the harvest" (COK 324). In
recognition of the necessary violence, giving thanks involves both the
harvest and the war. Yet, the Harvest Feast is not exemplary of the
kingdom, or even of the North. It is rather an ideal from which all
other meals fall short, a nostalgic image of an era gone by.
<BR><BR>  In the meals of the rich, not only the recognition of violence but
violence itself is absent as well. As Margaret Visser points out, eating
involves violence: "We need to eat in order to live; yet even
eating vegetables means cutting and killing first" (Rituals of
Dinner 34). The modern day consumer can ignore the process of cutting
and killing. As Montanari points out, "In industrialized countries
[...] the ensemble of techniques needed for the preparation of daily
meals is much more limited and less burdensome, given that a large
number of the preliminary tasks have devolved onto professionals, and to
the agro-food industry" (33). However, medieval cooks needed to go
through all the steps of preparing the food. The Good Wife's Guide
illustrates what was involved in a simple recipe: "to make boudin,
collect the blood of a pig in a suitable basin or pan. When the pig has
been butchered and the haslet washed thoroughly and set to cook, while
it is cooking, remove the clots of blood from the bottom of the basin
and discard them" (271). The advice is a reminder that before
technology distanced the consumer from food production, the contact with
blood and dirt was necessary and direct. In Martin's elegant meal
descriptions, food arrives at the elite's table the way it comes to
the contemporary consumer: without any hint of what is involved in
procuring it. The violence, left out possibly by design, re-emerges with
a vengeance among those who lost or never had privilege.
<BR><BR>  If the lack of medical and religious principles in food choices
makes the meals seem arbitrary, the abstraction of violence from their
preparation and its accentuation in the actions of the dispossessed
leads to a new dichotomy, which replaces the feasting/fasting contrast.
The axis according to which food behaviors align themselves in the
novels separates eating for pleasure and eating for survival. Eating for
pleasure is different from feasting, because it is unrestrained and
lacks a spiritual dimension. Eating for survival, although including
enforced fasting, is also unrestrained because hunger can lead to
abominable acts. The restraints imposed on feasting, as well as its
alternation with fasting, sustain an ordered society, where human
actions aim for balance. In the novels, the contrast between the
sumptuous meals of the elite and the total deprivation of the
dispossessed dominates the evolving disorder. There are numerous other
differences with regard to food--between men and women, cities and
castles, lower and higher classes, different cultures of the different
kingdoms--but they are all subsumed to the main dichotomy between the
well-fed and the hungry.
<BR><BR>  This perhaps more primal dichotomy highlights an element downplayed
by the critical attention to the cultural significance of food.
Virtually every book about food starts by pointing out that eating is
both a biological necessity and an element of culture. When Barthes
directed attention to food as sign, code, grammar, he removed the
biological aspect as a source of interest. Food as biological necessity
becomes, however, prominent when survival is at stake. Caroline Walker
Bynum illustrates the types of behavior generated by famine:
<Pre>
   In the Europe of the later thirteenth and fourteenth centuries
   famine was on the increase again, after several centuries of
   agricultural growth and relative plenty. Vicious stories--of
   food-hording merchants, of cannibalism and infanticide, of sick
   adolescents left to die when they could no longer work--survive in
   the sources, suggesting a world in which hunger and even starvation
   was not uncommon. (2)
</Pre><BR><BR>  As Walker Bynum demonstrates, cruelty increases with the sharpening
hunger. War is expectedly attended by violence, but its extreme forms
begin to appear when the initially enthusiastic armies of the would-be
kings begin to exhaust their provisions. The fear of running out of food
leads to a Hobbesian outlook on humanity, where wolves look like the
nobler creatures (FFC 669). One of the minor characters describes the
process of dehumanization for want of food: "The broken man lives
from day to day, from meal to meal, more beast than man" (FFC 535).
This descent into animal savagery mutates from consumption of raw meat
to sheer cannibalism. Having to survive in the streets of King's
Landing, Arya quickly learns to catch pigeons and trades part of the
meat for cooking facilities and bread. Before discovering the trade,
however, she eats "a couple raw" (GOT 718). Imprisoned and
starved, Theon Greyjoy consumes a live rat in a scene that makes
Arya's eating a couple of raw pigeons seem casual: "The rat
squealed as he bit into it, squirming wildly in his hands, frantic to
escape. The belly was the softest part. He tore at the sweet meat, the
warm blood running over his lips. It was so good that it brought tears
to his eyes" (DWD 161). Theon's enjoyment, as well as the
sensory adjectives that Martin's description accumulates--soft,
sweet, warm--intensifies the horror of the scene. The gustatory
experience clashes with the personification of the rat, hinting at
cannibalism:
<BR><BR>  "He's been eating rats," said the second boy.
"Look."
<BR><BR>  The first boy laughed. "He has. That's funny."
<BR><BR>  I had to. The rats bit him when he slept, gnawing at his fingers
and his toes, even at his face, so when he got his hands on one he did
not hesitate. Eat or be eaten, those were the only choices. "I did
it, he mumbled, "I did, I did, I ate him, they do the same to me,
please ..." (DWD 162)
<BR><BR>  Driven insane by the mental and physical abuse he had been
subjected to, Theon does not differentiate between his own species and
the animal's any longer, but he eats it all the same.
<BR><BR>  If Theon debases himself to the level of the rat he is eating, a
renegade of the Lannister army, Biter, unleashes literal cannibalistic
instincts, as he attacks with his teeth rather than his sword. His
ferocity culminates in a scene described from Brienne's point of
view:
<BR><BR>  Biter's mouth gaped open impossibly wide. She saw his teeth,
yellow and crooked, filed into points. When they closed on the soft meat
of her cheek, she hardly felt it.... Biter's mouth tore free, full
of blood and flesh. He spat, grinned and sank his pointed teeth in to
her flesh again. This time he chewed and swallowed. He is eating me, she
realized, but she had no strength to fight him any longer. (FFC 798)
<BR><BR>  Brienne's capitulation comes from her awareness of the horror
rather than from pain. She is not squeamish about blood or a stranger to
injury, yet Biter's relish at chewing her face goes against
chivalric codes of honor, and it shatters her faith in humanity.
Although Biter's chivalry is doubtful even before this scene, it
becomes evident that hunger throws humanity into question.
<BR><BR>  The threat of losing their humanity faces the Stark children, who
have been raised to accept violence as necessary but to atone for it as
well. A friend and contemporary of Robert Baratheon, Eddard Stark
believes that "the man who passes the sentence should swing the
sword" and spends hours asking the gods' forgiveness after
decapitating a deserter from the Night's Watch (GOT 16). Atoning
for the violence necessary to maintain social order extends to reverence
for food. As demonstrated in the Harvest Feast scene, the Starks have a
way of acknowledging both the work and the sacrifice that goes into
getting the food to the table. It is thus not surprising they have
difficulty sharing their wolves' irredeemable cruelty when it comes
to satisfying hunger. The six direwolf puppies found in the snow and
given to the Stark children appear to be loyal pets at first, but as the
family disperse, have to face danger, and suffer hunger, the wolves
become their masters' sensory extensions, and on occasion, their
killing instruments. This special relation with the animals threatens to
exclude the Starks from human society, as it becomes apparent in
Bran's conversation with his friend, Jojen:
<BR><BR>  "Warg," said Jojen Reed. Bran looked at him, eyes wide.
"What?"
<BR><BR>  "Warg. Shapechanger. Beastling. That is what they will call
you, if they should ever hear of your wolf dreams."
<BR><BR>  The names made him afraid again. "Who will call me?"
<BR><BR>  "Your own folk. In fear. Some will hate you if they know what
you are. Some will even try to kill you."
<BR><BR>  Old Nan told him scary stories of beastlings and shapechangers
sometimes. In the stories they were always evil. "I'm not like
that," Bran said. "I'm not. It's only dreams."
(COK 523)
<BR><BR>  Bran refuses the "evil" identity, in spite of its appeal:
"'I am not a wolf.' Yet he howled with them in the night,
and tasted blood in his wolf dreams" (COK 438). More than once,
both Jon and Bran distance themselves from the wolves and refuse to
identify with them. Jon feels tempted to be one with the wolf, but he
turns the thought away: "The taste of blood filled Jon's
mouth, and he knew Ghost had killed that night. No, he thought, I am a
man not a wolf' (DWD 145). Lady Melisandre urges Jon to embrace the
power of the wolf: "'Power in you and in this beast. You
resist it and that is your mistake. Embrace it. Use it.' I am not a
wolf, he thought" (DWD 380). Robb uses the wolf in battle, both to
kill enemies and to inspire fear, but when Catelyn tries to convince him
that the direwolf has magic powers, he also says, "I am not a wolf,
no matter what they call me" (SOS 197).
<BR><BR>  In spite of their reluctance to do so, both Bran and Jon resort to
the animal's hunting skills when they are hungry. The chapters
narrated from Bran's point of view often start with the point of
view of the wolf: "Deer, and fear, and blood. The scent of prey
woke the hunger in him" (SOS 123). Within the same scene,
Bran's consciousness begins to detach from that of the wolf,
regrettably, when he is about to feed: "The rich warm taste of
blood faded on his tongue. No, don't, don't. I want to eat, I
want to, I want ..." (SOS 125). Hunger is a bond between the boy
and the animal, making them one. Jon also has moments when he slips into
his wolf's skin. When he has to decide whether to accept
Stannis's offer and become the lord of Winterfell, Jon finds
himself overcome by hunger: "It was a hunger in him, sharp as a
dragonglass blade. A hunger ... he could feel it. It was food he needed,
prey, a red deer that stank of fear or a great elk proud and defiant. He
needed to kill and fill his belly with fresh meat and hot dark
blood" (SOS 1091). At face value, the hunger seems to be a metaphor
for Jon's desire to get what he always wanted. Yet a moment later,
Jon realizes that Ghost has returned after a long absence, and it was
the wolf's hunger he was feeling. That insight makes him understand
that the desire to be the lord of Winterfell was the wolf's desire
to eat. His decision changes, but for a moment, Jon is the wolf. Arya
dreams about being a wolf and sees the destruction committed by her
animal self among other animals and people alike: "Lamb and dog and
mutton and the flesh of man. Some of her little grey cousins were afraid
of men, even dead men, but not her. Meat was meat and men were prey. She
was the night wolf' (DWD 593). A long way from where her wolf is
feeding, Arya recovers the sensations of the animal and shares its
ferocity. Although only in her dream, Arya also becomes the wolf.
<BR><BR>  A similar relationship with a heraldic symbol literalized as an
animal familiar develops between Daenerys and her dragons. She
identifies as "the blood of the dragon" when she needs courage
to perform a Dothraki ritual: "I am the blood of the dragon, she
told herself as she took the stallion's heart in both hands, lifted
it to her mouth, and plunged her teeth into the tough, stringy
flesh" (GOT 489). When the three dragon eggs she received as a gift
from Illyrio hatch in the funeral pyre of Kahl Drogo, Daenerys becomes
the "mother of dragons" (COK 189). Even before her
brother's death, when she arrives in Vaes Dothrak, she knows her
destiny relates to the dragons: "If I were not the blood of the
dragon, she thought wistfully, this could be my home" (GOT 586).
Although she never refuses identification with the animals, Daenerys is
ambivalent about her destiny. Her doubts can be seen in her diet. Having
been raised in many countries, Daenerys learns to accept whatever food
locals eat. On her own, however, Daenerys chooses figs or dates to
appease her hunger (DWD 43 and 398). Nevertheless, she is not able to
avoid eating meat, as she is not able to keep away from violence and
bloodshed. Stranded alone with the strongest of her dragons, Drogon,
Danaerys has no choice but to eat what he has killed:
<BR><BR>  Drogon consumed his kill there, tearing at the charred flesh as the
grasses burned around them, the air thick with drifting smoke and the
smell of burned horsehair. Dany, starved, slid off his back and ate with
him, ripping chunks of meat from the dead horse with bare, burned hands.
In Meereen I was a queen in silk, nibbling on stuffed dates and honeyed
lamb, she remembered. (DWD 942)
<BR><BR>  The revulsion at swallowing the bloody meat of the stallion's
heart is all forgotten, and Danaerys eats with the same abandon as
Drogon. Like Bran, she feels the animal's hunger. Like Arya, she
participates in the violent destruction of its prey. And like Jon, she
realizes the political implications of her acts, as she thinks about her
Meereeneze noble husband: "Hizdahr would be horrified, no
doubt" (DWD 942).
<BR><BR>  The close contact with animals created a similar tension for
medieval lords in the hunt par force, also called a force. Like Naomi
Sykes, Susan Crane argues that the hunt a force exhibits the power of
aristocracy, but she finds it has a drawback: "it brings humans
into contact with animals. Once a complete earthly hierarchy is posited,
with hunters dominating nature, intimacy with animals is potentially
abasing" (72). It is this potential, as well as the way aristocrats
might have felt about it, that might motivate the ambivalence of the
Starks regarding their relationship with animals. Stark's children,
as well as Danaerys, are the most evidently dispossessed, but they are
not willing to descend into uncontrolled violence, even when hunger
pushes them to do so. They lack, however, the practice of restraint of
the previous generation, represented by Eddard Stark. Crane reveals how
the ritual aspects of the hunt give higher meaning to the contact with
the animal. She concludes that "the hunters' proximity to
animals demonstrates the knowledge and understanding that make their
domination of animals appear fully human: rational as well as
courageous, and hierarchical in keeping with creation's
hierarchy" (79). Ritual contains the violence and makes it
acceptable in view of the needs it fulfills. Repelled by unrestrained
violence, both Danaerys and the Stark children seem to be searching for
means to integrate it in rational human experience.
<BR><BR>  The lack of restraint in food-related behavior with its attending
hedonism and violence is both the result and the sign of disorder. In
this respect, Martin's "medieval" society is similar to
our own. Michael Pollan speaks about "our national eating
disorder" (1). In the absence of traditional restraints on eating,
contemporary consumers think little about respecting the food. The
violence involved in procuring food is banished from the everyday
experience of eating. Like the Westeros elites, contemporary consumers
ignore the sources of their food: "The industrialization--and
brutalization--of animals in America is a relatively new, evitable, and
local phenomenon: No other country raises and slaughters its food
animals quite as intensively or as brutally as we do. No other people in
history has lived at quite so great a remove from the animals they
eat" (333). Warren Belasco agrees that the end of the millennium
found contemporary Americans "without having the slightest idea
where their food came from" (239). The alternative to this ignorant
eating is, in Pollan's view, "a kind of ritual, a meal that is
eaten in full consciousness of what it took to make it worth
preparing" (409). In addition to their other effects, food
descriptions give Martin's novels contemporary relevance.
<BR><BR>  Martin's use of medieval recipes, both in and outside their
ideological contexts, creates a world that is indeed "present in
and signified by food" (Barthes 23). The nature of the conflict
between those in power and those who have lost it reveals itself in the
way they are situated in relation to food. Although they flaunt their
power in food displays, the privileged remain ignorant of its origins
and neglect the spiritual dimension of eating. The dispossessed, on the
other hand, come too close to losing their humanity and becoming as
savage as animals through hunger. Between indulging and devouring, food
consumption does not find the ideal balance. This balance, suggested by
the Harvest Feast, might be seen as a state of grace from which the
novels' world has fallen. The selective use of historical evidence,
far from diminishing the significance of food in medieval society,
highlights the importance of both the social and the spiritual
dimensions of eating, which are also relevant to our time.
