Write On! Crowding and Leaping, according to LeGuin

Hello all. Welcome back to our weekly series on writing. We’re in the second week of our annual Daily Kos Writing (& Other Artistic Pursuits) Month. It’s a choose your own adventure/goal sort of thing, so if you missed the first week and want to join in the gentle encouragement/accountability check-ins, please chime in in the comments, and we’ll add you to the goal list.

For what it’s worth, I’ve been doing poorly at my goal of five minutes of writing per day. But! I am definitely counting writing this diary in my minute count, so after tonight, I’m going to call the early part of January adequately covered.

wandering in samsara – rough draft of third quarter of WIP (1/12 — aiming for 5 min/day)

Work is beyond crazy, and I’m exploring purchasing something through a low-income housing program. My brain is tired. Tired.

So, tonight I’m going to let Ms. Le Guin take the wheel. In keeping with the chapter and diary title, we’re leaping over a number of other chapters and going with Chapter 10 from Steering the Craft. I’ve referenced Chapters 2 (Punctuation — but really only the challenge) and 4 (Repetition) in other Write On diaries from yesteryear, and it’s been awhile since I last picked up Steering the Craft.

As the workshop went on [Steering the Craft came about after a writing workshop Le Guin led], I began to think about an aspect of narrative technique that I hadn’t originally seen clearly. It has to do with what details are included, what omitted. It has to do with focus — the focus of the sentence, the paragraph, the piece as a whole. I call it Crowding and Leaping because those words describe the process in a physical way, which I like.

Crowding is what Keats meant when he told poets “to load every rift with ore.” It’s what we mean when we exhort ourselves to avoid flabby language and clichés, never to use ten vague words where two will do, always to seek the vivid phrase, the exact word. By crowding, I mean keeping the story full, always full of what’s happening in it; keeping it moving, not slackening, and wandering into irrelevancies; keeping it interconnected with itself, rich with echoes forward and backward. (slight snip)

But leaping is just as important. What you leap over is what you leave out. And what you leave out is infinitely more than what you leave in. There’s got to be white space around the word, silence around the voice. Listing is not describing. Only the relevant belongs. (snip)

Tactically speaking, I’d say go ahead and crowd in the first draft — put everything in. Then in revising decide what counts and what tells; then cut and recombine until what’s left is what counts. Leap boldly. (snip)

(from the first two pages of the chapter)

There’s more to this chapter, but I’m going to stop here and maybe use the rest some other time. Possibly even next week, unless one of our stalwarts or a newbie would like to contribute a diary. (Schedule’s a bit bare, folks.)

Given that this is the last chapter of the book, the exercise instructs the reader to choose a passage written in one of the other chapter exercises (about 400 words give or take 100 will do tonight) — and then cut the word count by half, while “keeping the narrative clear and the sensory impact vivid.” Also, strike the word ‘somehow’ wherever it has poked its head into your writing. Dialogue or long speeches get just as viciously chopped. Herein we create the above-mentioned ‘leaps.’

Tonight’s Challenge

If you’ve written a Write-On challenge in the past (month, season, year, etc.), go back and pull one up (300-500 words is all that’s necessary). Chop as described above. If you like, share the original word count and the whittled word count along with the shortened piece.

If you haven’t written a Write On challenge before or would like to write something new to take apart, feel free to use some of the following prompts to generate a 300-ish word piece — then slash as above.

Silly Prompts

Along with Steering the Craft, I dug out The Toughguide to Fantasyland, the charming, hysterical actual guidebook companion to Dianna Wynne Jones’ wonderful book, The Dark Lord of Derksholm. You must always first consult the map…

1. Forest of Doom, Nine Apes Mountains, Island of Dragons (where your characters are)

2. Confrontation type and flavor: small, medium, or large AND friendly, ambiguous, or hostile

3. Including: an elf, a dragon, or a sprite

4. An unlikely magical object.

Write On! will be a regular Thursday night diary (8 pm Eastern, 5 pm Pacific) until it isn’t.
