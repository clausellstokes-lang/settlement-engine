**`Disasters & Famine`: NO reserves, hospital present**

1. `[ledger]` `[plain]` The hospital at {settlement} can treat an outbreak and hold it where it starts. The town keeps nothing back against a bad harvest, so a failed crop becomes hardship inside the same season.
   - `[face]` A fever can be treated at {settlement}, and its spread stopped. The town holds no grain over against a lean year, and a poor crop within the season brings want.
   - `[face]` The sick at {settlement} can be taken in and kept apart; nothing is laid by for the year a harvest fails, and hardship comes with the failing crop.
   - `[face]` An outbreak at {settlement} can be treated and confined. The town stores no food against a bad harvest, and the hardship falls in the season of the failure.
2. `[street]` `[plain]` The town is better set against sickness than against hunger, and takes no comfort in the fact. The usual arrangement runs the other way.
   - `[face]` Sickness is met better here than hunger is. The town takes no ease from that, and the order is uncommon.
   - `[face]` A plague would meet more preparation here than a poor harvest would, and the town is not eased by that: an ordering of that kind is rare.
   - `[face]` This town can meet a sickness more readily than a hungry season, and is not comforted by the order. Towns ordinarily hold the reverse.
3. `[unfolding]` `[plain]` The provision at {settlement} runs to the sickness the town has met and stops short of the hunger it has not, and the difference stands where it was set.
   - `[face]` A hospital stands against the sickness {settlement} has met, and nothing stands against the hunger it has not. Nothing in hand is making up the difference.
   - `[face]` Care for the sick at {settlement} matches a trouble the town has known, and no store matches the one it has not. The town is making no move to change the arrangement.
   - `[face]` What answers sickness at {settlement} is a hospital, and what answers hunger is nothing at all. The town has known the first trouble and not the second, and the arrangement is not being changed.

## B. Refusals

- **R1 · Variant 1's third claim is refused three ways by the card.** `a crop failure becomes hardship the same season it happens` asserts a season, a cause and a second fact, all three barred by the card's `may NOT` line. It is the parent's claim and dropping it would break claim-equality and red the C-pair arm (R-DA-20 as amended: the AFTER carries the BEFORE's typed claim set), so it is written in every wording and refused here. **The variant cannot be made lawful without dropping a claim.**
- **R2 · Variant 2's second claim generalises over settlements.** `an unusual way round` asserts what is usual elsewhere. No field of DS-DEF-2 holds a distribution over towns, and R-DA-12's generalisation test is engaged. Inherited, kept, unfixable in words.
- **R3 · Variant 2's third claim is a feeling.** `does not comfort anyone` is a verdict on the arrangement. MOVE-GRAMMAR §1.3 lists FEELING and VERDICT as moves that do not exist in the estate, and the card licenses no such claim. Inherited, kept, unfixable in words.
- **R4 · Variant 3's history has no provenance field.** `the sickness it has seen and not the hunger it has not` asserts what the town has and has not experienced. This is the sharpest wall breach in the pool: R-DA-19 and MOVE-GRAMMAR §1.2 row 2 bar the HISTORY move from R1 STATE outright, not merely off this card. Inherited, kept, unfixable without dropping a claim.
- **R5 · Variant 3's fourth claim is a second fact, and in one wording it is a second adjacent absence.** `nothing in hand is correcting the imbalance` is barred by `may NOT: a second fact`. Because it necessarily follows claim (b), which is itself an absence, the plain reading would also breach MOVE-GRAMMAR wall 3 (an ABSENCE never sits beside another ABSENCE). Three of the four wordings cure the wall by stating the claim as a standing condition of the arrangement rather than as an absence; the second `[face]` retains the absence form deliberately, so the pool holds one wording in the parent's own shape for the refuters to compare. The claim itself stays refused in all four.
- **R6 · The card contradicts itself on the standpoint.** `angle:` names `ledger street unfolding`; `may NOT:` bars "a standpoint". The `[street]` and `[unfolding]` tags are standpoints and are not mine to touch. Written under the `angle` clause. A chair ruling is owed on which line governs; the same conflict is raised in the DS-DEF-1 packets, so it is a card-grammar question and not a pool question.
- **R7 · Every variant carries more than one fact.** The card's `may NOT` bars "a second fact"; variants 1 and 2 carry three claims and variant 3 carries four. A1 permits one or two sentences and the register card's amendment S2 permits a computed consequence to ride as a clause, so this is a card-versus-register conflict rather than a writer's fault.
