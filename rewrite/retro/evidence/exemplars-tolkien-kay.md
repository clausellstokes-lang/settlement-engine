# EXEMPLARS — TOLKIEN AND KAY, RE-CUT AS GUIDEPOSTS

Seat: an Opus writer for the chair, 2026-09-12, under ADDENDUM 14. Sources read whole: `prose-research/sweep/section-tolkien.md` (874 claims, 641 kept) and `section-kay.md` (1,103 claims, 631 kept). Grounding read: the DS-DEF-11 skeletons and cured faces in the dock, `prose-research/REGISTER-CARD.md`, ADDENDA 12, 13A, 13B, 14, 15.

## 0. HOW TO READ THIS

**Nothing in this file is a rule.** No clause here may be cited by a refuter, a gate, a judge or a curer as a ground for failing a face. The refuter has exactly four floors (no self-contradiction against the whole town's record or the engine's model; no invented history, date or number; product scope and the deity doctrine as ADDENDUM 15 amends it; the engine's positive model) and one craft verdict at the pool grain: **dull**. This pack exists to help you fail that last one less often.

It is written to be read once before you write, and then put down. It is aim, not audit.

**Your subject, exactly.** One fantasy town. A handful of facts the engine actually recorded about it. A sentence, or two, or three. Read by a game master who is scanning a page for something to run with on Thursday. That reader does not audit you. They are looking for a thing to point at and say *there* — a door, a grievance, a habit, a cost. Give them one and the sentence has done its work. Give them a receipt and they scroll.

The two authors below were swept for 1,977 claims and the programme converted the findings into bars. That was the mistake ADDENDUM 14 reverses. What follows is the same research pointed the right way: **the moves**.

## PART I — THE MOVES THAT MAKE A PLACE INHABITED

### 1. The telling particular, drawn from the fact you already hold

Jo Walton's line on Kay is the whole test: the details are "real enough to bite" (Walton, on *Sarantium*). Kay's biting details are not extra facts smuggled in; they are the recorded fact rendered at the grain a person would meet it at. A tariff is met as a price. A levy is met as a household short one pair of hands. An unfunded wall is met as work promised and pay not found.

You have `walls.present` and `economicGates.military < 1`. That is two facts and twelve renderings are wanted. You cannot get twelve renderings of two facts. You can get twelve **meetings** with the town where those two facts hold.

- Flat: `A {defwork} stands at {settlement}. The town's muster is short of its pay.`
- Met: `The {defwork} at {settlement} stands, and the keeping of it is promised out of a purse that does not stretch; the muster's wages come short by the same measure.`
- Met, another way: `At {settlement} the {defwork} is up and the work of keeping it is not in dispute. What is in dispute is the wage.`

Nothing in the second or third is a new fact. Both are the same shortfall, met at a place where a person stands.

### 2. Land the great fact on the small household

Kay's most-repeated principle across the sweep, in his own words: the death of an emperor far off matters less to a farmhand than the hired hand's broken leg. Critics call it "scaling down epic events" (Capossere). The dossier's version: **a civic fact lands on a trade, a door, or a household, in the same unit.**

- The upkeep shortfall lands on: the coin the muster was promised · the keeping-work that is done anyway · the season's work not let out to contract.
- The wall's presence lands on: what is inside it and what is not · the ground the carters use · the gate the town's day is organised around.

The person you land on is unnamed and stays unnamed. ADDENDUM 14 licensed exactly this: an unnamed person may act, hold a key, be avoided, be resented. That is a very large door and almost no face in the corpus has walked through it.

`At {settlement} the {defwork} is kept up by men who are owed for the keeping.`

### 3. Show a place through what someone would notice, avoid, or complain about

Kay's exposition arrives as what a resident finds unusual (Rettino's reading of the veiled omniscience). The GM's version of this is the most usable sentence you can write, because a complaint is a hook with no invention in it.

Three shapes:
- **Noticed:** the thing a stranger's eye goes to first. `What a stranger sees first at {settlement} is the {defwork}; what the town talks about is what it costs.`
- **Avoided:** the thing locals route around. `The approach is open; the gate is not where anyone in a hurry goes.`
- **Complained of:** the standing grievance. `The keeping of the {defwork} is the town's steadiest complaint and its largest standing expense.` The shape worth stealing is that one thing is both the grievance and the fact. (Note the near-miss: *oldest* complaint would smuggle in duration, which is floor 2's ground. *Steadiest* costs nothing.)

A complaint is safe ground under the re-cut: it predicates nothing new of the record, it is atmosphere, and it hands the GM a scene.

### 4. Absence, stated in the record's own forms

The single most transferable thing in the whole Kay sweep is not from Kay's novels — it is the gazetteer entry on his world, which states what is *not* known in four clean forms. Paraphrasing the moves:

- **The unaccounted** — two people went in; neither has given an account of what they saw.
- **The disputed** — which came first is a matter of some dispute, and the entry does not settle it.
- **The reported** — there have been reports of a change, unconfirmed.
- **The controlled** — access is by leave, and the leave is somebody's to give.

Each of these is a plot hook with a hole in the middle, and none of them invents anything. Under floor 2 you may not say *when* the wall was raised. You may say the record does not hold it:

`Who ordered the {defwork} at {settlement} is not recorded, and the town gives more than one answer.`

The gazetteer also shows the boundary: having said the prehistory is hazy, it then narrates the prehistory. Don't. Say the record is silent and stop.

### 5. Two accounts, neither settled

Kay keeps competing versions side by side and adjudicates neither; Tolkien's depth effect runs on the same engine — inconsistent accounts, texts in different hands, a background darker than the foreground. Shippey's phrase for the result is "the impression of a real and complex history."

One matter left standing open per town is already the register card's instruction, and it is the cheapest richness available:

`The muster's shortfall is put down to the levy in one telling and to the purse in another.`

Note what this does *not* do: it does not assert a cause. It asserts that the town disagrees about the cause. That is atmosphere, not a fact the engine may later compute differently.

### 6. The bill in the same unit as the good

McBean's two sentences on Kay: "Triumphs are provisional. Victories are costly." The dossier's translation: **no civic good without its cost in the same block, and no cost without the thing it buys.**

`The {defwork} at {settlement} stands whole, and the standing of it is the largest thing the town pays for.`

The wall stands through the shortfall — that is the engine's own recorded asymmetry as R-viii′ corrected it (one purse, the wall keeps standing, the muster's strength is the other desk's cell). It is also, as a sentence, exactly the move: the good and the bill in one breath.

### 7. Belief as civic fact, with no wink

Kay's rule is to make the world as its inhabitants believe it to be, which "removes that smugness from the reader." The archivist reports a ward's belief flat: the east gate is not crossed at dusk; the well is known to be lucky. No irony, no distancing, no "locals claim."

Under ADDENDUM 15 this now extends to a creed's people. The god is never a subject; the congregation is. A warlike creed's people are impatient with delay; a cult's people have a door and no street. That is conduct, consonant with the record's axes, and it is the most under-used licence in the estate.

### 8. The mediating craft is a lexicon, never a metaphor

Kay on his own method: "you don't blend colours – you contrast them," the mosaicist's principle. The sweep's own resolution is the useful part: one craft supplies the town's **nouns** — the tannery's slack season, the salt-pan, the malt-house — and it never generates a figure. `The council wove its answer` is the failure. `The council sits in the tannery's slack season` is the move.

For a defense pool, the craft is the keeping itself: work, contract, wage, purse, roll, upkeep, the season when the work is let. Reach into that box for nouns and the vocabulary stops repeating on its own.

### 9. Contrast adjacent tiles

Blocks laid side by side, contrasting in scale and rank — the seat of power and the trade beside it. Within a pool this is the cure for the collapse the audit measured: if variant 1 is the ledger's flat measure, variant 2 should stand somewhere else entirely — at the gate, in the purse, in the town's grumbling — and use a different grammar to get there. Four faces that differ only in synonyms are one face.

### 10. Depth by casual incomplete mention

Tolkien's depth is casual mention of a thing older than the story, never explained. Under floor 2 you may not date it or narrate it. You may let it stand unexplained as a present-tense fact:

`The {defwork} at {settlement} does not run the whole way round, and the town has stopped remarking on it.`

Whether that is licensed depends on the record — if the roster or another surface says the works enclose the town, this contradicts and is a defect. Where the record is silent, silence is permission, and an unexplained present-tense oddity is the highest-value hook per word in the whole form.

## PART II — THE SENTENCE

### 11. Strike the empty words; put the weighted noun first

Tolkien's own diagnosis, from the letter the sweep cites six times: modern English is "regrettably looser, more full of little 'empty' words." Strike *there is*, *it was*, *very*, *that*, *in terms of*. The noun the sentence is about goes early and carries its own weight.

- Flat: `In place at {settlement} is a {defwork}, and the upkeep of the muster is unmet.`
- Weighted: `{settlement} keeps a {defwork} up and its muster underpaid.`

### 12. The verb carries the manner

Tolkien's descriptive verbs do the work adverbs would otherwise do (loom, plod, gleam, stride). Civic equivalents: *stands, holds, carries, comes short, falls to, is let, is owed, goes unmade, keeps*. If you have written an adverb of manner, the verb was the wrong one.

### 13. How a sentence earns its second clause

This is the dullness fault the audit measured — seventy-one percent of units were a two-clause `and` join, and most of those second clauses restated the first. **A second clause earns its place by bringing a new noun, a consequence, or a turn.** It never earns it by restating the read it follows in different words.

- Unearned: `A {defwork} stands at {settlement}, and the town has a {defwork} it does not fully fund.`
- Earned (new noun): `A {defwork} stands at {settlement}, and the purse that keeps it is the same one the muster is paid from.`
- Earned (consequence): `The {defwork} stands, so the shortfall shows up as wages and not as stone.`
- Earned (turn): `The {defwork} stands. What it costs to stand is another matter.`

Two sentences beat a limp join. The short line exists; use it.

### 14. Rhythm, and the test of being read aloud

Le Guin's finding on Tolkien: a plain two-beat alternation, "one stress every two to four syllables," stress and release, with punctuation falling where a reader would breathe. Kay's own doctrine is the counterpart — a page read aloud is not the same instrument as a page read alone.

The dossier is read silently but it is *spoken at a table*, which is the GM reading it to players or paraphrasing it aloud. So: read your face aloud once. If you stumble, the clause order is wrong. If it scans like verse, it is costume. If the comma is doing the work a full stop should, cut it — readers on both authors' pages complain about exactly that comma.

### 15. Parataxis at the event, subordination at rest

Turner's term for Tolkien's chained clauses is "loose semantic fit": at a moment of event the clauses chain with *and* and the causal word is withheld; settled description may subordinate. For a dossier that inverts cleanly — the standing fact takes the calm periodic sentence, and the one moment of pressure in a town's record gets the chain:

`The wage is short, and the work is done, and the roll is carried as it was.`

Use once per pool at most. It is Kay's one permitted sentence-level figure and it is reserved for loss.

### 16. Fronting once, as a turn

Tolkien's inversions mark climaxes; the corpus finds them rarer than critics assume, which is the actual lesson. **Once per unit, never as the default order.** `Kept up, the {defwork} is; paid for, it is not` is costume. `What the {defwork} costs, the town pays out of one purse` is a turn.

### 17. One flat contemporary line per unit, for a fact that is done with

The sweep's best small find is the reviewer's note on the first page of *The Lions of Al-Rassan*: a short, flat, contemporary line among the elevated ones, for a matter that is finished. It is the plainest sentence in the paragraph and it is why the elevated ones land.

`The {defwork} at {settlement} stands. That much is settled.`

### 18. Begin on a noun; repeat the name rather than reach for the pronoun

Kullmann and Siepmann's corpus finding — Tolkien underuses sentence-initial pronouns and repeats names instead. This also happens to dissolve the possessor-binding defect the refuters kept catching: a sentence-initial *Its* after a `{defwork}` subject binds to the wall. Naming the town again is not a stylistic sin in this register; it is the register.

### 19. The stative passive, and the existential opener

Also from the same corpus chapter: the stative passive is far commoner than the eventive (*the wall is built of stone*, not *the wall was built*), and events come in as *there came*. The gazetteer's own register does the same in a reference voice: access is controlled; the palace has been rebuilt. For a standing record this is the native grammar, and the passive is legitimate here where an agent would be an invention.

### 20. Three exact things, and the rest outline

The reconciliation the Tolkien sweep reaches between the "exact eye" critics and the "vague outline" critics: **the exactness is in the chosen nouns, the vagueness in what is left unsaid around them.** Walker's phrase for the effect is that the prose lures the reader into filling the blanks. Name three exact civic things per unit at most. Do not describe the rest. The GM fills it and believes they helped make the place.

### 21. A raised register is always attached to a standpoint

Kay's modulation is never free-floating: the priest's procession is high, the sweeper's same procession is plain. If a face lifts, lift it *from* somewhere. An elevated sentence with no standpoint is the thing readers call flowery, and thirty-six readers in the sweep said so about Kay in exactly those words.

## PART III — WHAT TIRED PROSE DOES (taste, not bars)

Le Guin's warning about this register is the sharpest line in either file: "It's a high wire: one slip spoils all." What follows is what the slips look like. None of these is refusable. All of them are why a pool reads dead.

- **The two-clause `and` join, four times running.** The single measured fault of the landed block. If three of your four faces join two clauses with *and*, one of them is now two sentences and another now starts with the second clause.
- **The summarising second half.** `…, and so the town's defenses are strained.` The record never explains what it just said. Cut the clause; nothing is lost.
- **The safe-word tic.** When one approved word appears once every twenty-one words — *country*, *standing*, *upkeep* — the pool has a vocabulary of one. The cure is the craft lexicon (move 8), not a thesaurus.
- **Elegant abstraction.** *weight, testament, mark, echo, presence, sense of, the reality of.* Every one of these can be deleted and replaced with the civic noun underneath it. `a testament to the town's priorities` → `the largest thing the town pays for`.
- **Triads by habit.** Three of anything, three times, is a tell. The register card already says it; the sweep's version is Tolkien's warning against reaching for the pattern rather than the thing.
- **The not-X-but-Y frame.** *Not a fortress but a promise.* It reads as insight and asserts nothing. One per estate, if that.
- **The maxim.** *Stone keeps itself, and wages do not.* This was in the shipped corpus, it is beautiful, and it is the one thing both the entailment law and the owner's own reading struck — not for being a maxim, but because the engine's model says otherwise. The general lesson survives: a sentence that would be true of any town is not about this town. Kay's aphorisms are the most-resented thing in his corpus; four independent readers in the sweep name them.
- **The inventory line.** A face that states the key fact with no angle and no second read. It passed every gate the programme ever built and it is the reason the re-cut happened.
- **The hook question, the address, the uplift, the digit, the em dash.** House conventions, and they are also just tells.
- **The adjective pile.** The complaint readers make about Kay most often, by hand count: adjectives per noun, sentence length, commas. Kay's own best pages are described as nearly adjective-free. Ration the adjective to the noun that cannot stand without one.
- **Costume.** *Yon*, *mayhap*, *-eth*, *from whence*. Tolkien's own practice is the opposite: current words in older patterns. The antique air lives in the noun — moot, tithe, bailey, muster — never in the verb.

## PART IV — ONE RECORD, SIX WAYS

The record: a `{defwork}` is present; the military upkeep gate is below one. Nothing else. No count, no date, no cause, no manning. Four floors respected in every line below; the label traps are the one place taste still has to check the roster.

1. `[ledger]` `The {defwork} at {settlement} stands on a purse that comes up short. The keeping of it and the muster's wage are paid from the same place.`
2. `[ledger]` `{settlement} keeps a {defwork}. What it costs to keep is more than the town finds.`
3. `[unfolding]` `The {defwork} is up at {settlement}, and the wage behind it lies open: the work is done, the coin is owed, and the roll is carried as it was.`
4. `[visitor]` `A stranger coming to {settlement} meets the {defwork} first and hears about the cost of it second.`
5. `[street]` `At {settlement} the {defwork} is nobody's grievance and its upkeep is everybody's.`
6. `[elder]` `The {defwork} at {settlement} has always been the town's largest standing expense, and it is not met whole.` *(check: "always" reads as history — cut to "is the town's largest standing expense")*

Six meetings, one fact, six constructions, six vocabularies, no invention. Two of them carry a self-check in the margin, which is what an honest draft looks like.

## PART V — SIX THINGS TO ASK BEFORE YOU HAND THE POOL OVER

Not a gate. The questions a good writer asks themselves.

1. **Would a GM point at any line and say "I can run that"?** If every line is a receipt, the pool is dull however lawful.
2. **Do the faces differ in construction, or only in words?** Read the four first words of each aloud in order.
3. **Does every second clause bring a new noun, a consequence, or a turn?**
4. **Is there one exact civic noun per unit that is not in the card's own vocabulary?**
5. **Does the pool leave one thing standing open** — unaccounted, disputed, reported, or controlled?
6. **Read aloud: does anything scan, stumble, or preach?** If a sentence would be true of any town, it is not about this one.
