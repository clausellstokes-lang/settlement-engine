# THE EXEMPLAR PACK — LE GUIN AND WOLFE AS GUIDEPOSTS

Sources: `prose-research/sweep/section-leguin.md` (29 features, 666 verified rows) and `section-wolfe.md` (42 features, 766 verified rows), both read whole. Those two documents were built as **constraints to avoid**. This one is the same research turned the other way up: the **moves to aim at**.*

---

## 0. HOW TO READ THIS

**Nothing in this file is a rule.** No clause of it is ever a refuter's finding. A refuter may cite only a contradiction of the record, an invented history or number, a scope or deity breach, or the engine's own positive model. If a face in this pack's voice is charged with anything else, the charge is void.

This is what you read **before** you write, the way a painter looks at paintings. You aim at it. You miss a lot of it. Missing it is not a fault; not aiming is.

**Your subject, exactly.** One fantasy town. A handful of recorded facts, often only one. A sentence or three. The reader is a game master with a session on Thursday, scanning a panel for something to run with. He is not auditing you. He will change whatever he likes. What he wants from you is **a place he can stand in and a thing he can use.** Le Guin's sharpest instruction in the whole sweep is her test of a sentence: does it sound right. Wolfe's is four words on showing a virtue: "You simply show it and shut up." (Wolfe, *Black Gate*, 2010.)

## PART I — THE MOVES

### 1. The telling particular: one object, handled

The sweep's single most-attested Le Guin move. She halves a passage while keeping the sensory impact; she asks whether the doorknob needs mentioning and answers no. Her working line is that exact and vivid words make an exact and vivid world, and its negative is that a general sentence implies almost nothing.

Her own demonstration is worth the whole feature. She writes two sentences about an invented town. The first places it on a coast and, in her words, "This general description implies very little". The second names traders, windships and pelts, and the reader learns the town has a long-established trading economy **without being told it**. Same fact, same length. The difference is that the second sentence contains things a person could pick up.

> **Flat:** {settlement} maintains its {defwork} at considerable expense.
> **Aimed:** The {defwork} at {settlement} costs the town more than the mill does, and the town pays it the way it pays for weather.

The object must be one a resident **handles**: the bar of the gate, the tally stick, the cup, the wet thatch, the cracked ice. Not decoration. Le Guin: "Only the relevant belongs."

### 2. The detail that implies a life

Wolfe's version of the same move is harder and better: he does not describe the life, he describes the **work**. His own reason is that a long work which never shows people earning their living falls prey to a creeping sense of unreality. The tanner is at his pit; the clerk is at his desk; the guild is "matter of fact about servicing their clients" (a first-time reader of the *New Sun*, quoted in the sweep).

So: not "the settlement is poor", which is a verdict, but the thing a poor town does on a Tuesday.

> **Flat:** The muster is underfunded and morale is low.
> **Aimed:** The muster at {settlement} is short of its wage, and drill has quietly become the sort of thing that happens if the weather is fine.

Note what the aimed line did not do. It invented no number, no date, no desertion, no history. It invented a **habit**, and a habit is atmosphere.

### 3. Show the place through what someone would notice, avoid, or complain about

This is the move the record register was starving for, and ADDENDUM 14 has just licensed it outright: an unnamed person may appear, act, keep a key, be avoided, be resented.

Le Guin's Omelas is built almost entirely from civic lacks: no king, no slaves, no stock exchange, no advertisements. Wolfe's institutions are built from procedure and silence: what the office executes, for whom, in what order, and what its officers must have known for the rite to proceed at all. Lackey, working the *Urth* list, gets a whole social fact out of a maid who prompts calmly in a ceremony: she is calm because she has done it many times, therefore she has been there a long time, therefore the masters knew who she was.

Three reliable angles into any town fact:

- **What a stranger notices.** ("The gate is open when you arrive and nobody makes anything of it.")
- **What a resident avoids.** ("There is a stretch of the wall the children are told to walk past.")
- **What somebody complains about.** ("Ask who pays for the {defwork} and you will be told, at length, that it is not the person you asked.")

That third one is nearly free hook material. A complaint is a faction, a grievance and an NPC voice in nine words.

### 4. Make the absence vivid

Both authors converge here and it is the most underused move in the corpus.

Le Guin declares a gap **as a fact of the archive**, in the archive's own flat voice: the copy was damaged in transit; the circumstances were not disclosed; there is no record of further contact. The gap is never apologised for. Wolfe goes further: the entry stops before the death and the next entry opens after it, and the terrible thing is entered by the rites before it and the reckoning after it. His readers name the sharpest version: a habit of the record, a height always given, a name always spelled, becomes a signal the one time it is missing.

For a town with **no** wall, no watch, no market, this is the whole game. An absence is not a hole in the dossier; it is the dossier's most usable sentence.

> **Flat:** {settlement} has no wall.
> **Aimed:** Nothing marks where {settlement} ends; the town and the country have simply agreed to differ.
> **Aimed:** {settlement} has never built a circuit, and has got so used to not having one that visitors are the only people who mention it.

(The first aimed line is the shipped corpus's own, and it is excellent. The shipped text was often better than what replaced it; read it before you write.)

### 5. The second clause has to earn itself

The chair's audit found that seventy-one per cent of rewritten units were a two-clause `and` join, and that the second clause usually **restated the first**. That is the doubled beat, and it is the single biggest reason the block read flat.

A second clause is worth having when it does one of four things:

1. **Turns the fact into a consequence.** "...and the town has learned to plan around it."
2. **Puts the fact in somebody's mouth or eye.** "...and no one at {settlement} will say so to a stranger."
3. **Reverses it.** Le Guin's own record sentence does this on a semicolon: the farmers depend on the herds; the herds do not depend on them. Her doctrine is that reversal pairs are not binary flips, each half containing the other.
4. **Withholds.** "...and the reason is nobody's business but the town's."

Wolfe's rule for dialogue is the general rule for clauses: a line is admitted only if it does several jobs at once. "Dialogue is action." (Wolfe, interviewed, 1980s.) Anything that only passes the salt is cut.

> **Doubled:** A {defwork} stands at {settlement}, and the town keeps a {defwork}.
> **Earned:** A {defwork} stands at {settlement}, and nobody alive has needed it, which is the sort of luck a town stops noticing.

### 6. The exact old word in the noun slot, plain sentence around it

Wolfe's most-verified habit, attested twenty-three ways: the strange words are **real, old and rare**, none coined, each standing in the noun slot of a short plain sentence whose other nouns make the sense clear. Asked whether any term in the *New Sun* was invented, he answered: "None." Four independent readers give the same defence of it: the ornament sits in the noun slot and the sentence around it is plain.

Le Guin comes at the same ground from the other side and is the better guide for us: prefer the native word to the Latinate, and the old common word to the technical one. The sweep's line-reader put it exactly: she will not say crenellation, she will say roof and tower.

So: **hearth, byre, ward, toll, kin, muster, roll, gate, purse, keeping.** Not: residence, jurisdiction, infrastructure, remuneration, fortification expenditure. One uncommon word to a passage, never two in a sentence, and never a coinage.

### 7. The reversal, held on a semicolon

Le Guin's record sentences do this repeatedly, and the shipped corpus does it well: "walls are easier to keep than to raise, and {settlement} keeps this one." The shape is a small antithesis that turns a bare fact into a piece of reasoning the reader can feel the town doing. Her praise of her model annalist is that he moves from a riot to a dinner without even a period, only a semicolon.

> {settlement} pays little for its {defwork} now that it asks little of it; built work stands on its own patience.

That is the shipped line. It states the upkeep read, the quiet read, and a whole civic attitude, in one breath, with a semicolon doing the work an explanation would otherwise do.

### 8. A thing is known by what it does, never by its adjective

Wolfe, in his numbered advice and in three independent relays: the novice's paragon has virtues that "are only talked about. Never shown." The dossier therefore never says a family is cruel or a guild honest. It records what the family did and what the deed cost, and stops.

Le Guin's parallel is her adjective doctrine, which **rations and does not ban**: where the quality can go into the noun or the verb, it goes there. A growl, not a growling voice. Her line-reader's finest observation: the archmage is "cold, very cold", not frigid, not subzero, because the plain word repeated leaves the reader room to feel.

> **Stated:** The watch at {settlement} is corrupt and unpopular.
> **Shown:** At {settlement} the matter goes to the watch, and then it goes quiet.

### 9. The institution rendered by its procedure and its silence

Wolfe's guilds have offices, oaths and **silences**. A guild closes ranks over a scandal the way any office does, and the entry records the closing, not the scandal. Who is buried where is evidence of rank. The function of a building is not announced; it emerges in the course of the entry that uses it. Kelly's example is the whole method in eight words: it develops, as you read, that the large house is a brothel.

For us: never "the court handles disputes" (a category). Instead, the sequence, the exception, and the thing everyone knows and nobody writes down.

> The court at {settlement} sits when there is something to sit on, and the cases that never reach it are the ones everybody can name.

### 10. The dry note

Le Guin's humour in the record register is one move and one move only: wry cataloguing inside a plain statement of fact. Her handbook delivers a fiscal verdict on an entire people in two words: "Currently untaxable". Her editorial notes explain things dryly. Mitchell's finding on her dialogue is the guard rail: warmth and humour, "but no knowing wisecracks".

> The tithe barn is the largest building at {settlement} and the emptiest.

No wink. No joke told. The humour is in the arrangement of two true things.

### 11. The withheld thing leaves its trace

Wolfe's central engine, thirty-one sources deep: every fact the record withholds leaves **objective evidence** in the record, placed before the reader needs it, in the plain manner of everything around it, never labelled as evidence. Gaiman's rule for reading him: "Trust the text implicitly. The answers are in there."

We are not writing puzzles. But the move scales down beautifully to a three-sentence panel, because a GM reading a trace **invents the answer himself**, and that is the best thing a dossier can do to a GM.

> At {settlement} the {defwork} has a stretch the town does not use, and the reason is older than anyone who would tell you.

Nothing is asserted. A GM has a plot before he finishes the line. (Careful: this is atmosphere, not a receipt. It invents no history, no number and no decay. It is exactly as lawful as it is useful.)

### 12. One odd particular, left unexplained

Nine sources on Wolfe converge: an entry admits **one** strange particular among its ordinary furniture and explains nothing, and the sentences around it stay level. A green moon named as weather. A bar of iron at the height of a man's groin. The reader's word for it in the sweep: these little opacities feel realer than any explanation.

The level tone is the whole trick. If the sentence flinches, the particular becomes a special effect.

> A {defwork} stands at {settlement}, and the stretch beside the mill has been left to the brambles on purpose.

### 13. Deep time, reckoned vaguely

This is the licensed substitute for the dates floor 2 forbids, and it is not a consolation prize; it is a better move. Wolfe's rule, twelve sources: the record never numbers its ages. It says **many generations, before the founding, in living memory, longer than anyone troubles to say**. The older past survives as records nobody reads and relics with forgotten purposes, and the entry names the relic, not the age.

> **Refused (floor 2):** The {defwork} was raised three winters ago.
> **Aimed:** The {defwork} at {settlement} is older than the arrangement that pays for it.

The second is *more* evocative than the date, and it costs the promise nothing.

### 14. The close: short, and carrying past the entry

Eight sources on Wolfe. The last sentence of an entry is short, may be two words, does not summarise, and carries its implication past the end. Gevers calls it a slingshot; Keeley's version is that it asks the reader to reconsider what came before. Le Guin's counterpart: the shortest sentence is saved for the fact that matters most, and a dossier never ends on its earthquake.

> ...The books say what it went on instead. Nobody asks.

The summarising last clause ("...which means the town is vulnerable") is the opposite of this move and is the corpus's most common ending. Cut it and the entry gets better instantly.

### 15. Cadence: it is read aloud, at a table

Le Guin's test of a sentence is whether it sounds right, and her diagnostic is exact and usable: read it aloud, and wherever you hesitate there is a fault to be moved or removed. Her second rule is that there is no optimum sentence length, only variety, and her line-reader's measurement of her practice is that she **saves the shortest sentence for the highest impact**.

Wolfe measured is the same shape from the other end: after 1980 his narration sits around a dozen words a sentence, a third of them under eight, almost none over forty, with the variation high. A run of short sentences broken by one long one. Never a steady march.

Our panels are one to three sentences, so the whole of this collapses into one instruction: **do not write two sentences of the same length and the same grammar.** If sentence one is a nine-word `X stands at Y` and sentence two is a nine-word `Z is short at Y`, you have written a metronome, and every reader hears it.

### 16. Repetition across the pool, with variation

The pool, not the face, is now the unit a refuter can call DULL. Le Guin's coherence engine is repetition with variation, never verbatim: a refrain that recurs identically is mechanical. Her warning applies exactly to our twelve-renderings problem: if the rhythms become predictable, coherence becomes mechanical.

So write the pool as a set. Before you hand it over, read the twelve faces down the page in one pass and ask: how many grammars, how many openings, how many verbs. If the answer is one, one and two, the pool is dull no matter how lawful each face is.

### 17. Belief and custom, entered from inside

Wolfe's method, in his own words, is to write a pagan world "as the pagans themselves wrote about it", without a rationalist aside. An omen is entered as an omen. A rite is recorded exactly as it is done. A rite still kept in a ruined place is entered as kept, and the ruin is left to say what the entry does not.

ADDENDUM 15 has just opened this ground for the faith desk: the **followers** act, the god never does. The move above is precisely how to use that licence without ever predicating anything of a deity.

> Those who keep the feast at {settlement} are slow to answer a provocation, and the town has learned to send them first to a quarrel.

Subject: the people. Content: the recorded temper. No god, no cosmology, no count, no date. Immersive, and entirely inside the floors.

### 18. Quoted speech expresses the speaker

Twelve sources on Wolfe: the butler must not sound like the footman, and the reader should know the hand without a tag. Le Guin's counterpart is that dialect is **named and never spelled** ("she speaks with the fen vowels"), speech is brief, and it carries no knowing wisecrack. For the Herald's short pools, Wolfe's harder rule applies: a quoted exchange takes the form of an examination or a complaint, never two people explaining the town to each other.

## PART II — THE TELLS, STATED AS TASTE

None of these is a bar. This is simply what tired prose does, and you can hear all of it.

**The measured tells, from the chair's own audit of the last attempt.** The rewrite spent 168 distinct words where the shipped corpus spent 519 at the same token budget. Seventy-one per cent of units were a two-clause `and` join. The approved safe words became tics: the word *country* appeared once every twenty-one words. Every variant in one pool collapsed onto one grammar. That is what a licence test produces, and it is the thing to listen for.

**The inventory line.** A variant that states the key fact and nothing else, with no angle and no second read. "A {defwork} stands at {settlement}." It is true, it is lawful, and it is not a sentence anyone wanted.

**The doubled beat.** The second clause restating the first in other nouns. Listen for *and* followed by a synonym.

**The summarising close.** "...which leaves the town exposed." The entry telling the reader what to conclude. Le Guin's whole doctrine on this is that the record leaves the hearing to the reader.

**The slot-first opening, twelve times running.** Every face starting on `{settlement}` or `{defwork}`. Openings are free; spend them.

**The brochure adjectives.** Bustling, sleepy, quaint, thriving, imposing, humble, vibrant, rugged. Each is a verdict with no object in it. Also the stacked pair (*ancient, weathered stone*), which is two verdicts.

**The monument voice.** *Stands as a testament to*, *speaks to the enduring*, *a rich tapestry of*, *nestled in*, *whispers of a forgotten age*. These are travel-brochure sentences wearing a cloak.

**The balanced abstraction.** "Not merely a wall, but a statement of intent." The antithesis reflex, once per page, is a pleasure; four times is a tic.

**The tricolon of nouns that name nothing.** "Its safety lies in its distance, its neighbours, and its unimportance" is good because all three are concrete facts about this town. "Its strength lies in tradition, resilience and community" is the same shape with the contents removed.

**The lecture.** Any sentence explaining to the reader how towns work. Le Guin: art is not explanation.

**Ornament that stops the reader to be admired.** Her exact complaint about ornate prose is that it is always stopping to be admired. If a clause is pleased with itself, it goes.

**Archaism as costume.** No *mayhap*, *thee*, *ye olde*, no sentence-initial *And* as a mannerism. The register ranges from commonplace to stately **by subject**, never by mood. The failure mode is named precisely by a reader who tried to imitate Wolfe and failed: his prose had become bland modern writing with a superficial old-fashioned sound.

**The em dash and the digit.** Neither belongs in the register. Write the number in words or, better, do not write the number.

## PART III — TWELVE EVOCATIONS OF ONE BARE FACT

The chair's arithmetic problem was this: a pool's licensed claim set is often ONE fact, and the corpus asks twelve renderings of it. Twelve renderings of a bare fact is impossible. **Twelve evocations of a place where that fact holds is easy.** The fact below is `WALLED-STRAINED`: the town has a wall, and one purse over its keeping and the muster's wages is short. The wall stands through it. That is all the record says.

1. The {defwork} at {settlement} stands, and the purse that keeps it has been short so long the town budgets around the shortfall.
2. Nobody at {settlement} would call the {defwork} neglected. They would call it a thing that gets done last.
3. The {defwork} stands; the keeping of it is a subject people at {settlement} change.
4. Ask at {settlement} what the {defwork} costs to keep and you will get a long answer, and then a longer one about the muster's wage.
5. At {settlement} the {defwork} is the one thing the town pays for after everything else, and it is still standing, which is either luck or the nature of walls.
6. The {defwork} at {settlement} is older than the arrangement that fails to pay for it.
7. In {settlement}'s own reckoning the {defwork}'s keeping and the muster's wage sit under one heading, and the heading is short.
8. {settlement} keeps its {defwork} the way a family keeps a good coat it cannot afford to clean.
9. The {defwork} stands. What it costs to keep standing is not a thing the town says out loud.
10. At {settlement} the shortfall falls on the {defwork}'s keeping and on the muster's wages alike, and the wall, being a wall, does not notice.
11. The {defwork} at {settlement} is sound, the purse behind it is not, and the town has made its peace with the arrangement.
12. Whoever at {settlement} is owed for the {defwork}'s keeping has been owed a while, and says so.

Twelve. Zero invented numbers, dates or history. Zero named characters. The wall never decays and is never paid while the muster starves. Eleven distinct grammars. Four different people appear and not one of them has a name. Every one of them is a hook a GM can pick up and run with, which is the entire point of the exercise.
