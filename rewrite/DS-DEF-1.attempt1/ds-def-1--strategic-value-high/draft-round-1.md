1. `[ledger]` `[plain]` Something at {settlement} is worth taking, and the whole of the defensive problem sits in that. The town must be worth more to keep than it is to seize.
   - `[face]` The defensive problem at {settlement} is one problem and no more. The town holds a thing worth taking, and the keeping of it must come dearer than the seizing of it would be worth.
   - `[face]` A prize sits at {settlement}, and the prize is the whole of the town's defensive problem. What the town holds must be worth more in the holding than it would be in the taking.

   - `[face]` What the place is worth is no secret in the town, and none beyond it either. That worth is in view in much of what the town settles.
   - `[face]` Living here means knowing what the place is worth, and knowing that the knowledge is not the town's alone. That worth sits under a great many of the town's decisions.
   - `[face]` Nothing about what the place is worth is lost on the town, nor is the notice it draws. The town carries that knowledge into most of what it undertakes.
3. `[visitor]` `[plain]` A day at {settlement} is enough for a stranger to see why anybody would want the place, and a little longer is enough to see why the town is so careful about who it offends.
   - `[face]` A stranger needs a day at {settlement} to understand why the place is wanted, and not much longer to understand why the town chooses its quarrels with such care.
   - `[face]` Inside a day a stranger sees what draws others to {settlement}. Not long after, the same stranger sees why the town is sparing with its offence.
   - `[face]` Why anybody would want {settlement} is plain to a stranger by the end of a day. Shortly after, so is the town's care over whom it offends.

## B. Refusals

- **R1 · ONE FACT IS LICENSED AND EVERY VARIANT CARRIES THREE.** The card's `may claim` line licenses exactly one standing fact, and its `may NOT` line bars "a second fact". All three variants carry a licensed first claim and two unlicensed further claims. Under the rewrite rule "every claim in every face is licensed by the LICENCE CARD and by nothing else", **all three variants and all twelve wordings are unlawful and are banked as a refusal set** (§21.2: banked with its faces, never trimmed). They are written out in full because §22 (a) and (e) forbid removing a claim to buy lawfulness, and because the first claim of each is genuinely licensed. The cure is not a writer's: either the card must license the second-order claims (a second read, a `NARROWS` declaration, or a chair ruling that the pool's predicate carries the town's posture toward its own worth), or the chair must rule the second claims out and accept the claim-drop as a declared one-time content change. **This packet does not assert the pool is lawful.**
- **R2 · The card contradicts itself on the standpoint.** `angle:` names `ledger street visitor`; `may NOT:` bars "a standpoint". Two of the three variants ARE standpoints by their tags, which are not mine to touch. Written under the `angle` clause. This is the same conflict the `readiness STRONG` packet raised as its R2; one chair ruling settles both. If `may NOT` governs, variants 2 and 3 cannot be made lawful in any wording and the pool collapses to `[ledger]` alone, which would be a trim by another route.
- **R3 · The card's printed predicate is narrower than the code, and a writer told only the card would write a false face.** The card prints `predicate: … === Coastal`. `TERRAIN_PRIZE_OF` (`defenseStateProse.js:655-659`) maps **Coastal AND Mountain** to this pool. A face naming the sea, the harbour, the anchorage or the sea road would be licensed on a Coastal town and FALSE on a Mountain one; a face naming the pass or the height would be false on a Coastal one. **No wording in this packet names any terrain feature**, and none may. The card printing one key of a two-key map is a card finding for the chair, not a writer's cure. (The same line also prints the reads path's tail as the claim's subject, `` that `js)` (=== Coastal) holds ``, which is a second, cosmetic card fault.)
- **R4 · A CAUSE, in all three variants.** Variant 1's "the whole of the defensive problem sits in that", variant 2's "runs underneath a great deal of what the town decides" and variant 3's second clause all assert that the town's conduct FOLLOWS FROM the worth of its site. The card bars a cause outright, and the block's own PROVENANCE fence says terrain and strategic value "never license a causal clause". All three are the parents' claims; removing them is trimming (§22 (e): refinement is a one-for-one substitution, never a removal). Written, flagged, not cured.
- **R5 · A COUNT.** Variant 3's duration ("a day", and the shorter span after it) is a measure the card bars and no field holds. Kept because it is the parent's and because varying it across the four wordings would break claim-equality (arm A6).
- **R6 · FEELING, and it is variant 2's whole content.** Variant 2 asserts nothing but the town's awareness and the awareness of unnamed others. The FEELING non-move (MOVE-GRAMMAR §1.3) refuses it estate-wide, and unlike variants 1 and 3 there is no residue that stands without it. **Variant 2 cannot be made lawful in any wording while keeping its claims.** It is written and banked.
- **R7 · The generalisation.** Variant 1's closing proposition is a principle about what any prize town must be, not a fact about this one. R-DA-12's generalisation test is engaged and its walker baseline has not run. The claim is the parent's and is kept in all four wordings.
