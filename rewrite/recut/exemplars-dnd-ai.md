# THE EXEMPLAR PACK — what to aim at

*Re-cut from `prose-research/sweep/section-dnd.md` (1212 claims on the prose of official D&D content) and `section-ai.md` (1518 claims on where generated prose fails), under ADDENDUM 14: the author research is **guideposts, not laws**.*

**Read this before you write. Nothing in it is a bar.** No clause of this pack is ever a refuter's finding; no sentence fails because it missed a move here. The only hard refusals live in the four floors (contradiction, invented history/date/number, product scope + the deity doctrine, the engine's own model). Everything below is taste — what the best writers in this trade actually do, and what tired prose does instead. Where a source is copyrighted its move is **paraphrased** rather than reproduced; names attribute the move, not the wording.

---

## §0. The reader, and the job

You are writing **one fantasy town**. You hold a handful of recorded facts — a wall, a muster, a market day, a shortfall, a country that is quiet or is not. You have **a sentence, or three**. The person reading is a game master at eleven at night, looking for something to run on Thursday.

Two consequences run through every move in this pack.

**First: your reader is not an auditor.** The dossier is a set of plot hooks and descriptions the author reads to be immersed and changes at will. A GM who reads "the gate stands open more often than not" does not go looking for the field that licenses it; they think *who profits from that*, and the town is suddenly worth a session. That is the whole product. A sentence that is unimpeachable and gives them nothing to do has failed at the only job it had.

**Second: the record is the floor, not the ceiling.** The facts you hold are the ground you stand on. What you build on that ground — the noticing, the grievance, the habit, the thing nobody mentions — is yours, and silence in the record is permission. Say nothing the record denies. Then say something.

The trade's own standard for this, from Hartlage's cutting test (paraphrased): **cut the line a typical GM would have imagined anyway; keep the line only the record could have supplied.** That test is the sharpest instrument in either source file, and it cuts both ways — it kills the generic sentence *and* it demands that you use what you alone hold.

## §1. Eleven moves that make a place inhabited

### 1. The telling particular

One exact thing beats three adjectives. Lynch's position (paraphrased): one well-written specific sentence outruns a page of detail, and *detail* is not the same as *specificity* — piled detail is trivia, a specific is a handle. Gus L's practice is the same: not "an evil idol" but an idol of sharp-edged obsidian. The evaluative word tells the reader what to think; the exact noun lets them see it and decide.

> Weak: *the town's defences are in poor repair.*
> Aim at: *the gate at {settlement} closes, but it takes two to swing it.*

The second sentence carries the same fact and hands the GM a scene.

### 2. The detail that implies a life

The corpus's exemplar is Hommlet: a catalogue of mundane households whose small commercial facts — a price a shade above standard, what the inn actually serves, a merchant who moves broken nags — add up to a place that was living before anyone arrived. Prince's word for the effect (paraphrased) is *a world in flux*. One fact per household, and the fact implies a story the entry never tells.

> *Nobody at {settlement} buys rope from the second stall; everyone knows why, and no one will say it at the first.*

That is one recorded market, two stalls' worth of invention, and an evening's play.

### 3. Show the place through the person who would notice

The licensed magazine's thirty questions for a city put *what does it smell like* at twenty-four, and it is the most useful question on the list, because a smell is always somebody's problem. A place is most itself in what a particular person would notice, avoid, or refuse to walk past.

> *Carters coming in from the east take the long way round the tanners' row, and grumble about it, and take it anyway.*

Note what this does: it gives the town a geography, a trade, a grievance and a habit, and every one of them is atmosphere. An **unnamed** person may act, keep a key, be avoided, be resented (floor 3 bars only a *named* character's fate). Use them. The reeve who is slow with the books, the gate-ward who knows everyone's face, the widow who will not sell — these are the best carriers the register has.

### 4. The complaint as the carrier

The single most efficient way to state a civic fact is to state who is annoyed by it. A complaint carries the fact, the stake, and a voice at once, and it never sounds like a receipt.

> Receipt: *the town's upkeep for its defences is constrained.*
> Complaint: *At {settlement} the keeping of the {defwork} and the muster's wages come off one purse, and every year somebody argues about which gets paid first.*

Same fact. The second is a faction seed.

### 5. Make absence vivid

An honest absence is a hook; a manufactured sentence is filler. The trade's own rule (the DUNGEON staff, paraphrased) is that an area with nothing under any heading is not an area at all — and Alexander's counterpart is that even an empty room carries one flavourful thing. Do not write around a gap; write **the shape of the gap**.

> *No one at {settlement} keeps a night watch, and no one has needed to explain why for a long time.*
> *The bell that would be rung is still there.*

The absence is the fact. Something that *isn't* is a stronger hook than most things that are, because it implies a decision somebody made.

### 6. The second clause earns its place

A sentence earns a second clause by adding a **fact**, a **consequence** or a **voice** — never by adding a meaning. This is the single highest-value habit in the pack, because the failure it replaces (the gloss tail, §4) is the most common failure in generated prose.

> Earned (consequence): *The {defwork} at {settlement} stands through the shortfall, and the shortfall sits on the muster's wages just the same.*
> Earned (voice): *The country outside is quiet, and the older folk at {settlement} do not trust quiet.*
> Unearned: *The country outside is quiet, further reinforcing the settlement's position as a stable regional centre.*

The third one added nothing; it only told the reader how to feel about the first half.

### 7. The exact verb

Radiating Gnome's move (paraphrased): a causeway *climbs*; it does not *rise*. The verb is where the specificity hides once the adjectives are gone. Prefer the motion a thing actually makes.

> *The road out of {settlement} climbs before it forks.* — not *there is a road that leads out and splits.*

Plain verbs — is, has, holds, keeps, owes, pays, sends, stands — are welcome and always were; what to avoid is the verb chosen because it sounded weightier than *is* (§4).

### 8. The load first, the flavour after

The publisher's own field observation (Noonan and Decker, paraphrased): a GM reading aloud gets about two sentences before the table's attention goes, and the load-bearing fact had better be in them. The dossier is read, not spoken, but the shape transfers: **the fact that matters is never mid-ornament.** Open on the thing; earn the second sentence.

### 9. Leave one thing standing open

Skilled human fiction defers closure; generated prose ties every thread. The measured shape of the second is a tidy resolution and, often, a moral. A town entry that resolves itself is a town with nothing to run.

> Closed: *the dispute over the mill was settled and the two families work together now.*
> Open: *The mill's ownership has been argued twice and settled neither time.*

At least one civic matter in any town should be **standing**, not concluded — a debt unpaid, a boundary unsettled, a petition unanswered, an argument about which bill gets paid first.

### 10. Name the relation, or leave it alone

The record register's sharpest measured failure is a relation *gestured at* rather than stated: two things placed side by side so the reader infers a link no record holds. If you know the relation, name it. If you don't, put the two facts in separate sentences and let the GM do the linking — that's their job and their pleasure.

> Gestured: *the market and the garrison are closely connected in the life of the town.*
> Named: *The muster drills on market day, which the stallholders have never forgiven.*
> Honest: *Market day is the seventh. The muster drills when it drills.*

### 11. Only what the record could supply

Back to Hartlage's test, because it is the one to keep in your head while drafting. Every sentence should be one the GM could not have written for themselves from the town's name and tier. A wall that is a wall, a market that sells things, a watch that watches — those are what the reader already imagined. The bill that gets argued over, the stall nobody buys from, the bell still hanging: those are yours to give.

## §2. Voice, rhythm, and the ear

### 12. Read it aloud

Merwin's rule, and the most testable thing in either file (paraphrased): read the line aloud before you keep it. The ear catches what the page hides — the tongue-twister, the accidental alliteration, the clause that has to be re-read. The split the sources record is about **intent**: accidental sound is a fault, chosen sound is a device. Radiating Gnome keeps a *craggy causeway* on purpose. Keep yours on purpose too.

### 13. Length follows load

Generated prose is metronomic: sentence after sentence in a narrow band, because nothing in the objective rewards variation. Human prose in this register varies because the **load** varies. One fact, short sentence. Three holdings, long sentence. A quiet year, one line.

> *The wall stands. What it costs to keep standing is argued over every year, in the same words, by the same two offices, and the argument is older than either man holding those offices now.*
> *The gate is new.*

Never alternate long-short-long by rota either — a rota is the same failure wearing a different coat.

### 14. Authority without ornament

The corpus is unanimous that the Gygaxian *authority* was real — a record that treats its subject as seriously as a scholar would — and divided on whether the diction was worth its cost. Keep the authority. Leave the platinum word where a copper one serves, the habitual passive, the digression, the sermon. Winter's line from inside TSR (paraphrased): direct and clear, no flowery phrase, no clever wordplay. Weight comes from exactness, never from Latin.

### 15. Fifty in reserve

Mohan on Greenwood (paraphrased): for every word given, the reader feels fifty held back. That is the effect you are aiming at, and it is produced by *knowing more than you write* — write the one fact that implies the ten you didn't, and resist explaining it. The corollary is his second remark: of every ten facts given, three or four could have been kept.

### 16. The thread

Adjacent sentences should connect. In the strongest human writing each sentence depends on the one before it — you could not swap them without loss. In the weakest generated writing the order is arbitrary, because no sentence was caused by its predecessor.

> Threaded: *The country outside is quiet. That quiet is why the argument about the wall's keeping can be had out loud.*
> Swappable: *The country outside is quiet. The wall's keeping and the muster's wages come off one purse.*

Both are lawful. The first is a paragraph; the second is two rows of a table with a full stop each.

## §3. The pool is the unit

### 17. Twelve angles, not twelve paraphrases

This is the arithmetic that broke the previous round, and the re-cut fixes it. **Twelve renderings of one bare fact is impossible; twelve evocations of a place where that fact holds is easy.** When you are writing a pool, you are not restating the read a dozen ways. You are standing in a dozen different places in the same town and saying what you see from each: the elder's memory of it, the clerk's way of stating it, a stranger's first read of it from the road, the street's complaint about it, the thing as a matter still standing open, the counter-pressure against it.

### 18. One name per thing, one descriptor per pool

A thing keeps its one name throughout — the mill is the mill, never *the grain works*, never *the old structure*. Synonym-hunting is what tired prose does when it is afraid of repeating itself; a record is not afraid. But a **descriptive** word is a different matter: once you have spent *crowded* in a pool, it is spent. The measured failure here is going back to the same well and never noticing. Notice.

## §4. What tired prose does

*These are tastes, not bars. Nobody fails for one. But every one of them is a place where the sentence stopped being written by a person who knew the town.*

**The gloss tail.** A fact, then a participle explaining its importance. Wikipedia's own field guide illustrates it with a real article revision: a census figure followed by "further enhancing its significance as a dynamic hub" (Wikipedia, *Signs of AI writing*, on a June 2023 revision of the Douera article). Nothing *further enhances* anything. The fix is §1.6: make the second clause carry a fact.

**The evaluator.** *Nestled, vibrant, bustling, charming, breathtaking, thriving, rich heritage.* These are what a model writes when it has no particulars; they are also what a travel brochure writes when it wants your money. A market is what it sells and on which day.

**The vague authority.** *It is said, some say, experts hold, observers note.* If you know who says it, say who. If you don't, state the thing plainly or leave it out. (An in-world hedge — *by report*, *the ledger claims* — is a different instrument and is fine where the record is genuinely reporting a claim.)

**Not X but Y.** *Not a fortress but a promise.* Every source that still tracks lexical tells agrees this one has not drifted. A record has no misconception to correct, because it hasn't told the reader anything yet.

**The rule of three.** Three nouns, three clauses, three abstractions, always three. Three is a human device; the tell is a triad *regardless of the occasion*, and its documented purpose is to make a thin thought look complete. Let the list run to the true count: two guilds, five wards, one ferry.

**The copula dodge.** *Serves as, stands as, marks, boasts, features, represents, functions as, holds the distinction of being.* All of them are *is* in a costume. *Is* is the register's own verb; the publisher's own place prose runs it freely.

**The participial bookend.** Sentences that open on *-ing* clauses or close on them. One is fine. Three in a paragraph is a rhythm nobody chose.

**The piled metaphor.** Comparisons stacked at a sentence's end; the abstract welded to the concrete (*a studio that smelled of turpentine and something abstract*); scenery given intent (*dawn unfurls*, *the road remembers*). In this register the comparison that works is a measurement: the wall is the height of two men; the bell is heard at the far ferry.

**The mood vocabulary.** *Ache, hollow, linger, fragile, fractured, ember, veil, threadbare* — and the spectral set, *echo, whisper, hum, quiet, liminal, ghost.* Tired prose reaches for these because they correlate with literary writing, and attaches them to nothing.

**The tidy close.** The last sentence that sums up, moralises, or hands the reader the point. An entry ends on a standing condition. If your final clause could be moved to the top as a thesis, it is a summary, not an ending.

**The comfort tilt.** Conflict smoothed, harm reconciled, everyone learning to live together. Record the harm and its cost, and let it stand unreconciled. A town is allowed to have fallen and stayed fallen.

**The manufactured emphasis.** The one-word fragment for effect. The kicker. The bulleted list where a sentence belongs. The cliffhanger close.

**The answer-shaped sentence.** A count that counts nothing, a citation to a record no one made, a relation that names no parties. It has the shape of a fact and no fact in it. This is also the one place taste and floor 2 point the same way: *forty on the roll* and *three winters ago* are inventions of a number and a date; *more of them than the town can feed* is a hook.

**Sameness.** Default names, the same opening noun twice in one town, the lighthouse everyone's generator builds. If two pools in one dossier open the same way, one of them was written on autopilot.

## §5. A worked pool

One town. One recorded situation: it has a built defensive work; the upkeep gate reads short; the shortfall sits on the work's keeping and the muster's wages alike, from one purse; the work stands through it. Six angles, one fact:

- *At {settlement} the keeping of the {defwork} and the muster's wages come off one purse, and the purse is short.*
- *The {defwork} at {settlement} stands as it was built. What is short is the coin behind it, and that does not show from the road.*
- *Older folk at {settlement} will tell you the wall's keeping and the soldiers' pay were always one argument; they only differ on whether it used to be quieter.*
- *The bill for the {defwork}'s keeping stands open at {settlement}, and so do the muster's wages.*
- *Whatever is owed on the {defwork} and on the muster's wages is owed out of the same purse, and the question of which gets paid first has never once been settled in writing.*
- *A stranger walking up to {settlement} sees a wall that is doing its job. The people inside it are having a different conversation.*

Six sentences, one fact, six things a GM could run. None of them adds a number, a date, or a claim the record denies. That is the target.

## §6. Before you hand it over

Not a gate. Six questions worth asking of a pool while it is still yours to change:

1. **Could the GM have written this from the town's name alone?** If yes, it is the line to cut.
2. **Does every second clause add a fact, a consequence or a voice** — or does one of them explain the first half's importance?
3. **Is one thing in this town still standing open**, or has everything been settled?
4. **Does a person appear anywhere** — noticing, avoiding, complaining, refusing?
5. **Read it aloud.** Does any line make you slow down for a reason you did not choose?
6. **Read the pool whole.** Do the variants stand in different places in the town, or do they restate one sentence twelve times in twelve grammars?

If the answers are good, the sentence is doing the only job it has: making a game master want to run this town on Thursday.
