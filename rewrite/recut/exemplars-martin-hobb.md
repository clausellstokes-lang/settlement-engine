# EXEMPLAR PACK — MARTIN AND HOBB, RE-CUT AS GUIDEPOSTS

**Read this before you write. Aim at it. Nothing in this file is a rule.**

Under ADDENDUM 14 (owner, 2026-09-12: *"use all of the references to best authors as guiding posts not as laws"*) the two exhaustive sweeps `prose-research/sweep/section-martin.md` and `section-hobb.md` stop being constraints. They were converted once into bars a refuter could cite; that was the wrong reading of what the research found, and it produced the flat corpus the chair measured (168 distinct words against the shipped 519 at an equal budget; 71 % of units a two-clause `and` join). **No clause of this file is ever a finding.** A refuter may not cite it. The only hard refusals are the four floors, restated at the end. Everything between here and there is taste — moves that make a place feel inhabited, offered so you have somewhere to aim.

Where a move comes from a copyrighted text, the move is paraphrased and the illustration is our own. Quotations are from the critics, translators and interviews in the sweep, attributed, kept short.

---

## 0. WHAT YOU ARE ACTUALLY MAKING

One fantasy town. A handful of recorded facts. A sentence, or three. A game master with a session tonight reads it, and either something in it catches or it does not.

That is the whole brief, and it changes what "good" means. You are not writing a receipt the reader audits. You are writing a **plot hook and a description an author reads to be immersed, and changes as they see fit**. The reader is a collaborator with a pen, not an inspector. A sentence they can pick up and run with beats a sentence they can verify.

So the test of a face is not *can I prove this?* It is: **does a game master, reading this aloud at a table, get a place, and something to do in it?**

---

## A. THE TELLING PARTICULAR

The single highest-yield move in both authors. A place is not conveyed by a category; it is conveyed by one object with a taste, a weight, or an owner.

Hobb's line to a craft blog is the whole lesson in eleven words: **"The car isn't red, it's candy apple red with metal flake."** (Hobb, craft post). Her critics read the same thing back as a virtue — **"the objects of daily life are concrete and treasured"** (Teitelbaum). Mendelsohn, on Martin, says the period nouns — destriers, palfreys, vair, samite — **"give concrete reality"** to a world, and cannot be filmed.

**Name the object, never the class.** Beeswax, not lighting. Clean straw, not bedding. Barley bread, not provisions. A brazier, not defensive infrastructure.

> Tired: *The settlement maintains adequate defensive works and a functioning watch presence.*
> Aimed at: *The gate-bar is kept oiled. The brazier beside it burns whatever the ward can spare, and lately that is sea-coal, which everyone at the gate complains of.*

Three things happened in that second version and none of them was a new fact: an object, a smell, and a grievance. That is the density the sweep is pointing at.

**Every particular carries a civic fact.** Martin's food, cloth and blazon are defended by him as never gratuitous, and faulted by his critics as cost; the sweep's reconciliation is the one we want — the catalogue stays, but each item tells the reader something about rank, wealth, season or belief. A beeswax candle in a poor ward is a sentence about who paid for it.

**Describe a thing when it is in dispute or in use, not because it exists.** Page, on Hobb: **"She only delivers description when it's part of a conflict"**. Describe the mill because two wards claim it. Describe the gate because it is shut earlier than it used to be shut.

**Contrast is a second job for free.** One of Martin's readers notes that he cuts long description into short sentences and works by contrast, so one description does several tasks at once.

> *The gate is new. The wall it hangs in is not.*

Seven words, two facts, and a question a GM can ask at the table: who paid for the gate, and why only the gate?

## B. THE THREE DOORS: NOTICE, AVOID, COMPLAIN

If you do not know how to get inside a place, go in through a person who is not named. ADDENDUM 14 relaxed the person bar exactly here: **an unnamed person may appear, act, keep a key, be avoided, be resented.** A reeve who is slow to open the books is now a licensed plot hook, and it is a better sentence than any statement of the same fact.

**What someone would NOTICE.** The eye of a stranger, an incomer, a carter. This is Martin's visitor stance stripped of its costume: the visitor sees, and never decides.
> *A stranger notices the wall first and the watch-house second, which is the wrong order, and the town knows it.*

**What someone would AVOID.** Avoidance is the cheapest uncanny in the book. Martin's supernatural is a custom, a stone, a skeleton in the church, never an explanation — his critics call it deftly muted, and Hobb's rule is that the fantastic is familiar, scarce, and paid for.
> *The flat stone at the ford is stepped around, not over. Nobody will say why, and the record does not either.*

**What someone would COMPLAIN of.** Hobb's people quarrel over small concrete things and their standing shows in the quarrel. A grievance is a hook with a handle on it.
> *The argument at the moot is over whose straw goes to the gate-house. Everyone there knows it is not about straw.*

Those three doors will carry almost any block you are given. When you cannot find an angle, ask which of the three the record's fact would show up in.

## C. ABSENCE, AND THE THING YOU DO NOT CLOSE

Hobb's most distinctive sentence-level habit, and the one Oliver's article spends the most pages on, is **negative narration**: sentences that open by saying what is *not*. Oliver: the prose **"constantly focuses on what is not happening or what is not present"**, and the effect is **"training the reader to see what is not there"**. Martin's reconstruction lands in the same place from the other side — where the record is silent, say what did not happen; it carries more dread than an adjective.

This matters twice over for us. It is a beautiful move, and it is **free under the floors**: an absence invents no number and no date.

> *No boat crosses after dark. There is no rule about it.*
> *The bell is not rung for weddings here.*
> *Nothing has come out of the wood this side of the gate.*

And then — **do not close it**. Hobb is explicit that the nebulousness is engineered and stays put; the reader connects the dots; details return with relevance and are not explained on arrival. For a dossier read by a GM this is not a stylistic preference, it is the product: an open question is the hook. A face that raises a tension and then resolves it in its own last clause has handed the author nothing.

> Tired: *The gate is shut early these days, though the town remains secure.*
> Aimed at: *The gate is shut early these days. No one has given a reason that the ward accepts.*

## D. HOW A SENTENCE EARNS ITS SECOND CLAUSE

Our measured failure is the two-clause `and` join at 71 %. The cure is not fewer second clauses; it is second clauses that are **earned**. Four earners, all attested:

**1. The cost clause.** Hobb's rhythm of qualification — Page's phrase is **"Really, Hobb's buts are fractal."** Nothing arrives without its remainder. One qualification, never a chain.
> *The wall stands, and the keeping of it is short.*
> *The stores are held back, at the price of the ward that would have eaten them.*

**2. The consequence clause — who it lands on.** This is Hobb's strongest civic move and the sweep gives it fourteen sources: consequence lands on a **household**, and the domestic and the political are the same duty seen twice. A levy is a pair of hands missing from the boats.
> *A household that sends a son to the muster is a household short a hand at the nets.*

**3. The contrast clause.** The old wall and the new gate; the lord's mill and the freeholders'.

**4. The noticing clause.** The fact, then who would remark on it.
> *The watch keeps the gate. The carters keep their own count of when it is kept.*

And three clauses that never earn their place: the **restatement** (the summarising beat — a clause that says the first clause again in other words), the **reassurance** (*but the town endures*), and the **abstraction pair** (*both practical and symbolic*). These three are the bulk of what we have been shipping.

## E. TWO REGISTERS IN ONE BREATH, AND THE CONSEQUENCE CHAIN

Martin's stated method, in his own words across several interviews, is the one to steal wholesale. There is **"room for both plagues and pageantry"**; **"you have to root it in reality"** first, then play, and never make it smaller.

For us: **hold the fair and the fever in the same sentence.** The painted gate and the midden behind it. The feast and the tithe that paid for it.

> *The device over the gate is repainted every spring. The hinges are not.*

Then trace the consequence one step further than feels necessary. A river that floods has a ferry; a ferry has a toll; a toll has a dispute. Hobb says the same thing as advice — **"Make your economy and geography make sense"** — and shows institutions only through their transactions: a negotiation, a grant, a message sent. Her line via Bock is a plot hook in itself: **"if you do not exercise your power, it goes away"**.

> *The toll at the ford is taken in kind. What is taken in kind is weighed by whoever holds the scales, and the scales are the miller's.*

That is three clauses, no invented number, and an entire evening of play.

## F. WORDS

**The plain stock.** Both authors are measured as plain: Martin's reading ease is conversational, his sentences rarely past thirty words. Prefer hall, ward, hearth, ford, oath, bread, toll to their Latinate equivalents. *Population* is folk or households; *infrastructure* is roads, wells and mills.

**Three classes of old word, and only one of them is a tell.** The sweep's own reconciliation: (1) native-root words in daily use — unrestricted; (2) **real dated words that are the names of things** — reeve, tithe-barn, moot, toll-bar — unrestricted, because the name of an institution is not a flavour word, and these are what carry antiquity cumulatively; (3) **dated forms of manner** — mayhaps, oft, elsewise, whilst, must needs. Class three is the costume, and Gelzer-Govatos's verdict on it is the one to keep in your ear: **"In reality they just sound ridiculous."**

**Compounds that decode themselves.** Two plain roots: watch-fire, gate-bar, salt-road, tithe-barn, oath-day. Each should decode from its parts. Use these for ordinary things and habits; the institutions themselves already have their recorded names, and those names are the ones to use every time.

**No word the reader could date to their own century.** The modern leak is the fault that no critic in the entire sweep defends. *Infrastructure, resource, community resilience, logistics, oversight* — all of them put the reader in an office.

**Repeat one plain noun through a short passage, never an adjective.** The verified case of cohesion-by-repetition is a noun (a stigma word repeated across paragraphs); the disputed case is a mood adjective. Repeat *the ford*; do not repeat *quiet*.

**A formula recurs only if the town owns it.** Martin's fixed epithets are a real device and a real fault: his editor tried to cut a phrase from the fifth book and he kept it. The safe form is a saying the settlement itself repeats, built from a thing the settlement has — its river, its beast, its trade. Everything else is said once.

## G. RHYTHM THAT SURVIVES BEING READ ALOUD

A GM reads this at a table, cold, in front of four people. That is the only rhythm test that matters, and both authors pass it in the same way.

**Short, additive, one fact per sentence.** Hobb's translator has to *join* her English sentences to make idiomatic French — a direct rendering would be choppy. Subordination is the translator's business, not the record's. Martin's reader calls the effect **"a steady flow of low-density sentences"** and **"It's shotgun writing"** (Germani) — no sentence is a monument; the passage moves.

**Land the period on the strong noun.** Huffman's note on Martin: he **"knows just where to drop his periods"**. End on the concrete word, not on a qualifier.
> Weak: *The gate is shut early these days, for reasons that remain unclear.*
> Strong: *The gate is shut early. Nobody at the moot will give a reason.*

**Flow between sentences, not the weight of any one.** Germani's own considered verdict on Martin, and the best single sentence in the sweep for our purposes: **"Flow between sentences is the litmus test"**.

**Three is the ceiling for a list, and a roll of names is the register's known failure.** The chronicle reviewers are unanimous on this one — a reader's **"eyes glazed over"** at the lists of lords and knights (Jeroen). A force is hearths and one office, never a roster.

**No paired near-synonyms.** *Low, quiet* is one word said twice. Pick the better one.

## H. WITNESS, DOUBT, AND THE SHORT FAWNING ENTRY

This is the chronicle register's own machinery, and it is the most under-used source of hooks we have.

**Attribute to a class of witness, never to acquaintance.** Hobb's record cites testimony and public information and never the writer's relation to the subject. *The millers say. The roll records. As the carters have it.*

**Mark the distance of the report.** The further a thing is from the recorder, the more hedged it is. A near fact stands bare; a far one arrives by somebody's account.

**Give both versions and do not adjudicate.** Martin invented contradictory sources so he could tell one event several ways, because **"history is uncertain"** in his own words, and because he did not have to make up his mind. Two versions of one fact, side by side, is a session premise.
> *The ward says the toll was always taken in kind. The toll-book says otherwise, and the toll-book is the older hand.*

**Doubt it in the same sentence you cite it, and let nothing later soften the doubt.**

**The courtier's caution.** The chronicle's maester writes a short, very fawning entry on the reigning power and a long, frank one on the fallen. What a record omits is the reader's strongest clue. A conspicuously brief, conspicuously polite entry about whoever currently holds the town is a hook that needs no invention at all.

**And the archivist does not apologise for being a record.** Hobb's dryness is by design — her French translator's description is the target: **"pas de jugement chez elle, uniquement un exposé des faits"** (Mousnier-Lompré). The warmth comes from the particular, never from loosening the voice.

## I. WHAT TIRED PROSE DOES (taste, not a bar)

None of these is refusable. They are simply what our corpus does when nobody is aiming.

- **It reaches for the two-clause `and` join by default.** Seventy-one per cent of our units. Vary the shape before you vary the words.
- **It restates.** The second clause says the first clause again, in slightly grander terms. This is the doubled beat and it is the single commonest tell.
- **It reassures.** *…though the town remains resilient.* A closed tension is a dead hook.
- **It writes the inventory line.** The key fact stated flat, no angle, no second read, no one in it.
- **It says a thing "stands as a testament to", "speaks to", "serves as a reminder of".** Nothing in a town does any of these.
- **It pairs abstractions for cadence.** *Both practical and symbolic. Not only defensive but social.*
- **It turns a safe word into a tic.** Our approved vocabulary produced `country` once every twenty-one words. A word you reach for three times in a block has stopped being a choice.
- **It stacks adjectives where a noun would do.** *A large, well-maintained defensive structure* is a wall, and a worse wall.
- **It hedges without admitting anything.** *Something of a. A certain. Not insignificant.*
- **It describes categories.** Lighting, provisions, defensive posture, community. A GM cannot run a category.
- **It is grand about small things and small about grand ones.** Hobb's line is the corrective: **"silly is more dangerous than impossible"**.

## J. THE FOUR FENCES, AND THE ONE CONVERSION WORTH LEARNING

You have the whole field. There are four fences, and they are the only ones:

1. Do not contradict the town's own record, or another surface on the same page.
2. Do not invent a HISTORY, a DATE or a NUMBER. (A seed is a starting world forever. Atmosphere is free; a countable or datable fact is not.)
3. Product scope and the deity doctrine: no NAMED character's fate; a deity is a name with no predicate, though its followers may be shown behaving.
4. Do not deny what the engine positively models: walls never decay, `plagued` is monsters and not disease, the shortfall lies on one purse.

Fence 2 is the one writers feel most, and it has a conversion that makes the prose **better**, not merely legal:

> **Turn the history you wanted into a standing condition.**
> Not *the mill burned three winters back* — but *the mill's upper floor is not used, and nobody will say plainly why.*
> Not *forty on the roll* — but *the muster ground is bigger than the muster.*
> Not *built in the old lord's time* — but *the gate is older work than the wall it hangs in.*

Every one of those is a stronger hook than the fact it replaced, because a date closes a question and a standing condition opens one. Martin's own account of backstory runs the same direction: the present state first, the history after, and **"Everyone had a life before page one"** — an iceberg you can feel the weight of, and never a chronology you must recite.

## THE AIM SHEET (run down this before you call a block done)

1. Is there one object in it with a taste, a weight or an owner?
2. Does any sentence go in through what someone notices, avoids, or complains of?
3. Is there an absence anywhere, stated as a fact?
4. Did I close a question I should have left open?
5. Does every second clause cost, consequence, contrast or notice — or does it restate?
6. Does one fact land on a household?
7. Are the fair and the fever both here?
8. Read it aloud. Does a period land on a strong noun? Could a GM say it in one breath?
9. Did I say lighting, provisions, infrastructure, community, resilience, or testament?
10. Do the faces of this pool differ in **construction**, or only in vocabulary?
11. Is there anything in here a game master could start a session with tonight?
