- quote: "Entered to the town's name are a wall and a muster."
- finding: the second sentence drops both of the first sentence's head nouns (the country, creatures) and brings in

  {settlement} carries creatures. Entered to the town's name in that country are a wall and a muster."

## READ-ALOUD NOTES (information only, one per variant)

- **Variant 1.** The numbered line's inversion ("Entered to the town's name are …") is the pool's one change of
  shape and it lands well aloud; but heard as a set of four, the variant repeats "entered" on two faces and the pair
  "a wall and a muster" on three, so it sounds like one sentence being turned over rather than four wordings. The
  full stop in the numbered line is the pool's only one and it reads as a genuine two-beat.
- **Variant 2.** The four faces alternate between two openers only, the town and the creatures, and two of them land
  on a list; spoken in sequence, faces 2 and 3 are hard to tell apart until their last three words. "The town has a
  wall at {settlement}" also puts the town and its own name in two different grammatical places in one clause, which
  a reader hears for a beat as a wall the town holds somewhere else; and "the creatures" arrives definite with
  nothing before it. Neither is a finding.
- **Variant 3.** The strongest variant aloud: "stands unmet", "lies open", "stands open" and "is unsettled" are four
  real cadences rather than four synonyms, and the numbered line's close lands cleanly on the open matter. Face 3 is
  the one place the voice has to slow and back up, because the subject waits twelve words for its verb; face 2's
  "At {settlement}" start is the flattest opening in the pool.
