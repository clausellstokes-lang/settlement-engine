## I. The house guide and what it actually governs

**1. A written house style guide exists, in three versions, inside a bundle; the public copy has not moved since 2019.**
Support: 16 kept rows on 12 documents; 4 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. v1.04a itself 49; v1.08a itself 50, 241, 242; the 2021 docx 296, 297; DMs Guild tweets 52, 371; Crawford 370; EN World thread 58; Witchlight Dungeoncraft 57; Dungeoncraft v1.9c 363, 365; Critical Hits freelancer 225; Kobold Press 64; Half-Cover and Old Dungeon Master 54; Mearls 2025 (P) 240; the 2025 forum question (P) 383; POCGamer (P) 56; EN World bundle (P) 380.
Versions: v1.04a, ©2013, nine pages, Chicago 16 (49); v1.08a, ©2018 on every footer, eleven pages, Chicago 17, the January 2019 date only in the mirror's filename (50, 241); a Word revision created by Kim Mohan 2016 and last modified by Judy Bauer 2021-07-06 that ships inside the Adventurers League Dungeoncraft Pack of 2023-12-04 (296) and cites merriam-webster.com rather than the printed Collegiate (297). The docx's creator field is the only evidence that Kim Mohan made it (296); neither released PDF carries a byline and the round-9 staff finder found nothing external, so "created by Kim Mohan" is authorship by file property (feature 58). The general rules apply to every D&D product, "whether RPG books, board games, novels" (242). Organized-play authors are told they "should be familiar with the D&D Style Guide" (57) and "Adventures must use the template provided" (363). A freelancer of 2012 was told Chicago is "your main bible" (225). Community writers who said no guide existed (54) were wrong in fact and right about publicity: the guide reached the public on DMs Guild only in late 2018 (58, 52), and a 2025 asker reports the download "hasn't been updated since 2019" (P 383, the asker's own report).
Rule: The archivist's house is a Chicago house. Where this section says "house law" it means v1.08a as amended by the 2021 revision; a rule found only in v1.04a is history.

**2. The guide is a mechanics-of-style document; it says nothing about read-aloud prose or voice, and its one tone paragraph is the high-fantasy gate.**
Support: 5 kept rows on 2 documents; 1 PARTIAL row cited for their quotation only. 49, 50 (zero hits for "box" and "aloud" in either PDF); 72, 245, 246; premise check (P) 392.
True wording: "Avoid overusing sidebars." (50, the opening of a section new in 2019); "ring wraiths aren't used for gags" (72, 245; both versions); "Teamwork and friendship are a huge part" (246).
Rule: The archivist takes typography, terminology and grammar from the house and takes voice from nowhere in the house. Tone is gated once: a thing that would seem out of place in high fantasy does not belong; humour and tragedy are admitted while they do not overtake the general tone. The guide's rule that an adventure is about a small band rather than a lone knight is a rule for protagonists and is not transferred to the record (Reconciliation 9).

**3. Second person is the house instrument for gender neutrality in rules text, and only in rules text.**
Support: 2 kept rows on 2 documents; 1 PARTIAL row cited for their quotation only. 60 (v1.04a: "often achieve"), 248 (v1.08a: "prefer to ... whenever possible"); Grouling Cover on second-person immersion is PARTIAL (P 23, p. 107 not pp. 21/85).
Note: the first sweep's row 2, which stated the 2013 wording and the singular-they ban as house law, is CONTRADICTED by v1.08a.
Rule: Instruction addresses "you"; description never does. The dossier is description and has no second person anywhere. (See features 21 and 22 for the read-aloud side of the same rule, and Reconciliation 3 for the clerk's own voice.)

**4. The pronoun policy moved three times, and the current law is the 2021 revision.**
Support: 13 kept rows on 5 documents; 2 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. 2013 ban 293, 294; 2019 permission rationed (P 249) (and P 61), Crawford 370, DMs Guild 371; 2021 unrationed 299, running text 300, generic creatures 301, ambiguity 302, rare "it" 303, canon pronouns 304; devices to avoid 250; gender identity not assumed 247; dialogue exemption 252.
True wording: "Don't use 'they' or 'their' as singular pronouns" (293, v1.04a, superseded); "but don't abuse this liberty" (P 61, v1.08a); "you must ensure that the pronoun's antecedent is clear" (299, 2021); "In rules text, use 'it.'" (300); "A beholder never keeps its promises." (301); "Such exceptions should be rare." (303); "avoid 'he/she' and 's/he' altogether" (250).
Disagreement across versions: 2013 forbids singular they; 2019 allows it as a last resort; 2021 allows it freely with a clear antecedent. Not a disagreement between sources but a dated sequence; cite the year.
Rule: A person of unknown standing takes they and their with an unmistakable antecedent; where two people share a sentence the noun is repeated or the sentence goes plural. Humanoids and other sapient folk of unknown gender take they; beasts, cosmic horrors and things from other planes take it; a sapient creature takes it only as a rare deliberate cruelty. He or she is used sparingly, he/she never, alternation never. A named canonical figure keeps the pronoun canon gives. The archivist does not assume the reader's gender.

**5. Man and woman, gendered suffixes, and god: the 2019 rule and the 2021 reversal.**
Support: 3 kept rows on 2 documents; 3 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. (P 251) (2019: humans only), 298 (2021: any people), (P 253) and P 75 (suffixes), 254 (god), 252 (dialogue exemption).
True wording: "Man and woman reserved for describing humans only" (P 251, v1.08a); "we can write 'dwarf man' or 'gnome woman.'" (298, 2021); "Avoid gender-specific suffixes whenever possible." (P 75); "use 'god' whether the deity is male or female" (254).
Disagreement across versions: the 2019 restriction is reversed in 2021; the 2021 wording is current law.
Rule: Priest, actor, god, whatever the person's sex; priestess and goddess only inside a title the world itself uses. Dwarf woman and gnome man are permitted. In reported speech and first-person narrative a character may break any of this, because a character does not follow editorial style.

**6. Numerals belong to rules text; prose spells its numbers out.**
Support: 4 kept rows on 2 documents; 2 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. (P 4), 65 (v1.04a); 266, 276 (v1.08a); 267 (percent); 2021 examples still lowercase "hit points" (P 312); Chicago 9.2 named (266).
True wording: "In rules writing, we use numerals more often." (P 4); "we spell out whole numbers from zero to one hundred" (266); "The two cities are fifty miles apart." (276); "Use the word 'percent,' rather than the symbol %" (267).
Rule: The dossier is prose, not rules: every count is a word (forty households, six mills, three and a half leagues), which is also the dossier's own no-digits law arriving by a second road. Numerals live on the DM page beside game quantities, in tables, in labelled fields and in the chronicle's dated line-heads; a year in running prose is given relatively or by reign and event (Reconciliation 4; feature 54 carries the measurement of the publisher's own practice).

**7. Spelling, punctuation and heading mechanics are fixed: American, Webster's first variant, serial comma, negative contractions, run-in heads with terminal punctuation.**
Support: 11 kept rows on 2 documents; 6 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. 9 (v1.04a, six rules in one row); 259, 260, 261, 262, 263, 264, 265 (v1.08a); 255, 256, 76 (headings, new in 2019); (P 295) (Vertical Lists renamed Bulleted Lists); emphasis (P 257), sidebars (P 258), inline subhead (P 3, P 69), contractions (P 71).
True wording: "We require the serial comma." (9, 260); "we use 'toward,' not 'towards,'" (259); "non-elf, non-dwarf" (262); "Use a heading for a structural reason, not an aesthetic one" (255); "Use a period, an exclamation point, or a question mark" (256); "Use bold for such emphasis, unless it's in the heading" (P 257).
Rule: American spelling, the serial comma, contractions formed with not admitted (don't, can't, wasn't) and any contraction that can be misread avoided. A run-in heading closes with a period, not a colon. Headings descend without skipping a level and exist for structure. A sidebar that must follow a passage to make sense is not a sidebar. The dossier chooses the period for its run-in heads; the guide permits any terminal mark, so that choice is a house decision within house law.

**8. Cross-references name a title, never a page.**
Support: 4 kept rows on 2 documents. 10 (v1.04a); 268, 269, 270 (v1.08a).
True wording: "We avoid page references as much as possible." (10, 270); "Use cross-references judiciously" (268); "We use letters, not numbers, for appendices" (269).
Rule: The dossier points to another entry by its quoted title, to a chapter by its number, to a table by its unquoted title; it never cites a page, because pagination moves between printings.

**9. Game terms have a typography: capitalise the term classes, italicise spells and items as titles, bold a stat-block name once, and bold means a game object.**
Support: 11 kept rows on 8 documents; 1 PARTIAL row cited for their quotation only. 7, 68 (v1.04a); 273, 274, 275 (v1.08a); 306 (2021); Dragon guidelines 184; 2024 capitalisation 80, 82, 367, 43; 2021 creature types (P 305).
True wording: "The italicized terms are treated as titles" (7); "the wall of fire spell produces a wall of fire" (68, 274); "write 'four goblins,' not 'four loud goblins'" (275); "this indicates the term is a game object" (306); "'Cube' is capitalized, which indicates a term in the Rules Glossary" (P 81); "just being used in their normal sense" (43).
Disagreement: none on the rules; the 2024 books capitalise many more mechanics (Hit Points, Bonus Action) than the 2021 internal guide, whose examples still read "hit points" (P 312).
Rule: In the dossier no word wears game typography, because the dossier contains no game object; the moment a term is capitalised or bolded the reader is told it is a rule to be looked up. Bold as a run-in label at the head of a slot or an entry is a different device and is allowed once per paragraph (Reconciliation 1). Game typography belongs on the DM page, where a creature's name is bold at first appearance and nothing sits between the number and the name.

**10. The verb idiom of the rules is a closed list, and its phrasings are canonical.**
Support: 20 kept rows on 6 documents; 2 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. 62, 63, 66, 67, 6 (v1.04a); 277, 278, 279, 280, (P 281); 282, 283, 284, 285, 287 (v1.08a); 307, 308, 309 (2021); Kobold Press 64; Half-Cover 78; 2024 conditions 82; verb list (P 5).
True wording: "You make a saving throw, and it succeeds or fails" (62); "We have abandoned that practice." (6, 66, 279, 280); "Avoid referring to points of damage" (67, 282); "We often prefer the word 'total'" (280); "proficient in Elvish and Arcana" (284); "This term is a noun, not a verb" (285); "The word 'attack' confuses many of our readers" (307); "call it a stat block" (309); "Beware of using 'human' as a synonym for 'mortal.'" (287).
Rule: For the DM page only: cast a spell, deal and take damage, make a saving throw that succeeds or fails, make an attack that hits or misses, regain hit points, finish a rest, succeed on a check (never merely make one unless both outcomes follow at once), ten fire damage never ten points of it, an extra 1d4 fire damage, proficient in a skill or language and with a tool or weapon, Dungeon Master a noun. For the archivist one transfer holds: human is a people, not a synonym for mortal, and humanoid is a creature type and stays off the civic page.

**11. The word list fixes spellings and plurals; abbreviations are a fixed set; ordering is letter by letter; the 2024 books renamed core terms.**
Support: 7 kept rows on 5 documents; 2 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. 288, 292 (v1.08a); 310 (2021); 2024 renames 44, 236, 368, 46; 2021 additions (P 311); species (P 369).
True wording: "mithral (not mithril)" (288); "NPC nonplayer character" (292); "We use letter-by-letter alphabetization." (310); "Cast a Spell action -> Magic action" (44); "a monster that has a personal name" (368); "three paragraphs and a table to roll on" (46).
Rule: Dwarves, not dwarfs; staffs; war band; drow, kenku and yuan-ti unchanged in the plural. The dossier's own lists and registers run letter by letter, spaces ignored. Where the world is a D&D world the current word is species, not race, and a named, characterful monster is an NPC.

**12. Time is written in days and years in rules, and in the Realms the week is a tenday, the hour a bell, and the clock never o'clock.**
Support: 5 kept rows on 3 documents; 2 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. 286 (v1.08a; P 70 for v1.04a); 353, 354, 355 (Realms guide); readers on WotC's own breaches 381 (and P 77).
True wording: "say a game effect lasts for 7 days, not 1 week" (286); "'mid morning' or 'nigh sunset.'" (353); "'first day,' 'second day,' 'third day,'" (354); "tenday (never week)" (355).
Disagreement: readers note WotC's own hardcovers still print "week" (381), so the Realms rule is aspirational in the publisher's practice.
Rule: The archivist keeps time in the units the world keeps. Durations in rules are days or years, never weeks or months, because the length of a week varies by world. In-world speech gives loose times (mid morning, nigh sunset), bells where a temple has a clock, and counts the days of the tenday; there is no weekend. This is a setting-specific rule and the dossier adopts only its shape: name the local unit and keep it.

**13. The house keeps an in-world lexicon: forms of address, greetings, the trade tongue, and the word for a party.**
Support: 7 kept rows on 2 documents; 1 PARTIAL row cited for their quotation only. 289, 290, 291 (v1.08a, Forgotten Realms Terms and Adventuring Parties, both new in 2019); 356, 357, 358, 359 (Realms guide); P 73.
True wording: "goodsir (a form of address, not capped)" (289, 357); "godsforsaken (replaces Godforsaken)" (356); "use 'group,' 'band,' 'company,' or, especially, 'party.'" (291); "Common is little more than a trade" (358); "Well met. The most often used greeting" (359).
Rule: The dossier's address terms are lowercase when they are forms of address (goodsir) and capped when they are titles (Goodman). A polytheistic world says godsforsaken. A company of adventurers is a party, band or company, never a fellowship, because the word carries Tolkien and D&D parties are fractious. The common tongue is a plain trade language and nuance lives in the older regional tongues, which is a licence for the Herald's pools to be plainer than the chronicle line. These are Realms rules; the dossier borrows the method (a short list of house words with their reasons), not the words.

**14. Setting stewardship and content standards: leave the world as found, keep it G sliding to PG, no stereotypes, no real-world religions as evil analogues, and take setting facts from official sources.**
Support: 12 kept rows on 4 documents; 5 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. Standards 2020: 330, 331, 332, 333, 334; Realms guide: 348, 349, 350, 352, (P 351); IP guide 361, (P 360); general guide 244, 272, (P 271); Dungeoncraft (P 364), (P 382).
True wording: "D&D is, for the most part, a G-rated game." (331); "Don't use profanity." (330); "Don't attach specific traits or attributes to all members" (332); "their evil should be called out as awful and wrong" (333); "(Pack it in. Pack it out.)" (349); "The Forgotten Realms is a hopeful setting." (348); "Keep it PG." (352); "and their stuff is for taking" (361); "Don't rely on Internet searches" (244); "'She is in Elysium.'" (272).
Rule: The dossier never attaches a trait to all members of a people, culture, religion or sexuality; it never draws an evil faith from a living one; it names a villain's wrong as wrong; it keeps sex and gore at PG (the 2020 Standards and the Realms guide; the 2004 PG-13 of feature 18 is retired, Reconciliation 7); it has no profanity. It leaves established places, peoples and gods restorable. Its facts come from the setting's own record, not from a wiki. A character is on a plane and in Elysium. Note the tone split the guide itself records: hopeful Realms, cynical Greyhawk, tragic Dragonlance; a dossier declares which it is.

## II. The publisher's adventure doctrine, in its own documents

**15. The Adventure Design Guide (Perkins and Bilsland) is where the here-and-now, credible-threat and concision principles actually live; they are not in the style guide.**
Support: 12 kept rows on 2 documents; 4 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. 335, 336, 337, 338, 339, 340, 341, 342, 343, 344, 345; relays Shea 227 and (P) 18, (P) 226; the guide's own Chicago 16 citation (P 346) and its pointer to the format template (P 347).
True wording: "An adventure is about the here and now." (336); "An adventure needs a credible threat" (335); "to ease the burden on the DM" (337); "present information in a logical order" (338); "features, mannerisms, and quirks" (339); "as long as the adventure can still surprise" (340); "The player characters (PCs) are the heroes" (341); "players will feel 'railroaded' by the plot" (342); "An adventure needs to rise above the humdrum" (343); "Don't decide in advance how the adventure should end." (345).
Correction carried from the chair: the first sweep's "house bullets" (P 18) came from Shea's page and are these principles, not the style guide's; the guide itself is now fetched and verified.
Rule: The dossier's entries are about the here and now; a little history sets a thing up and the less of it the better. Information runs in the order a first-time reader needs it: a person is introduced before being named in passing, and nothing important is buried. Text is as concise as the reader's burden allows, and the licence to linger is reserved for people, dressing and treasure. ## III. The read-aloud craft rules, where official and community converge

**21. Third person, never the viewer; the dissent is one essay that wants second person for engagement.**
Support: 14 kept rows on 8 documents; 2 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. Merwin 104, 414 (and P 105); Paizo 11, 173; Heir (P 167); Lynch's readers 111; Alexander 106, 378, 661, 662; Dyson 107; BAATAG 109; DM's Workshop dissent 108, 407; compass directions dispute 479.
True wording: "Move from the second-person point of view to the third person" (104); "The bloated corpse of a headless dragon dominates the center" (414); "it does not make any reference to the viewer" (11); "Don't tell them how they're reacting to it." (662); "Write without assumption of perspective" (109); "addressed to them instead of the empty air" (108).
Disagreement, explicit: DM's Workshop (108, 407) offers the reverse shift, third-person objective to second-person subjective, conditionally, for a DM whose players lose focus; every other witness, official and community, forbids the viewer; the publisher's own specimens are the exception in practice, addressing the party as you while its platform column forbids it (feature 53). Compass directions are urged by BAATAG (109) and Designing Dungeons (110) and disputed in a forum exchange where relative placement is preferred and then shown to break when the party enters by another door (479).
Rule: The dossier's subject is always the thing, never the reader: the mill stands, the corpse dominates, the gate is barred. No "you", "your", "you see", "as you enter". Perspective is not assumed; a door is on the east wall, never on the far side.

**22. No assigned feelings, thoughts, actions or movements; the record shows the scene and leaves the reaction to the reader.**
Support: 10 kept rows on 7 documents; 1 PARTIAL row cited for their quotation only. Merwin (P 105), 104; Alexander 106, 378, 661, 662; Heir 168; Dyson 107; Gus L 123; Designing Dungeons 110; Paizo 11.
True wording: "declaring the thoughts or feelings or (worse yet!) actions" (106, 661); "read aloud text does not run the character" (168); "predicts the future for the party (upon approaching...)" (107); "Don't tell the players what to feel" (110, a heading).
Rule: The archivist never writes that a visitor is afraid, steps forward, smiles or approaches. It never predicts. Emotion in the dossier belongs to the townsfolk on the record (the miller is bitter), never to the reader.

**23. Describe what can be sensed and nothing that cannot; strike "seems" and "appears" from perception; but an archivist's hedge is a different instrument and is kept for inference.**
Support: 11 kept rows on 8 documents. Merwin 114, 115, 16, 417; Dausuul 441; Alexander's lorebook practice 563, 564; Volo device 523 (and 40, feature 42); Noonan 159.
True wording: "Describe everything the characters can sense, but not what they cannot." (114); "Everything they sense 'appears to be' what it is." (16); "For the characters, perception is reality." (417); "'Appears as' is just instinctive DM pedantry" (441); "It appears that the carach" (564); "which one can only hope are exaggerated" (563).
Disagreement, and it is a category difference not a quarrel: the read-aloud rule bans seems and appears because at the table perception is reality and the hedge telegraphs a trick (115, 417); Alexander's in-world lorebook hedges its source's boasts and marks its inferences with "It appears" because a document written by someone can be wrong (563, 564, 523). The first governs spoken description of what is in front of the party; the second governs a written record.
Rule: For the Herald's spoken pools: no seems, no appears; what is perceived is stated as fact. For the archivist: fact is stated as fact, and a hedge appears only where the record is drawing an inference or reporting a claim it cannot check, and then it is marked plainly (by report, it appears, the ledger claims). A hedge is never used as a trick-telegraph or as filler.

**24. Concrete and specific over generic and evaluative: one telling detail, the filler adjectives purged, three of five senses, the noun and verb carry it.**
Support: 21 kept rows on 14 documents; 3 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. Merwin 116, 415; Gus L 123, 451; Skerples 122, 144; Conowitch 124; Alexander 125; Brown via Gaming Ballistic 126; Lynch via Miller (P 119), Lynch 430, 431, 435, 724; Radiating Gnome 439, 440, 444; Dausuul 442; Hartlage 601, (P 454); 602; Shea 128; dScryb review 129; Hewitt (P 619).
True wording: "a party comprised solely of elves or florists" (116); "sharp edged obsidian" (123); "Every sentence needs to fight for its life" (122); "keep it simple with a few powerful details" (124); "Try to include three of them" (125); "the telltale sensory detail, rather than the generic abstract trait" (P 119); "the adjectives and adverbs were not used very well" (430); "the prim farmers wife tells you" (431); "what the PC's EXPERIENCE when they meet them" (435); "In this context detail is bad. It is trivia." (724); "those words are actually our mortal enemies" (439); "a flap of skin flaps under an old woman's arm" (440); "the ramp needs to climb" (444); "Scaliness was pure fluff from the start." (442); "focus on telling details and plot-critical information" (601); "sounds like it came from Ikea instructions" (128); "is a mystery to be unraveled" (451); "gothic atmosphere is clearly set through the daunting presence" (P 619, of the statue).
Disagreement: Lynch's cure for a generic town is one added modifier before a role noun (431); Radiating Gnome and Dausuul call modifiers the enemy and cut them (439, 442). Both agree on the test: does the word conjure a specific thing. Lynch himself separates specificity from detail and calls detail trivia (724). Shea wants fantastical language over instruction-manual flatness (128); Lux wants the classics' "expressive but functional language" (feature 33); the reconciliation is in the noun choice, not in ornament.
Rule: An idol is not evil; it has a toothy glare or is sharp-edged obsidian. A wife is prim, or a granny, or lusty, and the one word conjures the woman. Every adjective and adverb must earn its place or go; little, big, grand, tall and dark are hunted and replaced. Each entry touches more than sight (a smell, a sound, a texture) and gives one fact that implies a story. The verb is chosen for its exact motion (a causeway climbs, it does not rise). Whether atmosphere is carried by the objects named rather than by naming the mood is a house decision, not a sourced rule: Hewitt's page shows the objects (P 619) and draws no such contrast.

**25. No novelist's urge: no soliloquy, no cutscene, no story in the box; an adventure is not a short story and the record is not a novel.**
Support: 8 kept rows on 6 documents; 1 PARTIAL row cited for their quotation only. Merwin 117; Heir 169; DM's Workshop 404, 405; Paizo 14, 180; Perkins 200; Baur (P 216); Gearing 759 (the words are meat on a skeleton).

**26. Read it aloud to test it; the ear catches what the page hides; cadence is the delivery problem.**
Support: 8 kept rows on 5 documents. Merwin 118, 418; Hartlage 154, 453, 667; Radiating Gnome 445; Kobold guide via Shea 227; DM's Workshop 156.

## IV. The key: order, format, density

**30. Information order follows what is met first and moves from general to specific: the seen-at-a-glance block leads, the salient thing (an ogre in the open) comes first, the hidden thing does not, and the load-bearing fact is never mid-list.**
Support: 19 kept rows on 13 documents; 5 PARTIAL rows cited for their quotation only. Alexander 137, 652, 653, 136, 428, 635, 647 (and P 463, P 464, P 651); Designing Dungeons 138; Gus L 139; Skerples 144; Radiating Gnome 446; Hartlage 452, 666, 597; Alexander's prep habit 474; Perkins and Bilsland 338; Doyle via Gaming Ballistic (P 127); the forum's monsters-first players (P 480); Necropraxis 716, 717; Campbell 692.
True wording: "focus on the sequencing of information" (652); "move from the general to the specific" (653); "should talk about the ogre first" (635); "Complete sentences are overrated" (137); "Write a paragraph that describes everything immediately observable" (138); "That seems like a lot of redirection" (446); "the text mentioned the runes alongside other, less important details" (452); "buried in the middle of a paragraph" (474, of details essential to the adventure); "After the bar are the immediately visible items!" (692); "take in the area with a glance" (717).
Disagreement, explicit: Designing Dungeons puts monsters last in the first description and first in later keying (138); Gus L starts with the room's inhabitants (139); one poster's players want the creatures before the furnishings (P 480); Paizo keeps creatures out of the read-aloud altogether (feature 17). The agreed principle above the quarrel is Alexander's: order by what is salient on arrival, not by a template slot.
Rule: A locale or building entry opens with what is seen at a glance (a settlement entry opens on its head record and a region on its history: the level map of Reconciliation 2) and holds everything a visitor would know on arrival; it then moves from the general to the specific in the order a reader would ask. A conspicuous thing comes first; a hidden thing is never first. A fact that matters is never buried among ornaments mid-paragraph. The eye is not sent up and down (feet, cliff, causeway, tower); the progression is even.

**31. Against filling the format: a fixed slot order makes writers write where there is nothing to say and lands information out of sequence; the delve format is the extreme, and its one good idea is that everything needed is on the page.**

(656); "it artificially isolates the 'encounter'" (709); "a compact series of encounters appropriate for a specific level" (786); "They still signpost traps and treasure with bold italics" (789).
WITNESS SKIPPED BY TRIAGE: Bruce Cordell's own words that the fourth-edition tactical format made a compelling narrative difficult and left too little room for exploration, lore or character (783, 784, 785); fourth edition compressing encounter-less locations into single paragraphs (787). The designer's own statement on the delve was skipped. Not verified.
Rule: The dossier's slots are fixed for the reader's sake (feature 18) but empty slots are omitted, never filled: a place with no trade has no trade line. Within a slot the order is the scene's, not the template's. Everything needed to use an entry is in the entry; the reader is not sent elsewhere for a fact that belongs here.

**37. Present tense; drop the future "will"; active voice; replace to-be with the verb of the thing.**

The copula itself is not struck: the publisher's own place prose runs is and are freely (feature 59), and the clerk's voice of Reconciliation 3 is built on it.

## V. The place and the record: how official D&D writes a settlement

## Reconciliations: the ten self-contradictions of the round-8 section, resolved

The round-8 critic's section 6 found the section binding the archivist to rules that contradict one another. Each is resolved below by naming the unit, the register or the authority that separates them; where the resolution is a house decision rather than a sourced one it says so. The rules in Parts I to V are amended in place to match, and the register map is rewritten to the resolved rules.

**Reconciliation 1 (features 9 and 32): bold.** Rule 9 said no word on the civic page wears game typography, because bold tells the reader a term is a game object to look up (306, 275); Rule 32 said the entry bolds three or four things. Two typographies were confused. Bold as the mark of a game object is banned on the civic page and stays banned. Bold as a run-in label is a different device and is the publisher's own practice for place text: the Ravenloft guidance opens each tone with a one-word bold label and a sentence (906, 907), the 2024 city opens each neighbourhood with its name in bold as a run-in label (P 979's supported limb), the Adventurers League template bolds keyed objects and reads the run-in head as a heading of the lowest rank (322, 256), and the critics who bold do so to point at a following paragraph (139, 654, 715). Resolved rule (binds the archivist): bold in the dossier marks a run-in label only, the lead word of a slot or the name of a person or place at the head of its own entry, one per paragraph and at first appearance; never an inline term, never a second mention, never decoration. Because the dossier contains no game object, a bold word on the civic page can mean only one thing. The Herald's pools and the chronicle line carry no bold at all. Rule 32 is narrowed to this; Rule 9 stands.

**Reconciliation 2 (features 15, 30, 35, 38, 39, 44): the five "opens with" rules.** They are rules for five units, and feature 52 gives the level map: the document's first page states what the place is for (44); a realm or region runs history in broad strokes, politics and society, then locales (38); a settlement entry opens on its head record, the count and kind of residents and who rules (39, which is the publisher's Population and Government, feature 51), in the present state before any history (15, 35); a locale or building entry opens with what is seen at a glance (30); a person's entry opens on who, where found, what wanted (50). None competes once the unit is named. Resolved rule: each unit opens as its level prescribes; the rule of features 15 and 35 (the present before the past) is not an opening rule but an ordering rule inside every unit.

**Reconciliation 3 (features 41 and 42 against 37, 17 and 3): the declared clerk's voice.** The round-8 text modelled the clerk's honest omission as "the record is silent; the clerk was turned away", a to-be construction and an agentless passive, while Rule 37 strikes to-be, Rule 17 bans the first person outside a character's mouth and Rule 3 bans the second person anywhere. The measured baseline settles the verb question: the publisher's own place prose runs is and are at 24.84 per thousand words (1011), and the Ravenloft guidance's model sentences are place-is-adjective sentences (907); what Rule 37 strikes is the expletive there is, the habitual passive and the future will (112, 133, 183), not the copula. Resolved rule (binds the archivist): the clerk speaks in the third person and the present tense, active wherever an agent is known: the record holds no entry for the mill's founding; the warden refused the clerk entry to the keep. An agentless passive stands only where the agent is unknown and the sentence would otherwise invent one. The clerk never says I; the record names itself in the third person (this register, the record) and so keeps Rule 17, because the clerk is not a character; first and second person appear only inside a quoted in-world document set apart as an object (feature 42), which is a character's mouth. And by the WotC editor's law (1113) the clerk never says never: no entry, not no such thing.

**Reconciliation 4 (feature 6 against features 39 and 41): digits, and years.** Feature 54 carries the measurement: the publisher's lore and voiced registers carry no digit; its rules, DM-facing prose, tables and labelled fields carry numerals; a calendar year with an era mark is the one systematic exception in its gazetteer (980), and even there elapsed spans go both ways on one page (981). Resolved rule (binds the archivist): the dossier's running prose spells every count, span and measure. A year is a registry label: it lives in a table cell or the dated line-head of the chronicle line, where the house admits numerals (267), and in running prose it is given relatively (seventy-eight years ago, the 2024 guide's own form, 973) or by reign and event, never as a four-digit numeral. The registry facts of feature 41 (a founding year, a building class, a well's depth) are recorded in the entry's head or the chronicle's line-head as labels and described in the prose in words; quotations keep the digits their sources printed. Rule 6 stands; Rules 39 and 41 are amended to say where a number lives.

**Reconciliation 5 (feature 8 against feature 41): "See Durnan, p. 17".** Feature 41 endorsed the Yawning Portal's first official entry, a bare cross-reference to its keeper (456), as a shape the earliest record may take. The shape is endorsed; the page number is not. The house guide avoids page references because they are hand-added at galley and break across printings (270, 10) and points by title instead (268). Resolved rule (binds the archivist): the earliest entry for a place may be a pointer to its keeper's entry, and the pointer is by title, never by page: The Yawning Portal, an inn. See Durnan. The FR1 line is cited as history, and its page number is the part the house since abandoned. Rule 41 is amended.

**Reconciliation 6 (features 36, 41, 46): three minima for a minor place.** Feature 55 resolves them as tiers, not minima: a place the record only names gets a name and a class and no entry (36); a minor place with an entry gets one to three sentences under a label, one of them the flavourful thing (41), and none of them a bare epithet standing for an entry (46); a major place gets the full slot set. The tier is set by purpose in play (36; 955). Rules 36, 41 and 46 are amended to name the tier they speak of.

**Reconciliation 7 (feature 14 against feature 18): PG and PG-13.** The two figures come from documents of different date and standing: the publisher's own Standards of 2020 (331, D&D is for the most part G, sliding toward PG; PG-13 products need approval) and the Realms guide (352, keep it PG) against an organised-play editor's column of 2004 (172, keep your writing PG-13, because children play). The current publisher document wins. Resolved rule (binds the archivist): PG. The 2004 PG-13 is retired to the DM page as history of organised play; Rule 18 keeps its other two rules (fixed slots for the stranger's sake; bare-bones essential) and loses its rating.

**Reconciliation 8 (feature 43 against feature 45): Gygax's names and Gygax's diction.** Feature 43 admired Hommlet's names as Gygax at his best (P 409) while feature 45 leaves the Gygaxian diction. Proper names are nouns of the world and diction is the archivist's; the two never meet in a sentence's grammar. Hommlet's names (Jaroo Ashstaff, Ostler Gundigoot) are plain compounds, exactly the plain-name-in-a-strange-world model feature 43 wants (447), and the sermon feature 45 leaves is a matter of passives, digressions and platinum words in the running prose (30, P 31). Resolved rule (binds the archivist): a name may be as strange as its world, provided it is pronounceable and plain in its parts; the prose around it keeps the Gygaxian authority and none of the Gygaxian diction. Feature 57 carries the naming form the settlement books use.

**Reconciliation 9 (feature 2): the protagonist rule.** Feature 2 transferred the style guide's brand rule about adventure protagonists (a small band, not a lone knight; 246) to the settlement record without argument. The transfer is retracted. The rule is about who an adventure is about, and the dossier is not about anyone; its people are many because a place is many, which the critics show from the other side (a web of relationships among the residents, P 433's quotation and 438; a town that hates the next town, 832; two good people at least, 1084 skipped). Resolved rule (binds the archivist): feature 2 keeps the tone gate (what would seem out of place in high fantasy does not belong; humour and tragedy admitted while they do not overtake the tone; 245, 72) and drops the sentence about a company of people. The dossier records a place; the place has as many persons of record as its slots need.

The target is one or two sentences: the current official template (314), the publisher's 2005 observation (157/94/991), the publisher-hosted specimen (900), cygnus (705) and Dyson's one or two simple sentences (P 999's quotation) agree.
