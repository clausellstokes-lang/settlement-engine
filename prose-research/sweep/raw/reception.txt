{{good article}}
{{Use Oxford spelling |date=November 2017}}
[[J. R. R. Tolkien]]'s bestselling fantasy novel ''[[The Lord of the Rings]]'' had an initial mixed literary reception. Despite some enthusiastic early reviews from supporters such as [[W. H. Auden]], [[Iris Murdoch]], and [[C. S. Lewis]], scholars noted a measure of literary hostility to Tolkien, which continued until the start of the 21st century. From 1982, Tolkien scholars such as [[Tom Shippey]] and [[Verlyn Flieger]] began to roll back the hostility, defending Tolkien, rebutting the critics' attacks and analysing what they saw as good qualities in Tolkien's writing.<!--cited in body of article-->

From 2003, scholars such as [[Brian Rosebury]] began to consider why Tolkien had attracted such hostility. Rosebury stated that Tolkien avoided calling ''The Lord of the Rings'' a novel, and that in Shippey's view Tolkien had been aiming to create a [[Tolkien and the medieval|medieval-style]] heroic romance, despite modern scepticism about [[Anatomy of Criticism#mode|that literary mode]]. In 2014, [[Patrick Curry]] analysed the reasons for the hostility, finding it both visceral and full of evident mistakes, and suggesting that the issue was that the critics felt that Tolkien threatened their dominant ideology, [[modernism]].<!--cited in body of article-->

Interpretations of ''The Lord of the Rings'' have included [[Marxism|Marxist]] criticism, sometimes at odds with Tolkien's [[social conservatism]]; the psychological reading of heroes, their partners, and their opponents as [[Jungian archetypes]]; and [[Tolkien and the modernists|comparison of Tolkien with modernist writers]].

== Context ==
{{further|J. R. R. Tolkien|The Lord of the Rings}}

[[J. R. R. Tolkien]] (1892-1973) was an English [[Catholic Church|Roman Catholic]] writer, poet, [[philology|philologist]], and academic, best known as the author of the [[high fantasy]] works ''[[The Hobbit]]'' and ''[[The Lord of the Rings]]''.<ref>{{cite book |last=Carpenter |first=Humphrey |author-link=Humphrey Carpenter |title=J. R. R. Tolkien: A Biography |title-link=J. R. R. Tolkien: A Biography |year=1978 |orig-date=1977 |publisher=[[Unwin Paperbacks]] |isbn=978-0-04928-039-7 |pages=111, 200, 266 and throughout}}</ref> 

In 1954-55, ''The Lord of the Rings'' was published. In 1957, it was awarded the [[International Fantasy Award]]. The publication of the [[Ace Books]] and [[Ballantine Books|Ballantine]] paperbacks in the United States helped it to become immensely popular with a new generation in the 1960s. The book has remained so ever since, ranking as one of the most popular works of fiction of the twentieth century, judged by both sales and reader surveys.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Seiler |first=Andy |date=December 16, 2003 |title='Rings' comes full circle |url=https://www.usatoday.com/life/movies/news/2003-12-12-lotr-main_x.htm |access-date=5 August 2020 |website=USA Today}}</ref> In the 2003 "[[Big Read]]" survey conducted by the [[BBC]], ''The Lord of the Rings'' was found to be the "Nation's best-loved book." In similar 2004 polls both Germany<ref>{{Cite web |last=Diver |first=Krysia |date=5 October 2004 |title=A lord for Germany |url=http://www.smh.com.au/articles/2004/10/04/1096871805007.html?from=storyrhs |access-date=7 August 2020 |website=[[The Sydney Morning Herald]]}}</ref> and Australia<ref>{{Cite web |last=Cooper |first=Callista |date=5 December 2005 |title=Epic trilogy tops favourite film poll |url=http://www.abc.net.au/news/newsitems/200512/s1523327.htm |access-date=5 August 2020 |website=ABC News Online}}</ref> also found ''The Lord of the Rings'' to be their favourite book. In a 1999 poll of [[Amazon.com]] customers, ''The Lord of the Rings'' was judged to be their favourite "book of the millennium."<ref>{{Cite web |last=O'Hehir |first=Andrew |date=4 June 2001 |title=The book of the century |url=http://www.salon.com/books/feature/2001/06/04/tolkien/ |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20010610164904/http://www.salon.com/books/feature/2001/06/04/tolkien/ |archive-date=10 June 2001 |access-date=7 August 2020 |website=[[Salon.com]]}}</ref> The popularity of ''The Lord of the Rings'' increased further when [[The Lord of the Rings (film series)|Peter Jackson's film trilogy]] came out in 2001-2003.<ref name="Curry 2020"/>

== Tolkien's own commentary ==

Daniel Timmons writes that Tolkien launched a thread of commentary of his own on ''The Lord of the Rings'' with his own remarks, both in his two forewords (in the first and second editions) to the novel, and in [[The Letters of J. R. R. Tolkien|his letters]] and essays. In the first foreword, having stated that "This tale ... is drawn ... from the memoirs of the renowned Hobbits, Bilbo and Frodo, as they are preserved in the ''[[Red Book of Westmarch]]''" (i.e. [[Pseudotranslation in The Lord of the Rings|the book is a translation]]),{{sfn|Timmons|1998a|pp=107-110}} he comments that the book is "not yet universally recognized as an important branch of study".{{sfn|Timmons|1998a|pp=107-110}} This could be a whimsical allusion to the lack of analysis of the novel by historians, but Timmons states that there is "no clear hint of irony or whimsy".{{sfn|Timmons|1998a|pp=107-110}} Instead, Timmons writes, it could be "wryly indicating" that reception by critics might turn out to be hostile. Whatever the case, he comments, Tolkien hovers between writing as author and [[Editorial framing of The Lord of the Rings|as a fictionalised narrator]]<!--or "narrator/translator", Timmons p. 151-->. Further, Timmons states, Tolkien's other writings either reflect changes in his opinions over time, or border on the "disingenuous" to make an immediate point.{{sfn|Timmons|1998a|pp=107-110}} For instance, Tolkien states firmly in Letter 26 (1937) that "Celtic" things are "mad" and that the names and tales in ''The Silmarillion'' are not Celtic, when it was well known that he loved the Welsh language, and indeed mentioned "the fair elusive beauty that some call Celtic" in Letter 144 (1954). Timmons concludes that Tolkien could "[play] up to his audience" with a variety of "rhetorical techniques" when commenting on his own work.{{sfn|Timmons|1998a|pp=107-110}} 

The second edition foreword has been taken seriously by critics, but it too, Timmons writes, must be treated carefully. He notes that Tolkien's letters show that he began to write ''The Lord of the Rings'' in December 1937, three months after ''The Hobbit'' was published, shortly after several letters to and from his publisher. The foreword however claims that ''The Lord of the Rings'' was started before ''The Hobbit'' appeared. Further inaccuracies about dates and other details follow; Timmons remarks that these throw into question his denial of the Second World War's influence on his book, and any intention to create an allegory. Tolkien admits that "a story-germ uses the soil of experience" but decries scholarly attempts to explore that process as "at best guesses from evidence that is inadequate and ambiguous".{{sfn|Timmons|1998a|pp=112-124}} All the same, Timmons writes, Tolkien made exactly that kind of best guess in his own analyses of ''Beowulf'', ''Maldon'', and ''Sir Gawain''. Tolkien is equally dismissive of the search for parallels in his own life with anything in the story, leading him into further contradictions in his 1966 piece in ''Diplomat'', "Tolkien on Tolkien". He objects, too, to the accusations that the book contains "no religion" and "no women", and to the suggestion that Middle-earth has nothing to do with planet [[Earth]]. Timmons concludes that Tolkien seems to view "any piece of criticism as an unwelcome treatment of his work",{{sfn|Timmons|1998a|pp=112-124}} and that critics need to remain objective, judging Tolkien's claims against his books, rather than assuming that his claims are necessarily true.{{sfn|Timmons|1998a|p=130}}

== Enthusiastic early support ==

Early reviews of ''The Lord of the Rings'' were sharply divided between enthusiastic support and outright rejection. Some literary figures immediately welcomed the book's publication. The poet [[W. H. Auden]], a former pupil of Tolkien's and an admirer of his writings, regarded ''The Lord of the Rings'' as a "masterpiece", further stating that in some cases it outdid the achievement of [[John Milton]]'s 1667-1674 ''[[Paradise Lost]]''.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Auden |first=W. H. |author-link=W. H. Auden |date=22 January 1956 |title=At the End of the Quest, Victory |work=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1956/01/22/books/tolkien-king.html |url-status=live |access-date=4 December 2010 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110220002634/http://www.nytimes.com/1956/01/22/books/tolkien-king.html |archive-date=20 February 2011}}</ref> Kenneth F. Slater wrote in ''[[Nebula Science Fiction]]'', April 1955, "...&nbsp;if you don't read it, you have missed one of the finest books of its type ever to appear".<ref>{{Cite web |title=Something to Read NSF 12 |url=http://www.geocities.ws/linwood/SF/Nebula/KFS12.htm |access-date=19 November 2019}}</ref> [[Michael Straight]] described it in ''[[The New Republic]]'' as "...&nbsp;one of the few works of genius in modern literature."<ref>[https://newrepublic.com/article/136543/fantastic-world-professor-tolkien "The Fantastic World of Professor Tolkien"], Michael Straight, January 17, 1956, New republic</ref> The novelist [[Iris Murdoch]] mentioned Middle-earth characters in her novels, and wrote to Tolkien saying she had been "utterly&nbsp;... delighted, carried away, absorbed by ''The Lord of the Rings''&nbsp;... == Literary hostility ==

=== 20th century ===

[[File:Edmund Wilson.jpg|thumb|upright|The critic [[Edmund Wilson]] attacked Tolkien's work in 1955.<ref name="Wilson 1956"/>]]

Some literary figures rejected Tolkien and ''The Lord of the Rings'' outright. One member of [[the Inklings]], [[Hugo Dyson]],<!--d. 1975--> complained loudly at readings of the book; [[Christopher Tolkien]] records Dyson as "lying on the couch, and lolling and shouting and saying, 'Oh God, no more Elves.{{'"}}<ref>{{Cite AV media |last=Bailey |first=Derek<!--director--> |title=A Film Portrait of J. R. R. Tolkien |date=1992 |type=Television documentary |publisher=Visual Corporation |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=poO7zvPSMj0}}</ref> 

In 1956, the [[literary critic]] [[Edmund Wilson]] wrote a review entitled "Oo, Those Awful Orcs!", calling Tolkien's work "juvenile trash", and saying "Dr. Tolkien has little [[Tolkien's prose style|skill at narrative]] and no [[Storytelling in The Lord of the Rings|instinct for literary form]]."<ref name="Wilson 1956">{{Cite web |last=Wilson |first=Edmund |author-link=Edmund Wilson |date=14 April 1956 |title=Oo, Those Awful Orcs! A review of The Fellowship of the Ring |work=[[The Nation]] |url=http://jrrvf.com/sda/critiques/The_Nation.html |access-date=1 September 2012}}</ref> 

It was however not the case that early reviews were overwhelmingly negative.{{sfn|Timmons|1998a|pp=3-8}} An early reply to Wilson was the [[Classics|classicist]] [[Douglass Parker]]'s 1957 review "{{lang|ang|Hwaet We Holbytla ...}}"{{efn|"How much We of Hobbits [have heard]...", an echo of "{{lang|ang|Hwaet We Gardena}}...", the first words of ''[[Beowulf]]''.}} which stood up for ''The Lord of the Rings'' as a [[worldbuilding]] fantasy. Parker wrote that the "one serious attack" on the novel was "a rather nasty hatchet-job", which "appears to have resulted from Wilson's ineluctable conviction that all fantasy is trash, ''The Lord of the Rings'' is fantasy, ''[[wikt:ergo|ergo]]'' [the book was trash.]"<ref name="Parker 1957"/> Parker argued that the book was in fact "probably the most original and varied creation ever seen in the genre, and certainly the most self-consistent; yet it is tied up with and bridged to reality as is no other fantasy."<ref name="Parker 1957"/> He noted that the book is far from a "piddling" good-defeats-evil [[allegory]], not least because the characters on the good side "are not abstractions, nor are they wholly good, nor are they [all] alike".<ref name="Parker 1957">{{cite journal |last=Parker |first=Douglass |author-link=Douglass Parker |title=Hwaet We Holbytla ... |journal=[[The Hudson Review]] |date=1957 |volume=9 |issue=4 |pages=598-609 |jstor=4621633}}</ref>
 
In 1954, the Scottish poet [[Edwin Muir]] wrote in ''[[The Observer]]'' that "however one may look at it, ''The Fellowship of the Ring'' is an extraordinary book",<ref name="Muir 1954">{{Cite news |last=Muir |first=Edwin |author-link=Edwin Muir |date=22 August 1954 |title=Review: The Fellowship of the Ring |work=[[The Observer]]}}</ref> but that although Tolkien "describes a tremendous conflict between good and evil ... his good people are consistently good, his evil figures immovably evil".<ref name="Muir 1954" /> In 1955, Muir attacked ''[[The Return of the King]]'', writing that "All the characters are boys masquerading as adult heroes ... and will never come to puberty ... Hardly one of them knows anything about women", causing Tolkien to complain angrily to his publisher.<ref name="Lobdell 2013 in Drout">{{Cite encyclopedia |year=2013 |title=Criticism of Tolkien, Twentieth Century |encyclopedia=[[J. R. R. Tolkien Encyclopedia]] |publisher=[[Routledge]] |last=Lobdell |first=Jared |author-link=Jared Lobdell |orig-date=2007 |editor-last=Drout |editor-first=Michael D. C. |editor-link=Michael D. C. Drout |pages=109-110 |isbn=978-0-415-96942-0}}</ref><ref name="Letter #177">{{harvnb|Carpenter|2023|loc=letter #177 to [[Rayner Unwin]], 8 December 1955 }}</ref>

In 1969, the feminist scholar [[Catherine R. Stimpson]] published a book-length attack on Tolkien,<ref name="Stimpson 1969">{{cite book |last=Stimpson |first=Catherine R. |title=J. R. R. Tolkien |date=1969 |publisher=Columbia Essays on Modern Writers, No. 41 |isbn=978-0231032070}}</ref><ref name="Curry 2020"/> describing him as "an incorrigible [[England in Middle-earth|nationalist]]", peopling his writing with "irritatingly, blandly, traditionally masculine" one-dimensional characters who live out a "bourgeois pastoral idyll".<ref name="Curry 2020"/> This set the tone for other hostile critics.<ref name="Curry 2005"/> [[Hal Colebatch (author)|Hal Colebatch]]<ref name="Colebatch 2003">{{cite book |last=Colebatch |first=Hal |author-link=Hal Colebatch (author) |title=Return of the Heroes: The Lord of the Rings, Star Wars, Harry Potter, and Social Conflict |date=2003 |publisher=Cybereditions |edition=2nd |at=Chapter 6}}</ref><ref name="Colebatch 2013">{{Cite encyclopedia |last=Colebatch |first=Hal |author-link=Hal Colebatch (author) |title=Communism |encyclopedia=[[J. === 21st century ===

A measure of hostility continued until the start of the 21st century. In 2001, ''[[The New York Times]]'' reviewer Judith Shulevitz criticized the "pedantry" of [[Tolkien's style|Tolkien's literary style]], saying that he "formulated a high-minded belief in the importance of his mission as a literary preservationist, which turns out to be death to literature itself."<ref>{{Cite news |last=Shulevitz |first=Judith |date=22 April 2001 |title=Hobbits in Hollywood |work=[[The New York Times]] |url=https://www.nytimes.com/books/01/04/22/bookend/bookend.html |access-date=1 February 2011}}</ref> The same year, in the ''[[London Review of Books]]'', Jenny Turner wrote that ''The Lord of the Rings'' provided "a closed space, finite and self-supporting, fixated on its own nostalgia, quietly running down";<ref name="Turner 2001">{{Cite journal |last=Turner |first=Jenny |date=15 November 2001 |title=Reasons for Liking Tolkien |url=https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v23/n22/jenny-turner/reasons-for-liking-tolkien |journal=[[London Review of Books]] |volume=23 |issue=22}}</ref> the books were suitable for "vulnerable people. You can feel secure inside them, no matter what is going on in the nasty world outside. The merest weakling can be the master of this cosy little universe. Even a silly furry little hobbit can see his dreams come true."<ref name="Turner 2001"/> She cited the Tolkien scholar [[Tom Shippey]]'s observation ("The hobbits ... have to be dug out ... of no fewer than [[Frodo's five Homely Houses|five Homely Houses]]"<ref>{{Cite book |last=Shippey |first=Tom |title=[[J. R. R. Tolkien: Author of the Century]] |date=2001 |publisher=[[HarperCollins]] |isbn=978-0261-10401-3 |page=65 |author-link=Tom Shippey}}</ref>) that the quest repeats itself, the chase in [[the Shire]] ending with dinner at [[Farmer Maggot]]'s, the trouble with [[Old Man Willow]] ending with hot baths and comfort at [[Tom Bombadil]]'s, and again safety after adventures in [[Bree (Middle-earth)|Bree]], [[Rivendell]], and [[Lothlórien]].<ref name="Turner 2001"/> Turner commented that reading the book is to "find oneself gently rocked between bleakness and luxury, the sublime and the cosy. Scary, safe again. Scary, safe again. Scary, safe again."<ref name="Turner 2001"/> In her view, this compulsive rhythm is what [[Sigmund Freud]] described in his ''[[Beyond the Pleasure Principle]]''.<ref name="Turner 2001"/> She asked whether, in his writing, Tolkien, whose father died when he was 3 and his mother when he was 12, was not "trying to recover his lost parents, his lost childhood, an impossibly [[wikt:prelapsarian|prelapsarian]] sense of peace?"<ref name="Turner 2001"/>

The critic Richard Jenkyns, writing in ''[[The New Republic]]'' in 2002, criticized a perceived lack of psychological depth. Both the characters and the work itself were, according to Jenkyns, "anemic, and lacking in fiber."<ref>{{Cite magazine |last=Jenkyns |first=Richard |date=28 January 2002 |title=Bored of the Rings |url=http://www.tnr.com/article/bored-the-rings |magazine=The New Republic |access-date=1 February 2011}}</ref> Also that year, the science-fiction author [[David Brin]] criticised the book in ''[[Salon (website)|Salon]]'' as carefully crafted and seductive, but backward-looking. He wrote that he had enjoyed it as a child as escapist fantasy, but that it clearly also reflected the decades of totalitarianism in the mid-20th century. Brin saw the change from [[feudalism]] to a free middle class as progress, and in his view, Tolkien, like the [[Romantic poets]], as opposed to that. As well as its being "a great tale", Brin saw good points in the work; Tolkien was, he wrote, self-critical, for example blaming the elves for trying to halt time by forging [[Three Rings|their Rings]], while the [[Ringwraith]]s could be seen as cautionary figures of Greek ''[[hubris]]'', men who reached too high, and fell.<ref name="Brin 2002">{{Cite web |last=Brin |first=David |date=17 December 2002 |title=J.R.R. Tolkien -- enemy of progress |url=https://www.salon.com/2002/12/17/tolkien_brin/ |access-date=7 August 2020 |website=[[Salon.com]]}}</ref><!-- republished as --><ref>{{Cite book |last=Brin |first=David |chapter=The Lord of the Rings: J.R.R. Tolkien vs the Modern Age |title=Through Stranger Eyes: Reviews, Introductions, Tributes & Iconoclastic Essays |publisher=Nimble Books |year=2008 |isbn=978-1-934840-39-9 |at=part 1, "Dreading Tomorrow: Exploring our nightmares through literature", essay 3}}</ref>

The historian [[Jared Lobdell]], evaluating the hostile reception of Tolkien by the mainstream literary establishment in the 2006 ''[[J. R. R. Tolkien Encyclopedia]]'', noted that Wilson was "well known as an enemy of religion", of popular books, and "conservatism in any form".<ref name="Lobdell 2013 in Drout"/> Lobdell concluded that "no 'mainstream critic' appreciated ''The Lord of the Rings'' or indeed was in a position to write criticism on it - most being unsure what it was and why readers liked it."<ref name="Lobdell 2013 in Drout"/> He noted that [[Brian Aldiss]] was a critic of science fiction, distinguishing such "critics" from [[Tolkien research|Tolkien scholarship]], the study and analysis of [[Themes of The Lord of the Rings|Tolkien's themes]], [[Tolkien's influences|influences]], and methods.<ref name="Lobdell 2013 in Drout"/>

== Rehabilitation ==

=== Tolkien studies ===
{{further|Tolkien research}}

[[File:Patrick Grant's Jungian View of LOTR.svg|thumb|upright=1.5|Diagram of Patrick Grant's [[Jungian archetypes|Jungian]] View of [[Psychological journeys of Middle-earth|''The Lord of the Rings'' with hero, anima and other archetypes]]<ref name="Grant 1973" />]]

Tolkien's fiction began to acquire respectability in academia only at the end of his life, with the publication of [[Paul H. Kocher]]'s 1972 ''[[Master of Middle-Earth]]''.<ref name="Timmons 1998"/> Written before the publication of ''The Silmarillion'', Kocher inferred or guessed many of the key points about Tolkien's writings, later confirmed by [[Christopher Tolkien]]'s research.<ref name="Charlton 2013">{{cite web |last=Charlton |first=Bruce |author-link=Bruce Charlton |title=Review of Paul Kocher's Master of Middle Earth |url=http://notionclubpapers.blogspot.com/2013/10/review-of-paul-kochers-master-of-middle.html |website=Notion Club Papers |access-date=25 January 2022 |date=18 October 2013}}</ref><ref name="Hyde 2002">{{cite journal |last=Hyde |first=Paul Nolan |title=The Moral Mythmaker: The Creative Theology of J. R. R. Tolkien |journal=Religious Educator |date=2002 |volume=3 |issue=3 |pages=151-166 |url=https://rsc.byu.edu/vol-3-no-3-2002/moral-mythmaker-creative-theology-j-r-r-tolkien}}</ref> 

In 1973, Patrick Grant, a scholar of Renaissance literature, offered a [[Psychological journeys of Middle-earth|psychological interpretation]] of ''The Lord of the Rings'', identifying similarities between the interactions of the characters and [[Jungian archetypes]]. He states that the Hero appears both in noble and powerful form as [[Aragorn]], and in childlike form as [[Frodo]], whose quest can be interpreted as a personal journey of [[individuation]]. They are opposed by the [[Ringwraith]]s. Frodo's [[Anima (Jung)|anima]] is the Elf-queen [[Galadriel]], who is opposed by the evil giant female spider [[Shelob]]. The Old Wise Man archetype is filled by the wizard [[Gandalf]], who is opposed by the corrupted wizard Saruman. Frodo's Shadow [[Gollum]] is, appropriately in Grant's view, also a male Hobbit, like Frodo. Aragorn has an Ideal Partner in [[Arwen]], but also a Negative Animus in [[Eowyn]], at least until she meets [[Faramir]] and chooses a happy union with him instead.<ref name="Grant 1973">{{Cite journal |last=Grant |first=Patrick |date=1973 |title=Tolkien: Archetype and Word |url=https://crosscurrents.org/tolkien.htm#_ednref9 |journal=[[Cross-Currents]] |issue=Winter 1973 |pages=365-380 |archive-date=2002-01-03 |access-date=2020-08-19 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20020103172131/https://crosscurrents.org/tolkien.htm#_ednref9 |url-status=dead }}</ref>

[[Richard C. West]] compiled an annotated checklist of Tolkien criticism in 1981.<ref>{{Cite book |last=West |first=Richard C. |title=Tolkien Criticism: An Annotated Checklist |date=1981 |publisher=[[Kent State University Press]] |isbn=978-0-87338-256-4 |location=Kent, Ohio |author-link=Richard C. West}}</ref> Serious study began to reach the broader community with Shippey's 1982 ''[[The Road to Middle-earth]]'' and [[Verlyn Flieger]]'s ''[[Splintered Light]]'' in 1983.<ref name="Timmons 1998">{{Cite journal |last=Timmons |first=Daniel |date=1998 |title=J.R.R. Tolkien: The "Monstrous" in the Mirror |journal=[[Journal of the Fantastic in the Arts]] |volume=9 |issue=3 (35) The Tolkien Issue |pages=229-246 |jstor=43308359}}</ref> To borrow a phrase from Flieger, academia had trouble "taking seriously a subject which had, until he wrote, been dismissed as unworthy of attention."<ref>{{Cite book |last=Flieger |first=Verlyn |title=Splintered Light: Logos and Language in Tolkien's World |title-link=Splintered Light |date=2002 |publisher=[[Kent State University Press]] |isbn=978-0-87338-744-6 |edition=2 |location=Kent, Ohio |page=38 |author-link=Verlyn Flieger}}</ref>

Tolkien's works have since become the subject of a substantial body of academic research, both as [[High fantasy|fantasy]] fiction and as an extended exercise in [[Constructed language|invented languages]].<ref name="Timmons 1998" /> In 1998, Daniel Timmons wrote in a dedicated issue of the ''[[Journal of the Fantastic in the Arts]]'' that scholars still disagreed about Tolkien's place in literature, but that those critical of it were a minority. He noted that Shippey had said that the "literary establishment" did not include Tolkien among the canon of academic texts, whereas [[Jane Chance]] "boldly declares that at last Tolkien 'is being studied as important in himself, as one of the world's greatest writers'".<ref name="Timmons 1998"/>

[[File:Tom Shippey by Gage Skidmore.jpg|thumb|upright|[[Tom Shippey]], like Tolkien an English [[philologist]], helped to pioneer the serious study of Tolkien's [[Middle-earth]] writings with his 1982 book ''[[The Road to Middle-earth]]''.<ref name="Schürer 2015">{{Cite web |last=Schürer |first=Norbert |date=13 November 2015 |title=Tolkien Criticism Today |url=https://lareviewofbooks.org/essay/tolkien-criticism-today/ |access-date=7 August 2020 |website=[[Los Angeles Review of Books]]}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last1=Baugher |first1=Luke |last2=Hillman |first2=Tom |last3=Nardi |first3=Dominic J. |title=Tolkien Criticism Unbound |url=http://mythgard.org/blog/2015/11/18/tolkien-criticism-unbound-a-response-to-norbert-schurer/ |access-date=8 January 2016 |website=Mythgard Institute}}</ref>]]

Alongside their analysis of Tolkien's work, scholars set about rebutting many of the literary critics' claims. Starting with his 1982 book ''[[The Road to Middle-earth]]'', Shippey pointed out that Muir's assertion that Tolkien's writing was non-adult, as the protagonists end with no pain, is not true of [[Frodo Baggins|Frodo]], who is permanently scarred and can no longer enjoy life in the Shire. Or again, he replies to [[Colin Manlove]]'s attack on Tolkien's "overworked cadences" and "monotonous pitch" and the suggestion that the ''[[Ubi sunt]]'' section of the Old English poem ''[[The Wanderer (Old English poem)|The Wanderer]]'' is "real [[elegy]]" unlike anything in Tolkien, with the observation that Tolkien's ''[[Lament of the Rohirrim]]'' is a paraphrase of just that section;<ref>{{cite book |last=Shippey |first=Tom |title=[[The Road to Middle-Earth]] |date=2005 |publisher=[[HarperCollins]] |isbn=978-0-261-10275-0 |edition=Third |pages=175, 201-203, 363-364 |author-link=Tom Shippey |orig-date=1982}}</ref> other scholars have praised Tolkien's poem.<ref name="Higgins 2014">{{Cite journal |last=Higgins |first=Andrew |year=2014 |title=Tolkien's Poetry (2013), edited by Julian Eilmann and Allan Turner |url=http://scholar.valpo.edu/journaloftolkienresearch/vol1/iss1/4 |journal=[[Journal of Tolkien Research]] |volume=1 |issue=1 |at=Article 4}}</ref> As a final example, he replies to the critic Mark Roberts's 1956 statement that ''The Lord of the Rings'' "is not moulded by some vision of things which is at the same time its ''[[wikt:raison d'etre|raison d'etre]]''";<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Roberts |first=Mark |date=1 October 1956 |title=Adventure in English |journal=Essays in Criticism |volume=6 |issue=4 |pages=450-459 |doi=10.1093/eic/VI.4.450}}</ref> he calls this one of the least perceptive comments ever made on Tolkien, stating that on the contrary the work "fits together ... on almost every level", with [[Interlacing in The Lord of the Rings|complex interlacing]], a [[Tolkien's ambiguity|consistent ambiguity]] about [[One Ring|the Ring]] and the nature of evil, and a consistent theory of the role of "chance" or "luck", all of which he explains in detail.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Shippey |first=Tom |title=[[J. R. R. Tolkien: Author of the Century]] |date=2001 |publisher=[[HarperCollins]] |isbn=978-0261-10401-3 |pages=156-157 and passim |author-link=Tom Shippey}}</ref>

The pace of scholarly publications on Tolkien increased dramatically in the early 2000s. The dedicated journal ''[[Tolkien Studies]]'' was founded in 2004; that same year, the scholar Neil D. Isaacs introduced an anthology of Tolkien criticism with the words "This collection assumes that argument about the value and power of ''The Lord of the Rings'' has been settled, certainly to the satisfaction of its vast, growing, persistent audience, but also of a considerable body of critical judgment".<ref name="Isaacs Zimbardo 2004">{{Cite book |last=Isaacs |first=Neil D. |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=GEWXQbASXZUC&pg=PA7 |chapter=[[Understanding The Lord of the Rings|On the Pleasures of Tolkien Criticism]] |title=Understanding the Lord of the Rings: The Best of Tolkien Criticism |publisher=[[Houghton Mifflin Harcourt]] |year=2004 |isbn=0-618-42253-6 |editor-last=Isaacs |editor-first=Neil D. |pages=7-8 |editor-last2=Zimbardo |editor-first2=Rose A. |editor-link2=Rose Zimbardo}}</ref> The open-access ''Journal of Tolkien Research'' began publication in 2014.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Journal of Tolkien Research |url=http://scholar.valpo.edu/journaloftolkienresearch/ |access-date=8 January 2016 |website=Journal of Tolkien Research |publisher=[[Valparaiso University]]}}</ref> Pressure to study Tolkien seriously came initially from fans rather than academics; the scholarly legitimacy of the field was still a subject of debate in 2015.<ref name="Schürer 2015">{{Cite web |last=Schürer |first=Norbert |date=13 November 2015 |title=Tolkien Criticism Today |url=https://lareviewofbooks.org/essay/tolkien-criticism-today/ |access-date=7 August 2020 |website=[[Los Angeles Review of Books]]}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last1=Baugher |first1=Luke |last2=Hillman |first2=Tom |last3=Nardi |first3=Dominic J. |title=Tolkien Criticism Unbound |url=http://mythgard.org/blog/2015/11/18/tolkien-criticism-unbound-a-response-to-norbert-schurer/ |access-date=8 January 2016 |website=[[Mythgard Institute]] |archive-date=22 November 2015 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151122014039/http://mythgard.org/blog/2015/11/18/tolkien-criticism-unbound-a-response-to-norbert-schurer/ |url-status=dead }}</ref>

Tolkien was strongly opposed to both [[Nazism]] and [[Communism]]; [[Hal Colebatch (author)|Hal Colebatch]] in the ''[[J. R. R. Tolkien Encyclopedia]]'' states that his views can be seen in what he considers to be the somewhat parodic "[[The Scouring of the Shire]]". Leftist critics have accordingly attacked Tolkien's social conservatism.<ref name="Colebatch 2013"/> [[E. P. Thompson]] blames the cold warrior mentality on "too much early reading of ''The Lord of the Rings''".<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Thompson |first=E. P. |date=24 January 1981 |title=America's Europe: A Hobbit among Gandalfs |journal=[[The Nation (journal)|The Nation]] |pages=68-72}}</ref> Other [[Marxist literary criticism|Marxist critics]], however, have been more positive towards Tolkien. While criticizing the politics embedded in ''The Lord of the Rings'',<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Mieville |first=China |author-link=China Miéville |title=Tolkien - Middle Earth Meets Middle England |url=http://www.socialistreview.org.uk/article.php?articlenumber=7813 |journal=[[Socialist Review]] |issue=January 2002 |access-date=2011-11-13 |archive-date=2010-10-31 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101031000458/http://www.socialistreview.org.uk/article.php?articlenumber=7813 |url-status=dead }}</ref> [[China Miéville]] admires Tolkien's creative use of [[Norse mythology]], [[tragedy]], [[monster]]s, and [[Worldbuilding|subcreation]], as well as his criticism of [[allegory]].<ref>{{Cite web |last=Mieville |first=China |author-link=China Miéville |date=15 June 2009 |title=There and Back Again: Five Reasons Tolkien Rocks |url=http://www.omnivoracious.com/2009/06/there-and-back-again-five-reasons-tolkien-rocks.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090618115637/http://www.omnivoracious.com/2009/06/there-and-back-again-five-reasons-tolkien-rocks.html |archive-date=18 June 2009 |website=Omnivoracious}}</ref>

=== Literary re-evaluation ===
{{see|Influences on Tolkien|Themes of The Lord of the Rings}}

With the understanding that Tolkien was worth studying, scholars, authors, and critics began to re-evaluate his Middle-earth writings as literature. The humanities scholar [[Brian Rosebury]] stated in 2003 that ''The Lord of the Rings'' is both a quest&nbsp;- a story with a goal, to destroy the Ring&nbsp;- and a journey, an expansive tour of Middle-earth through a series of tableaux that filled readers with delight; and the two supported each other.<ref name="Rosebury 2003" /> Rosebury considered why ''The Lord of the Rings'' has attracted so much literary hostility, and re-evaluated it as a literary work. He noted that many critics have stated that it is not a novel and that some have proposed a medieval genre like "romance" or "[[wikt:epic|epic]]". He cited Shippey's "more subtl[e]" suggestion that "Tolkien set himself to write a romance for an audience brought up on novels", noting that Tolkien did occasionally call the work a romance but usually called it a tale, a story, or a history.<ref name="Rosebury 2003"/> Shippey argued that the work aims at [[Northrop Frye]]'s "heroic romance" [[Anatomy of Criticism#mode|mode]], only one level below "myth", but descending to "low mimesis" with the much less serious hobbits, who serve to deflect the modern reader's scepticism of the higher reaches of medieval-style romance.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Shippey |first=Tom |title=The Road to Middle-Earth |date=2005 |publisher=[[HarperCollins]] |isbn=978-0-261-10275-0 |edition=Third |pages=237-249 |author-link=Tom Shippey |orig-date=1982}}</ref>

[[File:Rosebury's analysis of Lord of the Rings.svg|thumb|upright=1.5|Diagram of [[Brian Rosebury]]'s analysis of ''The Lord of the Rings'' as a combined quest (to destroy [[One Ring|the Ring]]) and journey (as a series of Tableaux of places in [[Middle-earth]]); the two support each other, and must interlock tightly to do so.<ref name="Rosebury 2003"/>]]

Rosebury noted that much of the work, especially Book 1, is largely descriptive rather than plot-based; it focuses mainly on Middle-earth itself, taking a journey through a series of tableaux - in [[the Shire]], in the [[Old Forest]], with [[Tom Bombadil]], and so on. He states that "The circumstantial expansiveness of Middle-earth itself is central to the work's aesthetic power".<!--pp. 12-13--> Alongside this slow descriptiveness is the quest to destroy the Ring, a unifying plotline. The Ring needs to be destroyed to save Middle-earth itself from destruction or domination by Sauron. Hence, Rosebury argued, the book does have a single focus: Middle-earth itself. The work builds up Middle-earth as a place that readers come to love, shows that it is under dire threat, and - with the destruction of the Ring - provides the "[[eucatastrophe]]" for a happy ending. That makes the work "comedic" rather than "tragic<!--not the popular meaning of this word-->", in classical terms; but it also embodies the inevitability of loss, like the elves, hobbits, and the rest [[Decline and fall in Middle-earth|decline and fade]]. Even the least novelistic parts of the work, the chronicles, narratives, and essays of the appendices, help to build a consistent image of Middle-earth. The work is thus, Rosebury asserted, very tightly constructed, the expansiveness and plot fitting together exactly.<ref name="Rosebury 2003">{{Cite book |last=Rosebury |first=Brian |title=Tolkien: A Cultural Phenomenon |title-link=Tolkien: A Cultural Phenomenon |date=2003 |publisher=[[Palgrave Macmillan|Palgrave]] |isbn=978-1403-91263-3 |pages=1-3, 12-13, 25-34, 41, 57 |author-link=Brian Rosebury |orig-date=1992<!--2 new chapters-->}}</ref>

In ''[[Mallorn (journal)|Mallorn]]'' in 2004, the Tolkien scholar Caroline Galwey wrote the ironically-titled "Reasons for 'not' Liking Tolkien", inverting Turner's "Reasons for Liking Tolkien" and attacking her position, along with Edwin Muir's. In her view, "we cannot understand Tolkien-haters properly unless we go beyond their arguments to the things they do not say."<ref name="Galwey 2004">{{cite journal |last=Galwey |first=Caroline |title=Reasons for "not" liking Tolkien |journal=[[Mallorn (journal)|Mallorn: The Journal of the Tolkien Society]] |issue=42 |date=August 2004 |pages=5-10 |jstor=45320500 }}</ref> Those things, she argues, include the "greatest strength" of ''The Lord of the Rings'', that "in sensibility it is a (capital-R) Romantic work". In her view, Turner is "apparently so embarrassed by [Tolkien's Romanticism] that she won't even name it or admit that it has a pedigree."<ref name="Galwey 2004"/> Galway writes, too, that Tolkien-haters have an "existential fear" of Tolkien's happiness: they cannot accept that "Joy, wonder, reverence, the [[Sublime (literary)|Sublime]]" do mean something, that they stand alongside the world's suffering and evil, "undiminished by them, as a fact in this world."<ref name="Galwey 2004"/>

In 2013, the fantasy author and humorist [[Terry Pratchett]] used a mountain theme to praise Tolkien, likening Tolkien to [[Mount Fuji]], and writing that any other fantasy author "either has made a deliberate decision against the mountain, which is interesting in itself, or is in fact [[Standing on the shoulders of giants|standing on [it]]]."<ref name="Pratchett 2013">{{Cite book |last=Pratchett |first=Terry |title=A Slip of the Keyboard: Collected Non-fiction |publisher=[[Doubleday (publisher)|Doubleday]] |year=2013 |isbn=978-0-85752-122-4 |page=86 |oclc=856191939 |author-link=Terry Pratchett}}</ref> In 2016, the British literary critic and poet [[Roz Kaveney]] reviewed five books about Tolkien in ''[[The Times Literary Supplement]]''. She recorded that in 1991 she had said of ''The Lord of the Rings'' that it was worth "intelligent reading but not passionate attention",<ref name="Kaveney 2016">{{Cite news |last=Kaveney |first=Roz |date=24 February 2016 |title=An English mythology |work=[[The Times Literary Supplement]] |url=https://www.the-tls.co.uk/articles/an-english-mythology/ |access-date=6 August 2020}}</ref> and accepted that she had "underestimated the extent to which it would gain added popularity and cultural lustre from [[Peter Jackson]]'s film adaptations".<ref name="Kaveney 2016" /> As Pratchett had done, she used a mountain metaphor, alluding to [[Basil Bunting]]'s poem about [[Ezra Pound]]'s ''Cantos'',<ref>{{Cite web |last=Pound |first=Ezra |author-link=Ezra Pound |title=On The Fly-Leaf Of Pound's Cantos |website=AllPoetry.com |url=https://allpoetry.com/On-The-Fly-Leaf-Of-Pound%27s-Cantos |access-date=8 August 2020}}</ref> with the words "Tolkien's books have become [[Alps]] and we will wait in vain for them to crumble."<ref name="Kaveney 2016" /> Kaveney called Tolkien's works "[[Thick description|Thick Texts]]", books that are best read with some knowledge of his Middle-earth framework rather than as "single artworks". She accepted that he was a complicated figure, a scholar, a war survivor, a skilful writer of "light verse", a literary theorist, and a member of "[[The Inklings|a coterie of other influential thinkers]]". Further, she stated that [[Tolkien and the modernists|he had much in common with modernist writers]] like [[T. S. Eliot]]. She suggested that ''The Lord of the Rings'' is "a good, intelligent, influential and popular book", but perhaps not, as some of his "idolators" would have it, "a transcendent literary masterpiece".<ref name="Kaveney 2016" />

Andrew Higgins, reviewing the 2014 volume ''[[A Companion to J. R. R. Tolkien]]'', welcomed the "eminent line-up" of the authors of its 36 articles (naming in particular Shippey, [[Verlyn Flieger]], [[Dimitra Fimi]], [[John D. Rateliff]] and [[Gergely Nagy (scholar)|Gergely Nagy]]). He called it "joyous indeed that after many years of polite (and not so polite) disdain and dismissal by establishment 'academics' and the 'cultural [[intelligentsia]]{{' "}} that Tolkien had reached the "academic pantheon" of [[Blackwell's|Blackwell]] Companions. Higgins applauded the volume's editor, [[Stuart D. Lee]], for "the overall thematic structuring of this volume, which offers a progressive profile of Tolkien the man, the student, and scholar, and the mythopoeist".<ref name="Higgins 2015">{{Cite journal |last=Higgins |first=Andrew |year=2015 |title=[Review:] A Companion to J. R. R. Tolkien, ed. Stuart D. Lee |url=http://scholar.valpo.edu/journaloftolkienresearch/vol2/iss1/2 |journal=[[Journal of Tolkien Research]] |volume=2 |issue=1 |at=Article 2}}</ref> Curry, writing in the ''Companion''<!--just introduced-->, stated that attempts at a balanced response, finding a positive critic for each negative one, as Daniel Timmons had done,<ref name="Timmons 1998"/> was "admirably irenic [peaceful] but misleading"<ref name="Curry 2020">{{cite book |last=Curry |first=Patrick |author-link=Patrick Curry |chapter=The Critical Response to Tolkien's Fiction |editor-last=Lee |editor-first=Stuart D. |editor-link=Stuart D. Lee |title=A Companion to J. R. R. Tolkien |date=2020 |orig-date=2014 |publisher=[[Wiley Blackwell]] |isbn=978-1-11965-602-9 |pages=369-388 |url=http://www.patrickcurry.co.uk/papers/THE%20CRITICAL%20RESPONSE%20TO%20TOLKIEN.pdf<!--author's own page-->}}</ref> as this failed to address the reasons for the hostility. Curry noted that the attacks on Tolkien began when ''The Lord of the Rings'' appeared; increased when the work became "spectacular[ly] successful"<ref name="Curry 2020"/> from 1965; and revived when readers' polls by [[Waterstones]] and [[BBC Radio 4]] acclaimed the work in 1996-1998, and then again when Peter Jackson's film trilogy came out in 2001-2003. He cited Shippey's remark that the hostile critics Philip Toynbee and Edmund Wilson revealed "gross inconsistency between their self-professed critical ideals and their practice when they encounter Tolkien",<ref name="Curry 2020"/> adding that Fred Inglis had called Tolkien a fascist and a practitioner of "'country-based fantasy' that is 'suburban' and 'half-educated".<ref name="Curry 2020"/> Curry states that these criticisms are not simply demonstrably mistaken, but "rather how ''very'' (his emphasis) mistaken they are, and how consistently. That suggests that there is (as Marxists like to say) a structural or systematic bias at work".<ref name="Curry 2020"/> He noted that Shippey's 1982 ''The Road to Middle-earth'' and then [[Verlyn Flieger]]'s 1983 ''[[Splintered Light]]'' had slowly begun to reduce the hostility.<ref name="Curry 2020"/> That did not prevent Jenny Turner from repeating "some of her predecessors' elementary mistakes"; Curry wrote that she seemed to fail to grasp "two of the most important things about art, literary or otherwise: that reality is (also) ineluctably fictional, and that fiction and its referents are (also) unavoidably real",<ref name="Curry 2020"/> pointing out that [[metaphor]] is unavoidable in language.<ref name="Curry 2020"/> 

Summing up the history of attacks, Curry identified two consistent features: "a visceral hostility and emotional animus, and a plethora of mistakes showing that the books had not been read closely".<ref name="Curry 2020"/> In his view, these derived from the critics' feeling that Tolkien threatened their "dominant ideology", [[modernism]]. Tolkien is, he wrote, [[Tolkien and the modernists|modern but not modernist]], at least as well-educated as the critics (another thing that made them feel threatened), and not [[irony|ironic]] (especially about his writing). ''The Lord of the Rings'' is equally "a story told by a master story-teller; a story inspired by [[philology]]; a [[Christianity in the Lord of the Rings|story suffused with Catholic values]]; and a mythic (or [[Mythopoeia|mythopoeic]]) story with a [[Paganism in Middle-earth|North European pagan inflection]]". In other words, Tolkien was about as anti-modernist as possible. Curry concluded by noting that newer authors including [[China Miéville]], [[Junot Díaz|Junot Diaz]], and [[Michael Chabon]], and the critics [[Anthony Lane]] in ''[[The New Yorker]]'' and Andrew O'Hehir in ''[[Salon (magazine)|Salon]]'' were taking a more open attitude, and cited the work's first publisher, [[Rayner Unwin]]'s "pithy and accurate" assessment of it: "a very great book in its own curious way".<ref name="Curry 2020"/>
