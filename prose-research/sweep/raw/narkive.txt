 A recent thread on narrative voice in LotR (it is actually a subthread
of the Flotsam and Jetsam CotW thread) made me more alert to this in the
book, and I spotted a few examples:
1) Not too interesting: The sub-title of the Red Book reinforces the
'internal story' deceit: "THE DOWNFALL OF THE LORD OF THE RINGS AND THE
RETURN OF THE KING (as seen by the Little People; being the memoirs of
Bilbo and Frodo of the Shire, supplemented by the accounts of their
friends and the learning of the Wise.)"
So you can get any of Bilbo ('The Hobbit' and early LotR), Frodo, Sam,
and the other members of the Fellowship, supplementing the story, plus
the rather more cryptic: 'the learning of the Wise'.
2) A bit more interesting: "Of course, he [Sam] also firmly held the
incompatible belief that Mr. Frodo was the wisest person in the world
(with the possible exception of Old Mr. Bilbo and of Gandalf). Gollum in
his own way, and with much more excuse as his acquaintance was much
briefer, may have made a similar mistake, confusing kindness and
blindness. At any rate this speech abashed and terrified him." (The
Black Gate is Closed)
Which narrative voice is this? It can't be Sam or Frodo. It seems to be
a later annotator adding in some opinion, presumably someone who knew
Sam well enough to say this. It sounds almost like one of Tolkien's
letters!
Christopher

Well . . .
It seems that I have to first convince the wife that I should have
/Tolkien's Legendarium/. Then I have to order it, wait for it and read
the essay by Paul Edmund Thomas -- only then can I proceed.
Of course that's an excuse, but I'll use it anyway ;-)
My intention is to separate different 'dimensions' of the narration,
and look at them individually before trying to tie it up.
The 'dimensions' I've identified so far are:
1) The narrative layers (or voices), meaning the steps the story goes
through from the first hand witness to the event and until Tolkien as
the translator. Tolkien as omniscient author and sub-creator probably
has to be added to that list as well.
2) Focus: the viewpoint of a passage. This involves the first hand
witness, but the story doesn't always focus on the actions of the
actual witness -- for instance when Gimli and Legolas recounts their
journey from Dunharrow to Minas Tirith, they act as witnesses, but the
focus of their account is Aragorn. Both of these aspects are, I
believe, important. Another aspect of the focus is 'distance' --
occasionally when the witness and the focus character coincide, we are
'in the head' of the witness, but at other times, there is a distance,
as if the chronicler (another of my layers) were paraphrasing the
witness.
3) Knowledge. This is, of course, tied to the narrative layer. Each
step in the delivery of the story have been able to add knowledge, but
it isn't always that a posteriori knowledge is visible in the story.
4) Style. Somewhat less tangible to discuss. With respect to dialogue,
Tolkien describes this himself in the appendices -- how the various
dialects of westron are translated into English etc. I could never hope
of doing a better job than his (though I can of course praise his work
lavishly). There are, however, other stylistic differences that are
connected to language. Occasionally the narration is 'high' or 'epic'
while at other times it is far more down to earth. This creates
different moods, and should be included.
A lot of this isn't new or particularly ingenious, but it would be
necessary for me to organise my thoughts ;-)
The interesting part is the attempt to look at the interplay between
the various dimensions. For instance I think that our perception of the
various personalities of the characters also depend on their narrative
voice -- when we have passages where e.g. Merry is the witness, we
identify these (more or less conciously), and the passages where the
chronicler stays close to the account of the witness, this helps our
understanding of that person.
So, that's the direction in which I'm currently planning. All of it
needs to be illustrated with examples, of course, and there's a lot of
work to do.
