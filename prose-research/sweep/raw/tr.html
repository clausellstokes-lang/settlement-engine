After a discussion on Discord about AI use in fic writing and how to spot it, I thought I would put together a thread on the topic where people can share anecdotes, show comparisons of AI-generated vs human-generated writing, and generally discuss the problem.

Here are some resources I've found helpful for training myself to detect AI writing in fiction (fan and otherwise):

- Wikipedia's "Signs of AI Writing" page is pretty comprehensive (see especially the "Language and Grammar" section); while many of the AI tells listed here to not apply to fanfiction--if a fanfic references Wikipedia's Manual of Style, I have larger questions--many of them do, and taking a look through here can give a sense of how to identify AI writing beyond surface-level language anomalies like "they use a lot of em-dashes." The article includes many examples of AI-generated text and comments. The talk page also includes some interesting and recent discussion on the topic, and there's also a quiz to test your skills at identifying AI-generated Wikipedia articles, if you're interested in testing your detection knowledge in practice.

- Bona Books' The Machines are Coming for your Masthead is a report from a small press about how they accepted (and in one case even paid for) multiple short stories that they believe were AI-generated. This is less useful for describing common prose tells and is more about all the tells that aren't in the text, how the publisher was caught off-guard, and how they intend to defend against AI submissions in the future.

- Rose-Colored Glasses (note: NSFW) is an AI-generated, lightly human-edited Heated Rivalry erotica fanfic that was generated to provide an example of common AI tells. The first chapter is the lightly-edited output itself, with a large author's note at the bottom summarizing common AI tells. The second chapter is the output marked up with changes the author made and commentary both on their editing choices and the AI tells in the text. The third chapter (no NSFW content) is a brief discussion of the results of running AI-generated pieces of the fic vs human-written prose through various AI detectors. The fourth chapter (no NSFW content) briefly mentions an HTML artifact that can unambiguously identify some Claude-generated fics posted to AO3.

Claude starts off strong--it doesn't take long for the AI phrasing to become apparent. Personally, reading this, my suspicions about it being AI-generated are at a level of a faint "hmmm" until I get to the "He had a voice like a waiting room" line, at which point it's like BZZZT, GAME OVER. The rule-of-three + simile combined with the bizarre metaphor (his voice was designed to contain things??) is a screaming red flag.

And here's the author's note going over AI tells:

This fanwork, tagged "meta", is intended as an educational document to familiarize readers with AI output. LLMs are perfectly capable of generating Heated Rivalry fanfiction. There are tells, but many are subtle. Chapter 2 will expand on this further, but let me highlight several obvious issues:

  • Use of IRL Team Names: LLMs tend to use the names of IRL teams instead of names associated with the actual teams in either the GCU or the show Heated Rivalry. Use of Canadiens, Habs, Bruins, or Rangers in a work set in HR is indicative of AI use. It is not a sure sign, especially if an author has a Hockey RPF background. I did not change any names: the way they appear is the way Claude generated them.
  • Character Names & Numbers: Claude tends to use the same names over and over, and/or to forget or confuse canon names. Some names commonly appearing in Claude generated creative writing include Marcus, Marcus Webb, Sarah Chen, the last name Chen generally, Thorne, Volkov, Thompson, etc. You can find lists of these names by searching Google and Reddit. A one-off appearance is not dispositive, but the complete name "Marcus Webb" or "Sarah Chen" is an extremely good sign that Claude has been used. Claude also generates "47" for many random numbers—times will sometimes end with :47, things will take 47 minutes, or perhaps cost $47,000. If you find yourself noticing that an author really seems to like the number 47, that author might be Claude. One instance doesn't make it AI—but repeated, consistent usage alongside other AI tells can definitely be an indicator.
  • Incorrect canon details: Many authors have made small mistakes as to timelines or other details. This is normal. Authors may even change canon to support their plot. But if a fic includes factually wrong inconsistencies within the world it sets up, this is suspicious. For instance, if Shane is in his apartment in Montreal, he should not suddenly be in Calgary within the same scene, without traveling. Characters should not suddenly become older or younger (without a time skip or birthday), change eye colors, names, or any other feature. Unexplained inconsistencies can be a sign that a text was generated by an LLM.
  • Repetition within scenes or, occasionally, across a work: LLMs, unable to "think" about what they create, will sometimes generate scenes where a character does something, and then, shortly after, does the same thing—taking underwear off, for instance. Or it may use extremely similar phrasing in multiple places in the same fic. Humans do both of these things when they write–many an author has found a "third arm" in a sex scene while editing, for instance—so this is best evaluated alongside other tells. By similar phrasing, I don't mean the word "said" or any generic phrasing, but instead unique descriptions repeated in different spots, and not for artistic effect.
  • Claude's "voice": This is best identified through experience. Claude generates scenes that often have similar structures, phrases, tics, and word choices. Many Claude-generated fics will share the same descriptions of gameplay or sensory details. Claude loves to use figurative language (sometimes nonsensically), describe smells, and overuse repetition. For instance, these lines are really Claude-y:
    • "The conversation moved the way it did at this table—easy, interrupted by small crises, resuming without drama."
    • "Rozanov looked at him—that direct, unhurried way he had, the way that didn't apologize for itself."
    • "Not unkind. Not impatient. Just—waiting."
    • "The exam room was dim and smelled clinical but not unpleasant."
    • "The way Rozanov looked at him like Shane was legible—like he read Shane the way Shane read ice, patterns and gaps and what was coming next."
    • "The house had the particular energy of a place where small people lived: a tower of board books threatening collapse by the shoe rack, a plastic stegosaurus on the third stair, a smell of something good coming from the kitchen underneath the baby-soap smell that was just always there now."
  • Pacing: This is the most difficult element to actually point to, because pacing is a hard skill for many people to learn. Still, in AI text, especially longer texts, you will sometimes notice that everything has the exact same narrative pacing and emotional weight. Scenes drag on for no apparent reason and details serve no function. Even on the sentence and paragraph level, AI will often say in 1000 words what would be more effectively said in 500.
All these signs must be considered holistically. Besides prompts left in the text, no single thing can "prove" AI use. But if a work shows multiple signs and has Claude's generic voice, it is very possible that the work is AI. Edited to add: AI generated fic is even harder to detect in complete AUs, e.g., if neither Shane nor Ilya are hockey players. That's because the reader is more forgiving about "incorrect" canon details! In those cases, the only "tells" are in the prose itself.

Finally, here's an excerpt from Shy Girl, a self-published horror novel that was later picked up for traditional publication and even later removed from shelves due to accusations of AI use. Based on the AI tells discussed above, can you spot why people suspected this novel of being AI-generated?

I wear a pink dress, the kind that promises softness and delivers none. Its tulle is brittle and sharp, brushing against my fur like a thousand tiny teeth, a cruel lover that bites with every move. Every scratch keeps me in place, a reminder of what I am: a pet, a thing shaped for looking, for praise, for command. The bows on my pigtails pull too tight, yanking the skin and stretching my head into something neat, into something pleasing, a quiet violence made beautiful. White socks climb my legs, their frills delicate, a whisper of innocence over the bruises beneath, the ones he says shouldn’t happen if the socks are there—but they always do.

The ache is low and rhythmic, a second heartbeat in my ribs, steady and insistent, the kind of pain you get used to until it becomes part of you. Then the door bursts open, and he enters like a storm, dragging the sour stink of liquor behind him, his presence filling the room and turning the pastel air brittle. In his hands is a cake, gleaming, its pink frosting too smooth, like plastic dipped in sugar, like something that belongs on a screen, too perfect to hold.

A Different Kind of Team

The lab smelled like antiseptic and old paper, which Nox had always found strange for a place that gave out Pokémon — you'd think it would smell like grass, or fur, or something alive. Instead it smelled like the inside of a filing cabinet, and the fluorescent lights hummed just loud enough to notice if you were nervous enough to be listening for something to notice.

He was nervous enough.

"You've had that same face on since breakfast," said Wren, leaning against the doorframe with her arms crossed and the particular smugness of an older cousin who'd already been through this exact morning three years ago. "Anyone would think you were about to fail a test instead of get your first Pokémon."

"I'm not nervous." He was absolutely nervous. He'd redone the straps on his bag four times.

"Right." She didn't push it, which he appreciated more than he'd ever say out loud. Wren had a way of knowing which things to let go of.

He'd had the plan for two years. Not a rough idea, not a vague hope — an actual plan, written and rewritten in the back of three different notebooks, cross-referenced against battle records and type charts and every scrap of regional history he could get his hands on. A Luxray at the center of it, because Luxrays could see through anything, through walls, through lies, through the kind of fog that swallowed the old stories whole — and because if you were going to build a team around finding something ancient and half-forgotten, you wanted a partner who could actually see it when the rest of the world walked right past.

He knew exactly what a Luxray becoming his starter would mean. He'd built four contingency branches off that single point.

He had built zero contingency branches off anything else.

"Next," called the assistant at the counter, and Nox realized with a lurch that the line had moved and it was, apparently, his turn to walk up and become a trainer.

The case on the counter held three Poké Balls, same as it always did, same as it had for every kid in every town for as long as there'd been a starting line to stand at. The assistant — young, tired-looking, the kind of tired that came from doing the same speech every week — gestured at the case without much ceremony.

"Take your pick. Chikorita, Cyndaquil, or—" she paused, glancing down at a clipboard, and something crossed her face that Nox didn't like the shape of. "Actually, hang on."

"Hang on?"

"We had a mix-up with the regional order. Chikorita and Cyndaquil are already claimed for today—" she nodded at two kids near the door, clutching their new Poké Balls like trophies "—and the replacement shipment hasn't come in yet. I've got one starter left in the building, but it's not on the usual list."

Nox felt something in his chest do a complicated, hopeful thing it had no business doing. "What is it?"

The assistant disappeared into the back and came out holding a single Poké Ball, looking almost apologetic about it. "It's a Zubat. One of the rehab program's — orphaned litter from a cave collapse near Mt. Mortar, all of them placed except this one. It didn't take well to the other trainers who came through." She set it on the counter like she was setting down bad news gently. "You don't have to take it. I can put your name on the list for the next regional shipment, might be a week, maybe two—"

Nox looked at the ball. Small, ordinary, nothing about it suggesting the electric-eyed lightning he'd spent two years' worth of notebook pages planning around.

Behind him, Wren didn't say anything, which was its own kind of loud.

He thought about the plan. Four contingency branches, all of them built on a foundation he suddenly didn't have. He thought about the stories he'd read — really read, not skimmed, the ones about trainers who found their legendary partners not because they'd built the perfect team, but because something else had happened first, some detour nobody wrote into the official record. He thought, unwillingly, about a name he never said out loud in public, and what that name's whole legacy had been built on: control, order, forcing the world into the shape you'd already decided it should take.

Respect, not command, he reminded himself. That was the whole point.

"I'll take the Zubat," he said, before he could talk himself out of it.

The assistant blinked, clearly not expecting that answer. "You sure? It's got a bit of an attitude. Bit into the last trainer's hand twice out of pure spite before he gave up on it."

"I'm sure."

Common Sense


Dying, it turned out, was a lot less dramatic than every isekai manga had led her to believe.

One second Asuka was biking home from her shift with a bag of convenience store onigiri swinging off the handlebars, and the next she was staring up at a sky that was very obviously the wrong color — too saturated, too many suns'-worth of gold in it — lying flat on her back in a field of grass that rustled even though there was no wind.

"Okay," she said out loud, to nobody. "Okay, sure. This is happening."

She sat up. The grass wasn't grass, not exactly — it had a faint blue undertone at the roots, like someone had rendered it at slightly the wrong saturation, and when she brushed her hand across it, it gave off the faintest static crackle against her palm. Actual, physical static. Not a sound effect. Not a screen shader. A real, tactile zzt against her skin, like she'd dragged her socks across carpet.

That was the first thing that told her this wasn't a game anymore, even though every instinct in her screamed that it should be. Grass-type terrain building up ambient charge wasn't something she remembered from any game manual. It was just... true. A fact of the world, the way "grass is green" was a fact of her old one.

The second thing was the Rattata.

It came bursting out of the underbrush at a full sprint, and her brain — twenty-six years of gaming instinct deep — immediately slotted it into the mental category of early-route trash mob, catch three and move on. Except this one had a jagged scar over one eye and teeth that were very clearly built for tearing, not for the cute chomping animation she remembered, and it was being chased by something considerably larger that she didn't get a good look at before both of them crashed through the grass and vanished, leaving nothing behind but a snapped stalk and the smell of ozone.

Asuka sat very still for a long moment.

"Right," she said. "Not a game."


She figured out the broad strokes over the next several hours, mostly by walking, mostly by not dying, and partly by an old man in the first town she stumbled into who took one look at her games-brand backpack — somehow, impossibly, still with her, still full of her actual human world snacks — and decided the kindest thing to do was hand her a can of something warm and not ask questions.

The world worked like the games. Familiar shapes, familiar names, a region that looked suspiciously like Kanto if Kanto had been left to marinate in something closer to reality for a hundred years. But it wasn't the games. It had never been the games. The games, she was starting to suspect, were what happened when someone took a genuinely complicated, dangerous, alive world and boiled it down into something a ten-year-old could parse on a rented cartridge.

Moves weren't animations. They were physics wearing a costume.

She learned this properly a week later, crouched behind a fence with a retired trainer named Ochi who'd taken pity on the weird girl with no home region and no papers, watching a Geodude spar with a younger trainer's Machop in a scrap lot behind Ochi's house.

Not spite at the world, exactly. Grounded


The lab had a strict no-Pokémon-over-waist-height policy, which meant Flygon spent most days folded awkwardly outside the window, wings tucked, watching Reiko work through a magnifying lens like the answer to something was hiding in the glass.

It hadn't always been like this. There'd been a time — not long ago, not long enough to have stopped mattering — when Reiko's hands were steady on a Poké Ball instead of a pipette, and the horizon meant something other than the next paper due.

Flygon didn't move. It had learned, over the quiet stretch of months since the gym challenge got shelved — paused, Reiko always corrected, just paused — that an hour from her meant three, and three meant the sun would be down before she noticed, and by then the particular restlessness sitting under Flygon's scales would have curdled into something closer to grief.

It dreamed, some nights, of a shape it had never worn and had no right to want. A silhouette half-remembered from some fragment of old research Reiko had once left open on her desk — spines longer, wings wider, something crackling and half-formed around the edges like a change too big for the body holding it. It didn't have a name for the feeling. It only knew that when it pictured itself finishing that shape, actually becoming it, something in its chest sang with a certainty it never felt anywhere else.

The trouble was, there was no stone for it. There had never been a stone for it. Reiko had checked — quietly, at first, humoring what she assumed was some quirk of Flygon's temperament, and then more thoroughly, cross-referencing every mega evolution registry the region's League had on file, coming up empty every single time.

"It's not that I don't believe in you," she'd told it once, months ago, crouched at its eye level with the kind of gentle, careful honesty she used for things she didn't want to be right about. "I just don't think it's real. I think you want something that doesn't exist yet."

Flygon had said nothing, because it couldn't, not in words she'd understand. It had only looked at her with an expression she didn't have a name for either, and gone back to watching the window.

It didn't know how to tell her that doesn't exist yet and isn't real were not, in fact, the same sentence.

Falkner's gym was closed on Wednesdays, which meant Wednesdays were, by long-standing and entirely unofficial tradition, the day half of Johto's gym leaders ended up crowded into Bugsy's kitchen arguing about tea.

"It tastes the same either way," Whitney said, which was, as far as Falkner was concerned, the single most controversial sentence uttered in that kitchen all month.

Morty had claimed the windowsill, which he did every week, sitting with his knees drawn up and a mug balanced on one knee, watching the argument unfold with the detached amusement of someone who could apparently sense exactly how it would end and didn't feel the need to intervene. "You're both wrong. The good stuff's in Jasmine's cabinet and none of you have asked her yet."

"I have literally offered," Jasmine said, not looking up from the lighthouse maintenance log she'd brought along out of habit, because Jasmine did not know how to have a day off that didn't involve at least one piece of paperwork.

The kitchen was loud in the specific way that only happened when people who spent all week being in charge — of gyms, of towns, of a steady stream of ten-year-olds with more ambition than sense — got to spend one day not being in charge of anything except a teapot. Falkner liked Wednesdays for exactly that reason. Nobody expected him to have answers on Wednesdays.

Which was, of course, when the knock came.

Not at the door — through the window, from a very out-of-breath Pidgeot that Falkner recognized before its rider had even landed.

"That's Gold's Pidgeot," he said, already standing.

Bugsy craned around the doorframe just in time to see a fifteen-year-old half-fall off the bird's back, soaked to the skin, cap missing, one sleeve torn clean at the shoulder. He didn't look hurt, exactly. He looked like someone who'd spent the last six hours being extremely stubborn about something dangerous and had only just now run out of stubbornness to spend.

"Please tell me one of you has a working phone," Gold said. "Mine's ruined and Crystal's gonna kill me if she finds out I tried the whirlpool cave alone before telling anyone, and also I think I sprained something, and also—" He looked, for the first time since landing, genuinely uncertain of himself. "Also I don't actually know whose door I landed at. Sorry. Pidgeot picked."

"Pidgeot picked well," Morty said, already off the windowsill.

What followed was less an emergency and more a full reorganization of the household, conducted with the easy, practiced coordination of people who'd clearly done this before — for Gold, or for some other reckless kid with a Pidgeot and more nerve than plan. Jasmine had a first aid kit out before Falkner had even finished asking if anyone had one. Bugsy commandeered the phone to call the whirlpool cave rangers so nobody else went looking for whatever Gold had apparently found. Whitney, with startling gentleness for someone who'd been arguing about tea infusion times ninety seconds earlier, sat Gold down and started working his soaked jacket off his shoulders before the shivering got worse.

"You're not in trouble," she said, when his face did the thing where he braced for a lecture. "You're an idiot, but you're not in trouble. Those are different categories."

"That's basically what Crystal says," Gold muttered.

"Crystal's usually right." Whitney wrapped a towel around him before he could argue and pointed, with total authority, at the nearest chair. "Sit. Drink something warm. Bugsy, is that infusion done being not boiled yet or are we still doing philosophy over here?"

"It's done," Bugsy said, aiming a look at Whitney that she ignored entirely.

Falkner crouched down at Gold's eye level while the others worked, the way he'd once crouched down for a much younger, much more overwhelmed kid on his very first gym challenge, years before any of this — the kitchen, the Wednesdays, the reflex of someone's kid is in trouble, move — had become second nature.

"Tell me what happened," he said. Not sharp. Not the voice of a Leader assessing a challenger. Just the voice of someone who'd clearly be listening for a while, and had nowhere else he needed to be.

Gold, teeth still chattering slightly under the towel, started talking. And across the kitchen, without anyone quite deciding to, the day quietly reorganized itself around making sure he was alright — which was, Falkner privately thought, probably the truest thing any of them did all week, work or not.

"Only partially concealed"? What kind of coat can conceal breadth? This is a common AI tic - it understands that it's hot to suggest that an attractive trait can't fully be concealed, but its model of physical reality is wonky (not to mention the safety guardrails) so it struggles to find work-friendly ways to describe people as attractive. But you can't really conceal breadth, in any body type, because clothes are always going to mark the outline of the body as far as they go. Even if he's wearing the baggiest coat of all time, it will naturally fall off the edge of his shoulders and mark where his shoulders end.

Throwing in my two cents as someone who sometimes has to field writing submissions for work: I become suspicious when the way an author talks about their text doesn’t match the skill level of the text.

For example, I recently encountered a young poet who wrote about the work in short, vague statements. Meanwhile the poems themselves were super long and included language that was shockingly powerful and specific. They didn’t sound like the same person.

Fic often has author’s notes or is tied to forum and Discord communities where you can see how the writer talks in the wild. (Like, try asking Persephone about camping and behold how extremely aligned with Broken Things that conversation becomes, hahaha.)

People can express different sides of themself through writing, but they don’t usually become entirely new people.
