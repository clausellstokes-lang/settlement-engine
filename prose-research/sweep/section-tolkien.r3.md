# Tolkien: the prose style, reconstructed from verified claims only

Scope. Built from `sweep/kept-tolkien.json`, the 356 claims that survived verification (316 VERIFIED_VERBATIM, 40 VERIFIED_SUBSTANCE) out of 373 swept across four angles. The 17 dropped claims (12 contradicted, 2 not found, 3 blocked) are listed in the coverage table and, where they bear on a feature, named inline. Claims are cited as #n. Every quotation is the verifier's recorded true wording, trimmed to a contiguous span under twelve words. A feature's source count is a count of distinct voices (a critic, the author, a corpus paper), not of pages or claims: Siepmann's one conference paper, cited twenty-four times, counts as one voice. A feature resting on one voice is flagged SINGLE SOURCE. Disagreements between voices are marked DISAGREEMENT.

The reconstruction rules at the end of each feature are written for the settlement dossier's archivist: present tense, concrete civic nouns, no digits, no em dash, one calm voice that translates old records rather than authoring events.

## 1. One plain modern base, and a higher register that rises only where the matter is old

Support: 4 voices, 9 claims. Rosebury (#0, #95, #267, #324), Le Guin (#244, #314), the Tolkien: Medieval and Modern course blog (#132), Tom Simon (#97, #99).

Wording: "Tolkien begins this in plain language, modulating into a higher register" (Wikipedia summarising Rosebury on the Merry and Witch-king passage, #0); "essentially plain syntax" (#267); "It ranges easily from the commonplace to the stately" (Le Guin, #244); "flexible, clear, simple" (#132).

Caveat: #0 is substance-only because the claim turned what Rosebury said of one passage into a rule; the page states it of that passage. The generalisation is carried independently by Simon's account of the whole book's arc (#97) and Le Guin's account of range (#244). Rosebury's mechanism (#267): the rise is signalled by small syntactic clues woven into plain clauses (heed rather than notice), not by a change of vocabulary.

Rule: the dossier's default sentence is plain modern English in ordinary order. The register rises, by one word choice or one fronted phrase, only in the sentence that touches the founding, the old road, the dead or the ruin, and it returns to plain in the next sentence.

## 2. The archaic feel comes from sentence structure, not from antique words

Support: 8 voices, 16 claims. Simon (#98, #99, #104), Oakley Gordon (#117, #338), Shippey (#6, #16, #44, #296, and via Alden #340), Le Guin (#247, #316), Walker (#108, #322), Rosebury (#52, #265), a Goodreads reader (#310).

Wording: "coupling archaic syntax with deliberately ordinary vocabulary" (Simon, #104); "the sentence structure begins to approximate an older diction" (#99); "archaic in tone and diction" but refraining from archaic words, which Simon names neo-archaic (#98); "he has no ichor" (Le Guin, #247); "a simple, monosyllabic style" (Shippey via Lee, #44); "familiar phrasal verbs 'have on' and 'get off'" preferred to wear and dismount (Rosebury via Turner, #265); "could switch it on and off at will" (Shippey on Tolkien's technical command of archaism, #16); "unlooked-for" chosen over "unexpectedly" (#310).

DISAGREEMENT: Walker locates the antique feel in diction, in familiar but old-fashioned words such as deem, moot and wight: "antiquity comes not from Old English but more familiar words" (#108). Claim #19, which put the syntax reading into Walker's mouth, was CONTRADICTED for exactly this reason. All voices agree that genuinely archaic words are few and used rarely (#108, #322). Read the two positions together: familiar words with an old shade, set in older arrangements.

Rule: never use thee, thou, hath, whither, ere, or any word a reader must look up. Choose the plain native word (mill, ford, ward, toll, gate, hall, reeve). Let age enter through arrangement: a fronted object, a delayed subject, a postponed adjective, at most one to a paragraph. Prefer the native compound (unlooked-for, ward-house) to the Latin word.

## 3. Fronting and inversion, used sparingly, for delay

Support: 7 voices, 11 claims. Turner (#23, #329), Lee (#37, #38, #39), Rosebury (#53, #96), Simon (#100), Alden (#340), Shippey (#320), a Tumblr close reader (#361).

Wording: "Weapons they have laid at your doors" (Turner's specimen of object fronting, #23); "the object is moved to the beginning" (Lee, who finds it the commonest device in the siege passage, #37); "the inverted syntax creates delay and suspense" (#39); "loses force and rhythm" (Simon on modernising Ask not, #100); "the Object-Subject-Verb sentence form" (Alden, #340); "powerfully musical sentence" (Rosebury on inversion with assonance, #53).

Lee adds two sister devices (#38): the postponed adjective (the orcs innumerable) and the delayed subject after a fronted adverbial. Turner notes fronting cannot be rendered in French and is unmarked in German (#23, #329): it is an English-specific marked effect. Lee also finds (#37) that the fronted object usually picks up something named in the previous sentence.

Rule: three forms are allowed: the fronted object (the toll the millers pay), the delayed subject (at the ford stands the old ward-house), and the postponed adjective (the fields unploughed). Use one per passage, on the thing just named in the sentence before, so that fronting does the work of a connective. Never stack two in one sentence.

## 4. Parataxis: short clauses joined by and, with no causal connective

Support: 8 voices, 15 claims. Turner (#54, #158, #323), Shippey (#159, #254), Kullmann and Siepmann (#55, #331), McIntosh citing Garth (#157), Halbrooks (#295, #302), Lee (#41, #43), the Fandomentals (#354), Ephraim (#312), Drout (#166).

Wording: "sentences without subordinate clauses or causal conjunctions" (Turner, #54); "loose semantic fit" (Shippey's name for the and that avoids explicit logic, #159); "stylistically marked" (Turner on the New Testament and at the Pelennor, #158); "breathless excitement" (Garth via McIntosh, #157); "Tolkien deliberately condensed the prose" (Lee, from the typescripts, #41); "pairs nicely with a simplicity of prose" (short plain sentences at the Cirith Ungol escape, #354); "archaic diction, parataxis with clauses linked with 'and'" (Kullmann and Siepmann's definition of the epic style, #55); "also reflects the semantics of the Bible" (Lee on the narrator's Behold and Lo, #43).

Caveats: #344, which fused two of McIntosh's examples and gave Éomer's and-chain to Théoden, was CONTRADICTED; the device stands. Drout's apo koinou reading (#166) rests on one phrase and his own hedged "can be read as". The Behold and Lo point (#43) is substance-only.

Rule: the dossier's oldest and most decisive sentences are short and stacked. Chain them with and, never with because, so that or therefore; the reader supplies the logic. Follow Ephraim's shape (#312): one long sentence to track a movement across difficult ground, then a run of short sentences for the particulars. The archivist never exclaims; Behold and Lo belong to the epic narrator, not the record.

## 5. Two beats: stress and release at every scale, and the prose read aloud

Support: 2 critics through six pages, 23 claims. Le Guin (#58, #59, #60, #61, #62, #63, #64, #65, #149, #150, #151, #152, #153, #154, #250, #251, #252, #348, #351, #352), Drout (#148, #195, #325).

Wording: "as simple as a rhythm can be: two beats" (#62); "a repeated stress and release pattern" (#148); "one stress every two to four syllables" (the normal narrative ratio, rising toward one in two in intense passages, #58); "except for Tom, it is irregular, it can't be scanned" (#59); "dignified, often stately gait, but not a regular stress-beat" (Elves and Aragorn, #60); "balanced cadences in passages of epic action" (#61); "Short rhythms repeated form long rhythms" (#154); "the cadences are graceful and inevitable" (#152); "Tolkien must have heard what he wrote" (#153); "They are like drumbeats." (the quiet closing sentences of the Barrow-downs chapter, #348); "both journeylike and architectural" (#65); "irreducible simplicity of the trochaic beat" (#150).

Attribution caveat (#195, #325): Wikipedia credits the stress-and-release formulation to Drout and gives Le Guin only a sympathetic account; Le Guin's own essay carries the two-beat thesis (#62, #149). Treat it as Le Guin's finding restated by Drout.

Le Guin's polarity list for one chapter (#63, #251, #252): darkness and daylight, resting and travelling, confusion and clarity, menace and ease, imprisonment and freedom, fear and courage, each reversed "some three or four times" in the chapter. "Directionality is extremely important" (#64): up and hilltops are mostly luckier than down and valleys, with the Barrow-downs as her own exception.

Rule: read every paragraph aloud and put the punctuation where the breath falls. Alternate a tense sentence with an easing one. Let each dossier section move between one pair of civic polarities (scarcity and plenty, siege and market, exile and return) and reverse the pair at least twice before the section closes. Treat up and down as real directions on the settlement's ground, and let the higher place be, mostly, the safer one.

## 6. Alliteration and metre cross from verse into prose at the high points

Support: 13 voices, 18 claims. Lee (#40), Brauer (#69, #309), Halbrooks (#305), Venkatesh (#155, #156, #308), Zimmer (#66), Walker with Forest-Hill and Dettmann (#162), Simon (#102), Le Guin (#59), Nagy via Galin (#313), Hall (#142), Ephraim (#312), Kullmann (#67, #68), Turner (#273), Sanders and Marchese on the first sentence of The Hobbit (#356, #357).

Wording: "seeing the further opportunity for alliteration" (Lee on a manuscript revision that replaced like thunder with and rattled, #40); "makes its way into Tolkien's prose" (Brauer on the Rohirrim chapters, #69); "like a drumbeat, marking the relentless forward drive" (Venkatesh on the iambic base of the martial prose, #155); "elevated, incantatory, almost trance-like" (the alliterative shift, #156); "written by a poet of high technical skill" (Zimmer, #66); "essentially all in a poetic metre" (Bombadil's speech, #162); "alliteration, an understated bit of rhyme" (Ephraim on the Dead Marshes, #312); "fitted in style and contents to the characters" (every poem matched to its speaker, #67); "markedly English and rural in character" (hobbit verse, #273); "a well-constructed portal of words" (Marchese on the first sentence, #357).

Caveats: the metrical readings of the opening sentence (#356, #357) are two lay readers on one sentence. SINGLE SOURCE: the claim that the horn passage scans in both metres at once rests on Venkatesh alone (#308). The repetition clause of #156 (All is lost) was not on the page. #67's quoted wording is Tolkien's own from a letter, not Kullmann and Siepmann's.

Rule: in the sentence that names the founding or the fall, let two or three stressed words share an initial sound (the ford and the field and the first hall). Do not sustain it past one sentence; it is a lift, not a pattern. The archivist writes no verse, but a rite, a toast or a lament quoted from the record may carry a four-stress alliterating line.

## 7. Repetition, variation and the withheld name

Support: 5 voices, 5 claims. Lee (#42), Drout via Lee (#47), the Universe in Words blog (#146), Nepveu (#345), Le Guin (#151).

Wording: "Horns, horns, horns" with the identity of the arriving force withheld to the final sentence (#42); "repetition and mirroring, and non-standard sentence structure" (Drout's observed effects, #47); "relies on the repetition of a single phrase" (the lament's Where is, #146); "but he was ever before them" (the repeated but that carries momentum, #345); "repetition, semirepetition, suggestion, foreshadowing, recollection, echo, and reversal" (Le Guin's list of the devices that build the world, #151).

Rule: a thing may be renamed across three sentences (the mill, the wheel-house, the miller's seat) before its plain name is given. A phrase may return once with one word changed. When a force or a family arrives in the record, withhold its name until the final sentence of the paragraph.

## 8. Lexis: several centuries of English at once, and never the modern world

Support: 5 voices, 29 claims; Siepmann's one paper supplies 24. Siepmann (#25, #26, #27, #28, #29, #30, #31, #32, #33, #109, #110, #111, #112, #113, #114, #115, #116, #286, #287, #288, #289, #332, #333, #334), Kullmann and Siepmann via Lee (#35), Lee (#36), Drout (#165), Nepveu's commenters (#347), Tolkien's Guide (#235).

Wording: "partly due to three simple lexical causes" (#25): overuse of nineteenth-century fiction words, avoidance of words tied to the modern world, and dense rare or obsolescent words. The direction set: "whither, thither, thence, whence, upwards, yonder, afar" (#26), one of four sets with landscape, warfare, and descriptive verbs and adjectives (#287). "borrows a host of words from his translation of Beowulf" (valour, valiant, corslet, stretched to new uses, #111); "a black smoke swirled in the air" (a modern word in a nineteenth-century pattern, #28); "abundant use of descriptive verbs" carrying their own manner (clutch, cower, flicker, gleam, glint, loom, plod, stride, #29); "atmosphere of medievalism or otherworldliness" from words rare rather than archaic (fastness, sheath, yesteryear, along, among, outwards, #30); "marked preference for the most homely collocations" (#31); "Tolkien tends to resort to hyperbole" (great with peril, deeds, halls, shadow, smoke, #32); "he is a linguistic postmodernist" (#33); "around twice as many key words with nineteenth-century fiction" as with late twentieth-century fiction (#35); "words that have fallen out of general use and feel archaic" (Lee's battle list: hardiest, slain, foe, thrice, asunder, riven, steadfast, #36); "an archaic form of 'elephant' used as a 'rusticism'" (Oliphaunt, #235).

Caveats: Siepmann hedges with partly (#332), and the plant vocabulary in #32 is presented by the paper as realism, not hyperbole. Drout draws the line between functional and ornamental archaism: "merely an ornamental archaism" for thrawn, against words like eyot that do a describing job (#165). Tolkien revised fire and flood to fire and death to remove an ambiguity (#347), and the older non-positive sense of wise is exploited in a pale wise face (#347).

Rule: draw the dossier's word-stock from landscape, position and direction, craft and warfare, and manner-laden verbs (loom, gleam, plod, stride). Admit no word that names the modern world. Pair the plainest adjectives (great, long, dark, deep) with concrete civic nouns, and allow one hyperbole of measure per section. Use no old word that does not describe something exactly. Where an old word has two senses, use the older one on purpose and let the context fix it.

## 9. Discrete registers for peoples and ranks: speech is character

Support: 14 voices, 31 claims. Turner (#1, #2, #272, #319), Tolkien (#204, #217, #231), Kullmann and Siepmann (#15, #134, #291), Kirk via Rogers (#74), Shippey (#4, #256, #335, via Yee #337), Le Guin (#246, #315), Rosebury (#12, #14, #135, #137), Vaninskaya (#13, #136), Milom (#138), Bayona via Wikipedia (#330), Meyer (#128, #129), La Porte (#189), Shippey on Saruman (#10, #126, #321).

Wording: "discrete linguistic registers for different characters, peoples, and cultures" (Turner, #272); "you've got to make them different" (Tolkien on having several peoples in hand, #204); "some differentiation of style to represent dialectal differences" (Letter 144, #217); "Speech expresses character." (Le Guin, #246); "bring out differences in status and character" (Shippey via Yee, #337); "Colloquial, rustic, working class" (Sam in the Kullmann and Siepmann table; Aragorn archaic and literary, Gandalf plain and flexible, #134); "three types of Orc-dialogue for different ranks" (Rosebury, #12); "the clipped language of army dispatches" (Vaninskaya on orc speech, #136); "talks like a politician" using empty words like real change (Shippey on Saruman, #126); "politically minded, abstract manners of speech" (#10); "a style that is only subtly archaized" (Meyer on Gandalf, #128); "versatility and by the ability to hear and replicate other styles" (#129); "plural nouns were most likely to be injected with extra sibilants" (Milom's corpus on Gollum, #138); "more degraded and filthy than I have shown it" (Appendix F on orc speech, #231).

Caveats: #15 is substance-only; the page says eight members barring Boromir. #12 and #135 fold Gorbag's bureaucratic idiom into Rosebury when the page gives it to Vaninskaya. #126's "the one modern register" was not supported, since Aragorn is also called deceptively modern (#124). #10's "modern" is the claim's word, not the page's. Sam and the Gaffer carry double negatives, duplicated subjects and altered agreement; Gollum carries ungrammatical syntax and the plural pocketses (#330).

Rule: the dossier speaks in one archivist voice, but every quoted or reported speaker (a reeve, a herald, a market-woman, a garrison order) keeps a register keyed to rank and trade: the herald formal and fronted, the reeve proverb-heavy with many words and few to the point, the garrison in clipped dispatch, the trader in plain phrasal verbs. The deceiver gets abstract nouns and empty intensifiers, never the archivist's own register. Non-standard grammar marks the lowest rank and nothing else.

## 10. The council as showcase: no speaker answers in the previous speaker's manner

Support: 4 voices, 21 claims; Shippey through six relays. Shippey (#4, #6, #8, #9, #124, #125, #126, #127, #256, #258, #259, #296, #306, #307, #320, #335), Jaffe (#130, #306), Le Guin (#245, #315), the course blog (#131, #133).

Wording: "consistently archaic and formal" (Elrond, #130); "achieved not just by vocabulary" but also by grammar (Elrond's archaism, Shippey, #6); "Now, therefore, things shall be openly spoken" (Elrond's inversion, #320); "short, broken sentences and apposition" (Glóin, #258); "many words and few to the point" (the Gaffer as the baseline of ordinary speech, #125); "slightly wooden magniloquence" (Boromir, #124); "Boromir speaks sort of Elrondian" and Aragorn answers him in that register to impress him (#256); "His manner of speech changes directly with the situation" (Aragorn, #9); "an English extraordinary for its simple timelessness" (Le Guin, #245); "Feel ready for the great council?" (Bilbo's dropped subject marking informality, #133); Boromir's "very slight whine" as the tell of envy (#315).

Caveats: #7 (Dwarvish curtness credited to Rosebury) and #5 (the -eth endings put on Elrond rather than Isildur's scroll) were CONTRADICTED on attribution; the observations are Shippey's and stand under #258 and #6. #127 (twelve speakers plus seven quoted in Gandalf's speech, which fills half the chapter) is substance-only. Jaffe's point (#130): range marks the elf-friend; Aragorn can descend, Boromir cannot.

Rule: when the dossier reports a council, a hearing or a market dispute, let each voice keep its own syntax across the exchange, and let rank show as range: the high speaker can descend to the plain register, the pretender cannot. The lowest voice is the measure against which the others are heard.

## 11. Register is thought: modern idiom would falsify what an old speaker can think

Support: 3 voices, 12 claims, all resting on Tolkien's Letter 171. Tolkien (#17, #18, #196, #197, #198, #199, #339), Gordon (#117, #118, #338), Simon (#101, #105).

Wording: "a real archaic English is far more terse than modern" (#196); "word orders that are terser, more vivid" and sometimes nobler (#117); "a disunion of word and meaning" (#198); "would not have had those thoughts" (a king who spoke in modern fashion, #118); "moderated or watered archaism" (Tolkien on Théoden's dialogue, short of authentic antique diction, #197); "Shake yourself out of this parochialism of time!" (#199); "looser, more full of little 'empty' words" (modern English, #17).

Caveats: #338's specific mechanism (that the final clause order carries the thought) is not on the page; the general point is. #197's claim named Middle English where the source says antique diction.

Rule: the archivist's register is moderated, not authentic antique. Where the record reports what an old office or an old rite held, phrase it in the terser older order, because the modern paraphrase would put a modern thought in an old mouth. Cut the little empty words: really, actually, quite, just, very.

## 12. Timeless, not archaic: a high wire with no permitted fall

Support: 8 voices, 17 claims. Le Guin (#57, #119, #244, #245, #248, #315), Ostadan (#120), Sperling via the course blog (#57), Rateliff (#122, #123, #297, #342), Alden (#340, #341), Mythgard posters (#343), Tolkien (#226, Appendix F #230), Shippey (#300).

Wording: "simple timelessness" avoiding both thee and thou and modern slang (Le Guin, #57); "the language of men of character" (#119); "It's a high wire: one slip spoils all." (#248); "pop up at random in the midst of more timeless speech" (Ostadan on Okay, like and conflicted, #120); "Tolkien is trying for one effect and instead achieving another" (Rateliff on Éowyn's thou, #122); "used the familiar for people of all ranks" (Pippin in Minas Tirith, Appendix F, #123); "we've lost 'poetic diction'" (#297); "Yea/Nay for Yes/No" as the cheap marker (#343); "the air of timeless Elvish enchantment" (Letter 131, #226); "archaic though they often are" (Shippey on lines delivered with conviction, #300).

DISAGREEMENT inside the thou material: Tolkien meant thou as intimacy (#122, #230, #341, #342); modern readers hear formality (#122, #343); Alden reports Christopher Tolkien that his father was in two minds (#341). Whatever the intent, the marker now misfires. Le Guin also faults alternating slang with formal usage as breaking character coherence (#248).

Rule: no thou, thee, yea or nay anywhere in the dossier; the register is simple timelessness. Equally no modern filler: okay, basically, impacted, conflicted, focused. One slip breaks the world, so the ban is absolute. Intimacy and distance are shown by what is said, never by pronoun.

## 13. The Rohirrim pattern: Old English syntax, rescued words, slower delivery

Support: 9 voices, 14 claims. Walker (#20, #322), Wikipedia editors (#21), Wilton (#22, #144, #368), Shippey (#24), Tolkien (#11, #232, #237), Hall (#142), Bracewell (#145), Nielsen Hayden (#371), Halila (#369).

Wording: "stirring, alliteratively stately" and stern diction with Anglo-Saxon syntactic patterns (Walker, #20); "words rescued from total obscurity like éored, dwimmerlaik, and mearas" (#21); "seem vaguely familiar and evoke images of ages past" (Wilton on mathom, smial and the Rohan usages, #144); "much slower and more deliberate" (Tolkien on Rohirric speech in the Common Tongue, a second language, #11); "alliteration of the stressed syllables" (Hall, #142); "Modernized form of Rohan grēg-hama" (Greyhame in the Guide, #237); "Westu Théoden hál" (#368); "Hail the victorious dead" as Maldon-grade Anglo-Saxon (#371); the double gate-and-door challenge at Edoras as the most direct Beowulf homage (#369).

Caveats: #139 and #253 credited Walker's phrases to Shippey and were CONTRADICTED; #143 (that Tolkien absorbed Beowulf's appositive layering) was CONTRADICTED because Hall says the opposite. #24's mapping (Old English to Modern English as Rohirric to Westron) holds but the page leaves the sentence unattributed. SINGLE SOURCE: the slower-delivery point (#11) is one relay of one letter.

Rule: for the settlement's oldest institutions use worn native compounds (ward-house, moot-hill, mark-stone, ford-toll) and modernised forms, never Latinate or foreign terms. Give the oldest speakers fewer words, delivered slowly, and one alliterating pair. A greeting or a toast from the record may keep its old form entire.

## 14. Hobbits mediate: the plain voice is the reader's way in, and it closes the epic

Support: 8 voices, 14 claims. Shippey (#3, #140, #261), Turner (#141, #319), Simon (#107), Tolkien (#203), Lee (#45), Kullmann and Siepmann (#290, #331), Kirk via Rogers (#74, #171), Lewis (#278), the Never Felt Better re-read (#372).

Wording: "mediators between the ordinary modern world" and the heroic archaic realm (Shippey, #261); "simple and modern for Hobbits" (#319); "O Wise People!" (Pippin's attempt at the formal register, which mediates it for the reader, #141); "a point of contact between the legendarium and the modern reader" (what The Silmarillion lacks, Simon, #107); "Hobbits are just rustic English people" (Tolkien, #203); "About half of The Lord of the Rings consists of dialogue" or verse or embedded story, and the viewpoint is a hobbit's about eighty-five parts in a hundred (#290, #331); "many a snug fireside and many an hour of good cheer" set against anguish (Lewis, #278); "a grand philological experiment" with a deliberate fairy-tale hobbit style (Kirk, #74); "Well, I'm back" as the hobbit answer to epic events (#372).

Caveats: #203's reason for hobbit smallness is imagination, not speech. #372's "restraint" framing is not the page's; the page calls the scene emotional. #74 is a blog summary of Kirk.

When the record reaches an ancient thing, let a plain observer's attempt at formality show and fall short, once. Close the great event on a small domestic fact.

## 15. The stylistic arch: a jolly opening, rising seriousness, formal archaism, and back to plain

Support: 10 voices, 17 claims. Simon (#97, #98, #102, #103), Turner via Lee (#46), Lewis (#181), Halbrooks (#294, #301, #304), the Fandomentals (#353), Forchhammer (#299), Ephraim (#311), Lobdell (#94), Rosebury (#56, #260), Auden (#179, #283).

Wording: "The story begins in a jolly Edwardian style" (#97); "Asymmetrical stylistic arch" (Turner's second of four areas for assessing the style, #46); "like an historian, or a nature writer, or an epic poet" in turn, depending on the setting (Halbrooks, #294); "the effect is one of sustained phantasmagoria" in the Old Forest chapters (#102); "the hosts of Mordor wailed, and terror took them" (the heroic register of Book Five after the hobbit-colloquial Book Four, #304); "His tempo was typically slow, but never plodding" (Ephraim, #311); "bouncing one out of the story" (Simon's warning against style that calls attention to itself, #103); "light comedy is not Mr. Tolkien's forte" (Auden on the opening chapters, #179); "a sustained archaism" modelled on Morris in the early prose, later shed as "impossibly heavy archaism" (Rosebury, #56, #260); "the mist was rolled up and thrust aside" as the language shifting with the atmosphere (#353).

Caveats. SINGLE SOURCE: the Amon Hen caesura (#102) and the deliberate limiting of fast passages so that they contrast (#311) each rest on one essayist. Lobdell's Edwardian adventure-story thesis (#94) is substance-only from a catalogue record. Lewis's "taking a risk" on the light first chapter (#181) is softer than the claim's "risks losing readers".

Rule: the dossier moves through registers by place and age, never at random: the market plain, the ward-house sober, the founding stone formal. Fast passages are rare so that they read fast. The archivist never draws attention to the shift; a reader who notices the style has been bounced out of the record.

## 16. The narrator: the avuncular aside was a fault, and he cut it

Support: 11 voices, 17 claims. Tolkien (#211, #214, #224), Thomas via Nepveu (#70), Litschko (#71, #169), Mambrol (#168, #358), Shippey (#257, #336), Forumshire readers (#360), Drout (#163), Lee with Kullmann and Siepmann (#45, #81), Nepveu (#349), Evan Þ (#359), Rateliff (#72).

Wording: "They hated asides" (Tolkien on his children, #211); "sillinesses of manner caught unthinkingly" from the children's-book convention (Letter 163, #214); "a bad style, as if one were talking to children" (#168); "impartial and self-effacing" (the Lord of the Rings narrator; early drafts failed when they returned to the intrusive voice, #70); "does not take part in the story" (heterodiegetic, ranging over the battlefield with the Old English poet's freedom, #45); "a failure of tone" (Drout on the fox's thoughts, against the successful contented-logs aside, #163); "as you probably know" and of course, treating the strange as obvious (#257); "business manner" (Bilbo's, in the see-saw with the narrator's matter-of-fact tone, #336); "softens the blow" of the deaths (#360); "the narrator's voice isn't as explicit" but persists in offhand foreknowledge (#359); "lower the tone towards that of a more childish fairy-tale" (Letter 210 on Old scamp, #224); "the narrative repeatedly leapfrogs" between threads with synchronising landmarks (#81); "losing much of the humour" (why the 1960 rewrite of The Hobbit was abandoned, #72).

DISAGREEMENT: Nepveu (#349) and Evan Þ (#359) find the intrusive narrator still present in the later book, raising and discounting the supernatural, or knowing the future; Thomas (#70) and Lee (#45) call it self-effacing. Both hold at different densities: rare and impartial, not absent.
