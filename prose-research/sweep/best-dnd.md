**How to read a row.** *What it does* is one sentence. *On the page* is at most twelve verbatim words from a cited **kept** row. *Rows* are kept-row indexes. *Sources* counts INDEPENDENT voices among those rows (one page twice is one; an author's two separate columns is still one voice where the section itself flags ONE SOURCE, and the count says so). *Strength* is mechanical: three or more independent sources STRONG, two MODERATE, one SINGLE. *Anti-AI risk* names the structural fault the technique trips if it is applied as a template rather than as a grammar — a fixed move order, a metronome of one sentence shape, the summary second sentence, antithesis, tricolon. *Conflicts* names another rule of this author or of an exemplar the section names. *Transferable* is judged against the owner's constraints: the archivist voice is never transplanted onto chrome; no digits in running prose; a seed is a starting world; faith is culture, never theology; never a named character's fate; every element gets its full form, and full form is never manufactured content.

## A. The narrator's distance and the address of the reader

### 1. Viewer-absent third person: the subject is always the thing, never the reader
**What it does.** Every descriptive sentence takes the thing as its grammatical subject and admits no "you", no "your", no "as you enter", so the record describes a place rather than staging a visit to it.
**On the page.** "Move from the second-person point of view to the third person" (104)
**Rows.** 104, 1199, 11, 1085, 1086, 109, 168 · **Sources.** 5 (Merwin's two columns count once; the DUNGEON guidelines, BAATAG, Heir, Alexander) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · herald-pools · chronicle-line · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** The subject-first declarative becomes a metronome: forty consecutive sentences of *The mill stands / The gate is barred / The quay runs*. The cure is not to relax the person rule but to vary where the sentence's weight falls — a subordinate clause first, an object first, a one-word opening (see 19).
**Conflicts.** The publisher's own specimens address the party as "you" (872, 1041, 900) while its own platform column forbids it (1199); DM's Workshop recommends the reverse shift to second person for a table losing focus (108, 407). The section records the disagreement rather than hiding it, and so do we.
**Transferable.** YES — this is the single most load-bearing transfer in the author. It is already the archivist's law and it now has five independent witnesses instead of a house preference.

### 2. No assigned reaction, no prediction: the record shows the scene and leaves the response to the reader
**What it does.** The prose never tells the reader what they feel, think, do or are about to do, and never runs ahead of them into an event that has not happened.
**On the page.** "Don't tell them how they're reacting to it." (662)
**Rows.** 662, 661, 168, 107, 110, 1200 · **Sources.** 5 (Alexander, Heir, Dyson, Designing Dungeons, Merwin) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · herald-pools · chronicle-line · chrome
**Anti-AI risk.** Applied mechanically it strips emotion from the townspeople too, and the record goes flat. The ban is on the READER's interior, not the resident's: the miller may be bitter on the record; the reader may never be told they are uneasy.
**Conflicts.** Nothing in this author; it is the same rule as 1 from the other side. It sharpens against feature 39's requirement that every resident of record have something going on.
**Transferable.** YES — and it is the cleanest available test for AI-tell prose, which habitually tells the reader what to feel about the place it just described.

### 3. Evidential marking: fact stated as fact, the hedge reserved for inference or an unchecked claim
**What it does.** Perception is reported as fact with no "seems" or "appears", except where the record is drawing an inference or repeating a claim it cannot verify, and there the hedge is explicit and names its ground.
**On the page.** "Describe everything the characters can sense, but not what they cannot." (114)
**Rows.** 114, 990, 441, 563, 564, 1078 · **Sources.** 4 (Merwin, Dausuul, Alexander, Perkins) · **Strength.** STRONG — but the archivist half (a written record may hedge, because a document's author can be wrong) rests on **Alexander alone**, two pages, and the section flags it ONE SOURCE; the no-hedge half carries three.
**Registers.** dossier-archivist (hedge admitted, marked) · herald-pools (no hedge at all) · compendium-docent · chronicle-line
**Anti-AI risk.** The hedge becomes a verbal tic — *it appears*, *by report*, *the ledger claims* on a three-paragraph cycle — which is the metronome wearing a scholar's coat. Rule: a hedge marks a SOURCE, never a quantity, and never appears twice in one entry.
**Conflicts.** Sims cuts weasel words without mercy and prefers examples to hedged generalisations (1111, 1115); the section does not say how the two coexist. Our resolution: Sims bans the quantity-hedge (*generally*, *typically*), feature 23 licenses only the provenance-hedge.
**Transferable.** YES — evidential marking is what makes a record a record rather than an omniscient narrator, and it is the mechanism by which a settlement dossier can be honest about what it does not know.

### 4. The declared, bounded vantage: a clerk of the place, never an "I", never a claim to contain a people
**What it does.** The document states whose record it is and how far that record reaches, speaks of itself in the third person, and refuses the encyclopedic pose that claims one text can hold a people.
**On the page.** "produce encyclopedic knowledge about an imagined and exotic other" (584)
**Rows.** 584, 585, 522, 523, 1113, 907 · **Sources.** 4 (Trammell, Appelcline on the Volo device, Sims, the publisher's Ravenloft guidance) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** A declared vantage invites a recurring first-move ("The record of X notes…") that becomes a fixed opening; the vantage is declared once, on the document's own front matter, not re-declared at each entry.
**Conflicts.** The in-character narrator is praised for licensing rumour and error (523) and condemned when it claims completeness (584, 585); and Baur says state the expected arc plainly, don't be coy (835), against Cook's leave-it-unsaid (149).
**Transferable.** YES, and it discharges two owner laws at once: faith is culture, never theology (the record describes what a people does, not what is true of its gods), and the record never contains a people.

### 5. Register modulated by audience, and the setting's cant kept out of the record's own sentences
**What it does.** The document is written for the reader who will use it rather than for its author, and a setting's slang, wry voice or house cant lives in the mouths of its people and never in the narrating register.
**On the page.** "communicating the basic info and less on dazzling the audience" (1145)
**Rows.** 1145, 1146, 1072, 833, 828, 1124, 358 · **Sources.** 5 (Vallese, Cook interviewed, Cook at Kobold, Baur, Perkins) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** all six — this is the rule that separates chrome from the archivist
**Anti-AI risk.** None as a rule; the failure is the opposite one, a single flattened register everywhere, which is exactly what AI prose with better vocabulary sounds like. The technique demands register DIFFERENCE, measured, not a uniform calm.
**Conflicts.** Perkins wants the reading DM entertained and puns embedded (198, 199); Winter wants flowery phrase and clever wordplay struck (1129, 188). The tone gate (72, 245) arbitrates.
**Transferable.** YES — it is the sourced statement of the owner's own law that the archivist voice is never transplanted onto chrome. The Realms guide's plain trade tongue against its expressive older tongues (358) gives the same rule a diegetic form: the Herald may be plainer than the chronicle line, and this is a reason, not a preference.

### 6. Documentarian, not novelist: the props of a world, not a plot; no scene, no witness at the event
**What it does.** The record carries the places, people, creatures and past events of a world as objects a reader can use, in plain exposition a novel could not bear, and never stages a scene, a soliloquy or a rising action.
**On the page.** "the writer as a documentarian of an imaginary world" (190)
**Rows.** 190, 191, 117, 404, 14, 200, 232 · **Sources.** 5 (Mearls, Merwin, DM's Workshop, the DUNGEON guidelines, Perkins; Crawford for the standing-alone limb) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · chronicle-line · compendium-docent · herald-pools
**Anti-AI risk.** None; it is a cure. But "no narrative" applied mechanically to the chronicle line produces a list of dated facts with no causal grammar, which is the opposite failure — the chronicle records events without staging them, and keeps its because.
**Conflicts.** Feature 42's in-world documents are narratives of a sort, and the chronicle line records events; the resolution is that they are quoted objects and dated entries, never scenes with a witness placed inside them.
**Transferable.** YES. Owner-constrained on one point: the record documents a place, and a named character's fate is not among the props it may decide.

### 7. The text must stand on its own, without its author's intent, and be judged in use
**What it does.** Treats the written record as the only thing the reader will ever have, and tests it by whether a stranger can lift the place off the page, not by whether it reads well.
**On the page.** "The words on the page are only a memory cue" (686)
**Rows.** 686, 687, 232, 164, 165, 699, 720, 712, 713 · **Sources.** 6 (Lynch, Crawford, Noonan, Lux, Necropraxis, and Lux's counter-case 720) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** all six — a test, not a device
**Anti-AI risk.** None. This is the acceptance test our reconstruction most lacks: prose that reads well and plays badly is precisely what a fluent generator produces.
**Conflicts.** None internal.
**Transferable.** YES — adopt as a gate, not a style rule: an entry passes when a reader who has never seen the seed can run the place from the entry alone.

## B. The sentence

### 8. Concrete and specific over generic and evaluative: one telling detail, the filler adjectives hunted
**What it does.** Replaces evaluative abstractions with an exact physical fact, makes every adjective and adverb earn its place, and chooses the verb for the exact motion of the thing.
**On the page.** "Every sentence needs to fight for its life" (122)
**Rows.** 123, 122, 124, 439, 440, 444, 442, 431, 724, 725, 601, 128 · **Sources.** 9 (Gus L, Skerples, Conowitch, Radiating Gnome, Dausuul, Lynch, Hartlage, Shea, and Lynch's specificity/detail split) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · herald-pools · chronicle-line · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** HIGH if templated. "One telling detail per paragraph" is a fixed move order in disguise, and "three of five senses" (125) becomes a sense-tricolon in a fixed order (sight, sound, smell) which is exactly the metronome. Take the TEST, not the quota: does the word conjure a specific thing.
**Conflicts.** Explicit and useful: Lynch cures a generic town by adding one modifier before a role noun ("the prim farmers wife", 431); Radiating Gnome and Dausuul call modifiers the enemy and cut them (439, 442). Lynch himself separates specificity from detail and calls detail trivia (724). Both sides agree on the test and disagree on the instrument.
**Transferable.** YES — the strongest transferable in the author for the sentence level, and it is the direct antidote to the generic-place failure our prose has.

### 9. Present tense, active voice, the expletive struck, the copula kept
**What it does.** Writes the world in the present, makes the thing the agent of its own sentence, strikes "there is/there are" and the future "will", and does NOT strike the copula, which the publisher's own place prose uses freely.
**On the page.** "If the agents reach the safe house, Keletny burns the car." (112)
**Rows.** 112, 133, 183, 1011, 907 · **Sources.** 4 (Laws, BAATAG, the Dragon guidelines, the in-house SRD measurement; the Ravenloft guidance for the copula model) · **Strength.** STRONG — but note the section's own flag: no official document STATES the present-tense rule; it is shown in every example and measured at 1011.
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · herald-pools · compendium-docent · chrome (chronicle-line takes past for dated events)
**Anti-AI risk.** A blanket copula purge produces the strained "the mill grinds" for every "the mill is" and reads as machine-avoidance. The measurement (is/are at 24.84 per thousand against was/were at 0.65, 1011) licenses the copula and forbids the past, which is the more useful rule.
**Conflicts.** The section itself records that the "strike to-be" advice (133, 183) and the measured copula rate (1011) pull opposite ways; Reconciliation 3 resolves it as expletive-and-habitual-passive, not copula.
**Transferable.** YES.

### 10. Economy applied with skill, not as a word count — and Hartlage's cutting test
**What it does.** Cuts a page to a paragraph and a paragraph to a sentence where the sentence carries it, and decides each cut by one question: would the reader have imagined this line anyway?
**On the page.** "the first thing a typical DM would imagine at the table" (749)
**Rows.** 749, 742, 468, 691, 144, 718, 701, 725, 726, 756, 465 · **Sources.** 8 (Hartlage, Campbell, Skerples, Necropraxis, Davis, Lynch, Gearing, Prince) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** all six
**Anti-AI risk.** Minimisation as a rule strips voice — Lux's explicit objection (695: ultra-minimalism strips flavour, depth and inner complexity) and Necropraxis's objection that the stripped key has no poetry left in it (the section cites that row for its quotation only, so it is named here and not counted as evidence). A word-count target IS a metronome. The test is the transferable, the count is not.
**Conflicts.** Sharp and named: the ultra-minimalist key (691, seven words) against Lux and Necropraxis; Hartlage argues a LONGER description spares the reader inventing the obvious (742). Davis names the middle way (701).
**Transferable.** YES — and the cutting test is directly implementable as a generator constraint: a line only the record could supply stays; a line any reader would supply goes.

### 11. Economy WITH a voice: the one-word opening, the chosen adjective, the dry aside
**What it does.** Keeps a recognisable register inside terse prose, so the record reads as written by someone rather than emitted by a form.
**On the page.** "Austere. Uncurtained windows let the gloomy light" (756)
**Rows.** 756, 465, 701, 728 · **Sources.** 4 (Gearing's own demonstration passage, Prince, Davis, Lynch on the parenthetical) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · chronicle-line · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** HIGH if taken as a device. A one-word opening sentence used at every third entry is a tic, and a parenthetical aside on a schedule is the same. These are exceptions that earn their place by rarity; a grammar must ration them, not deploy them.
**Conflicts.** Against 10's economy, and against Winter's "no clever wordplay" (1129); the reconciliation is that the voice is in the noun and the verb, not in ornament.
**Transferable.** YES, PARTIAL in practice — it is the hardest thing here to make mechanical, and the most valuable if we can.

### 12. Read it aloud: accidental sound is a fault, chosen sound is a device
**What it does.** Tests every line by ear before keeping it, striking tongue-twisters and unintended alliteration while permitting sound chosen on purpose.
**On the page.** "Read your boxed text aloud to check readability" (118)
**Rows.** 118, 418, 445, 154, 227 · **Sources.** 4 (Merwin, Radiating Gnome, Hartlage, Shea) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** herald-pools · chronicle-line · chrome · dossier-archivist
**Anti-AI risk.** None — it is a cure for the metronome, because a metronome is audible long before it is visible.
**Conflicts.** Explicit and instructive: Merwin lists unintended alliteration as a fault (418); Radiating Gnome keeps "craggy causeway" on purpose (445). The split is intent, which is a rule a generator can hold: alliteration is allowed only where a rule chose it.
**Transferable.** YES — implementable as a phonetic check in the walker, not merely as advice.

### 13. Absolutes, superlatives and weasel words refused; an example where a hedge would go
**What it does.** Bars *always*, *never*, *all of a people* and every superlative, cuts quantity-hedges, and replaces a hedged generalisation with a named instance.
**On the page.** "Absolutist statements are, instead, editorial nightmares." (1113)
**Rows.** 1113, 1111, 1112, 1115, 1114, 1116 · **Sources.** 1 (Sims, one page) · **Strength.** SINGLE
**Registers.** all six, chrome included
**Anti-AI risk.** None; it removes one of the most reliable AI tells (the hedged universal). But note the risk of over-application: a record that may never say "never" cannot state an invariant, and some invariants are true.
**Conflicts.** Feature 23's licensed hedges (*by report*, *it appears*); the section does not reconcile them and the critic flags it (§6). Our resolution stands in 3 above.
**Transferable.** YES, though SINGLE-sourced: the rule is ours already, with Sims as its nearest kin and not its author (the section says so, honestly, in Reconciliation 3).

## C. The paragraph, the order, the shape of the entry

### 14. Salience order: general to specific, the conspicuous thing first, the hidden thing never first
**What it does.** Orders an entry by what a reader meets first and what is most salient on arrival, not by the order of a template's slots, and moves outward from the whole to its parts.
**On the page.** "should talk about the ogre first" (635)
**Rows.** 652, 653, 635, 138, 446, 452, 474, 692, 717, 338 · **Sources.** 7 (Alexander, Designing Dungeons, Radiating Gnome, Hartlage, Campbell, Necropraxis, Perkins & Bilsland) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · herald-pools · compendium-docent · chronicle-line
**Anti-AI risk.** This is the technique most easily mistaken for a fixed move order and it is in fact its opposite: Alexander's principle is *order by salience, not by slot*. Templated, it becomes "glance → detail → history" every time — the exact fault the owner's latent-grammar directive names. The transferable is the criterion, and the criterion produces a DIFFERENT order per place.
**Conflicts.** Explicit: Designing Dungeons puts monsters last in the first description and first in later keying (138); Gus L starts with inhabitants (139); the DUNGEON guidelines keep creatures out of read-aloud altogether (1089). The section's resolution — salience over template — is the one that survives.
**Transferable.** YES, and it is our best defence against the fixed move order.

### 15. Load-bearing fact never buried; it goes first, never among the ornaments
**What it does.** Puts the fact that changes what the reader does at the front of the unit, and never mid-paragraph among descriptive matter.
**On the page.** "note crucial details buried in the middle of a paragraph" (474)
**Rows.** 474, 452, 157, 851, 1204, 852 · **Sources.** 4 (Alexander, Hartlage, Decker & Noonan — ONE WORK, per critic §4 — Merwin) · **Strength.** STRONG on the count; the "two sentences" observation behind it is ONE WORK read at four routes and the critic says so.
**Registers.** herald-pools · chrome · chronicle-line · dossier-archivist
**Anti-AI risk.** Becomes the summary-first sentence — the AI tell where paragraph one announces what paragraph two will say. The rule is *fact first*, not *summary first*: a fact is not a précis.
**Conflicts.** Feature 42's lorebook rule puts the key information where it is NOT the document's main subject (550) — the opposite placement, for an in-world object rather than for the record.
**Transferable.** YES for the Herald and the chronicle line; PARTIAL for the archivist, whose unit is longer and whose head record already carries the load.

### 16. Empty slots omitted, never filled — the refusal to write where there is nothing to say
**What it does.** Refuses to produce a paragraph because a heading exists, on the finding that a rigid slot order makes writers write nothing at length and lands information out of sequence.
**On the page.** "tends to encourage writers to 'fill the format'" (136)
**Rows.** 136, 634, 633, 648, 709, 1091, 1090, 783, 784, 787, 1120, 1121 · **Sources.** 6 (Alexander, the DUNGEON staff, Cordell in his own words, Deathtrap, Abadia, and Abadia's Season-8 case) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · compendium-docent · dm-page
**Anti-AI risk.** None as stated; the risk is in the opposite direction, and it is ours: under the owner's full-form ruling every element is written, so the fault this technique prevents (manufactured filler) must be prevented by the honest-absence sentence instead of by omission.
**Conflicts.** THREE-WAY, and live for us. Feature 31 says omit; the owner's ruling of 09-07 says write an honest absence in the clerk's third person; and the one official witness (1090) says the editors dislike printing that a room is empty — that is, it argues against the absence line. The critic names this contradiction first among ten (§6). **Our reading: 1090 and 1091 bind the DM page (an adventure need not number an empty room); the dossier writes the absence, because a record's silence is itself a fact about the record.**
**Transferable.** PARTIAL — the diagnosis transfers completely (a slot must never manufacture content), the remedy does not (we write absences rather than omitting elements).

### 17. Everything needed is in the entry; the reader is not sent elsewhere for a fact that belongs here
**What it does.** Puts every fact needed to use a place inside the place's own entry, rather than splitting one door across two sections both of which must be read.
**On the page.** "Everything you need is on the page" (656)
**Rows.** 656, 709, 1120, 1121 · **Sources.** 3 (Alexander, Abadia, Abadia's verdict) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · compendium-docent · chrome
**Anti-AI risk.** None. The compensating fault is the isolated entry that duplicates the region's facts; Alexander names it (709: it artificially isolates the encounter).
**Conflicts.** Against 33's cross-reference rule, which points elsewhere by title; the resolution is that a pointer replaces an entry the record does not yet hold, never a fact the entry needs.
**Transferable.** YES.

### 18. Fixed form so a stranger can find things; consistency is for the reader, not the writer
**What it does.** Keeps the same slots in the same order across entries so that someone who has never read this entry can find a fact in it by habit.
**On the page.** "allows judges to quickly find information they need" (171)
**Rows.** 171, 170, 654, 884 · **Sources.** 3 (Heir — ONE SOURCE for feature 18 per critic §4 — Alexander, the fey-ancestry reviewer on alphabetised parts) · **Strength.** STRONG on the count; the organised-play statement is one author's two columns.
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · compendium-docent · chrome
**Anti-AI risk.** This is the fixed move order, stated as a virtue. The owner's latent-grammar directive is the corrective: the SLOTS are fixed and findable; the ORDER OF MOVES INSIDE a slot varies by a seeded choice among a closed set of grammars. Fixed form at the record level, varied grammar at the sentence level — that distinction is the whole of it.
**Conflicts.** Directly against 14 and 16 (salience over template; do not fill the format). The section resolves it: the slots are fixed for the reader's sake, the order inside a slot is the scene's.
**Transferable.** PARTIAL — the findability transfers; the fixed order must be quarantined at the record level or it becomes the fault we are trying to cure.

### 19. Padding named and refused: no sentence exists because the slot looked empty
**What it does.** Identifies the specific failure of a customary length — a good middle sentence padded like a school paper to an assigned length — and cuts back to the sentence.
**On the page.** "pads it like a school paper stretched to an assigned length" (743)
**Rows.** 743, 422, 745, 536, 537, 484, 507, 426 · **Sources.** 5 (Hartlage, Lynch, Prince, Idle Cartulary, Alexander) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** all six
**Anti-AI risk.** None — it is a named diagnosis of the fault our generator most reliably commits. "Being overwritten is a signature" (507) is said of fifth-edition modules and is equally true of generated prose.
**Conflicts.** Hartlage's own pro-length argument (742) and Gearing's rewrite from bullets into prose (754) are on the other side; both agree padding is the fault and disagree about where the floor sits.
**Transferable.** YES — implementable as a ratchet: an entry may not grow without a new fact.

### 20. The run-in bold label as the only emphasis, one per paragraph, pointing at what follows
**What it does.** Uses bold solely as a label at the head of a slot or an entry, so a bold word can mean exactly one thing on the page and the eye can scan the record.
**On the page.** "the bold title makes it easy to find the information" (654)
**Rows.** 654, 715, 139, 322, 906, 907, 256 · **Sources.** 5 (Alexander, Necropraxis, Gus L, the AL template, the publisher's Ravenloft guidance) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · compendium-docent · dm-page (herald-pools and chronicle-line carry no bold at all)
**Anti-AI risk.** A bolded label on every paragraph is a visual metronome and reads as a form rather than a record; the publisher's own model (906, 907) is a one-word label and a sentence, which is a different rhythm from a label grid.
**Conflicts.** BAATAG defaults to unformatted paragraphs with no bolding at all (140), each paragraph recognisable from its first words; Campbell strips prose to bolded nouns (690). The section's Reconciliation 1 chooses the middle, and rests it on the publisher's own place text.
**Transferable.** YES — and it discharges the no-game-typography law: because the dossier holds no game object, a bold word can only be a label.

## D. The record: the place, its facts, its people

### 21. The head record: name, size band, who they mostly are, who rules
**What it does.** Opens a settlement on its own registry facts — the name, the count and kind of residents, the leader — before any description, and keeps the count and the leader in labelled fields.
**On the page.** "The leader of the village, Starosta Ernath Noack" (1167)
**Rows.** 1166, 1167, 1193, 511, 1153, 1158, 1159, 940, 1160 · **Sources.** 5 (the Icehaven column, the Boroftkrah column, the Saltmarsh opening via Blackman, the Settlement Tracker, the tracker's coin field) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** The head record is a table and tables are honest; the risk is the sentence that follows it, which becomes "X is a village of Y souls, ruled by Z" forever — a fixed opening move. Vary the entry into the body, never the head.
**Conflicts.** Explicit and unresolved in the corpus: the hardcovers, by two witnesses, open a town on its rank among its neighbours and bury the ruler (905, 883), while the publisher's own free columns open on name, count and people and name the leader at once (1166, 1167). The section follows the columns.
**Transferable.** YES — with the owner's digit law applied: the count is spelled in the prose and lives as a numeral only in the labelled field.

### 22. Registry facts accumulating on a place across the record
**What it does.** Grows a place's record by discrete, dated, checkable facts — a class of building, a founding year, the words chalked on its door, the depth of its well, a census with the year it was taken — each of which is a fact rather than a description.
**On the page.** "Durnan established the Yawning Portal in 1306 DR." (560)
**Rows.** 456, 555, 556, 557, 559, 560, 561, 809 · **Sources.** 2 (Alexander's textual-history pages — the section flags ten of twenty-five rows on ONE page — and the GAZ1 census via a reader's writeup) · **Strength.** MODERATE
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · chronicle-line · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** A fixed inventory of registry facts per place (class, year, door, well, census) is a template, and the tell is that every place has exactly the same five. The transferable is that facts ACCUMULATE unevenly across a record's history — different places carry different registry facts, and some carry one.
**Conflicts.** Feature 8's rule that a cross-reference names a title and never a page: the first Yawning Portal entry is "See Durnan, p. 17" (456), and Reconciliation 5 keeps the shape and drops the page.
**Transferable.** YES — this is the single most directly applicable technique in the author for a PLACE-RECORD engine, and it is the mechanism by which a settlement's record can be a record rather than an essay. Owner-constrained: the year is a label in a table cell or a chronicle line-head, never a four-digit numeral in running prose.

### 23. Omission as information, declared honestly and never by a pretext
**What it does.** Lets the record leave a thing out and say so plainly, and marks the coy dodge (an in-world excuse for a gap) as the fault version of the same move.
**On the page.** "Details of the inn itself have been omitted from these pages" (558)
**Rows.** 558, 457, 524, 572, 605, 835 · **Sources.** 4 (the Undermountain text via Alexander, Appelcline on the Volo dodge, Peterson on the stripped map, Gray via Hartlage; Baur for the don't-be-coy rule) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · dm-page
**Anti-AI risk.** HIGH. "The record holds no entry for X" repeated across a dossier is a metronome and reads as an excuse generator. An honest absence must be rare, specific, and different in shape each time; and it must never stand where the world holds a fact.
**Conflicts.** LIVE, and the critic's first (§6). 1090 (the editors dislike printing that a room is empty) argues for silent omission; the owner's ruling requires the absence line; features 31, 41 and 51 say three different things. Also: what the townsfolk may not know is placed on the record (41), off the record (35) and on the DM page (56) in three different features — the critic's second contradiction, unresolved, and ours to decide.
**Transferable.** PARTIAL — the honesty transfers; the frequency must be governed, and the secrets' home is an open decision, not an inherited rule.

### 24. Common knowledge split from what is not meant to be known
**What it does.** Marks plainly, and typographically, which facts a resident could learn and which belong to the keeper of the record alone.
**On the page.** "divided between common information and bold text" (483)
**Rows.** 483, 572, 588, 880 · **Sources.** 4 (Prince on T1, Peterson, Merwin, Alexander) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · dm-page
**Anti-AI risk.** None as a device; the risk is that "secret" becomes a slot and every place gets exactly one.
**Conflicts.** The three-placements contradiction above (35 / 41 / 56).
**Transferable.** YES for the split; the placement is an open design decision.

### 25. The mundane household catalogue: small commercial facts and one exact fact that implies a story
**What it does.** Records ordinary residents in the houses where they live with small trading facts — a price a little above the standard, what the inn serves — and gives each household one exact fact that implies a story without telling it.
**On the page.** "stashed seventy-three silver coins in the hollow of a tree" (412)
**Rows.** 486, 487, 412, 517, 438 · **Sources.** 4 (Prince, Nelson, Blackman, Lynch) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · chronicle-line
**Anti-AI risk.** Inventory-ism: the "odd Victorian room catalogs" the same reviewers condemn in the same breath (485), and one exact-fact-per-household becomes a fact generator. The technique's own witnesses disagree about where the floor is, which is the honest signal that it needs judgement.
**Conflicts.** Explicit: Prince and Nelson praise the mundane catalogue for the living world it makes (486, 487); Lux and Blackman call the same detail random and more than necessary (697, 516); the thick-block delivery is faulted by the same reviewer who praises the content (484 against 486).
**Transferable.** YES — a settlement's households ARE the dossier's substance, and this is the corpus's only worked model of how to write many of them without a catalogue.

### 26. Relational facts: a web among named residents, and what one town thinks of the next
**What it does.** States relationships between the people and the places of a record so that the place exists independently of any visitor, and gives each settlement an opinion of its neighbours.
**On the page.** "this town hates this town over here" (832)
**Rows.** 438, 486, 832, 512, 531 · **Sources.** 4 (Lynch, Prince, Cook, the Saltmarsh reception via Blackman; Prince again on motivations) · **Strength.** STRONG — but the neighbour-opinion slot rests on **Cook alone** and the section says so, and the reception-of-strangers slot on the Saltmarsh overview alone.
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · herald-pools · chronicle-line
**Anti-AI risk.** A relations matrix produces symmetrical, interchangeable rivalries — the tell is that every town's opinion of its neighbour has the same shape. Cook's own framing is that the worst world-building books never say it at all, which makes it an unusual and therefore valuable move.
**Conflicts.** Feature 60 refuses any slot that sorts the peoples of a place against one another (the tracker has no relations field); the neighbour-opinion slot is about TOWNS, and "kind of stranger" means role — merchant, pilgrim, sailor — never a people. That boundary is load-bearing under the no-stereotype law.
**Transferable.** YES, with the constraint above stated in the generator, not left to the prose.

### 27. The person of record: who, where found, what wanted — plus one playable distinguishing thing
**What it does.** Reduces a person to the minimum a reader can use — role, location, want — and gives them one feature, mannerism or quirk that can be performed.
**On the page.** "I generally like to give an NPC one distinguishing characteristic" (1075)
**Rows.** 1075, 339, 434, 531, 603, 604, 125, 124 · **Sources.** 6 (Perkins, Perkins & Bilsland, Lynch, Prince, Hartlage relaying Cordell/Mearls and Nephew, Alexander) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · herald-pools · chronicle-line · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** HIGH. Three-beat person lines (name, office, want) across forty residents is the purest metronome in the whole design, and "one quirk each" becomes a quirk generator whose outputs are visibly drawn from one list. The cure is the same as 14: the ORDER of the three moves varies, and some residents carry two facts and some one.
**Conflicts.** LIVE (critic §6, third): features 39 and 57 give a person one sentence; feature 50 gives a paragraph of appearance and past plus a want plus an epithet plus a quirk, with the one-line form reserved for a roster. Under the full-form ruling, which is a person's full form? Open.
**Transferable.** YES for the minimum; the full form is our decision, not the author's.

### 28. Naming form fixed, and the same name spelled the same on every page
**What it does.** Names a person once as Title Given Surname followed by the office, follows the world's own usage where it is title-and-surname, and never varies the spelling between pages.
**On the page.** "Townminster Weston hasn't been of any help" (1192)
**Rows.** 1167, 1170, 1189, 1190, 1191, 1192, 890, 928 · **Sources.** 4 (the publisher's free columns, the Phandalin Post handout, the platform's own editorial text — the failure case — the Sigil book via Exposition Break; Elven Tower for currency among readers) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · chronicle-line · herald-pools · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** None from the rule. The valuable part is 1192, a measured FAILURE: the publisher's own platform spells one officer's title and surname two ways inside one article. A generated dossier will do this at scale unless the name is a resolved reference, not a re-emitted string.
**Conflicts.** The parenthetical of creature, alignment, species or gender (890, 1185) is a game object and belongs to the DM page only, which the columns' running sentences do without.
**Transferable.** YES — and it converts directly into a walker: every name in the record resolves to one canonical form.

### 29. Plain, pronounceable names made strange by their use
**What it does.** Prefers simple names deployed so that the world around them seems strange, over strange names that make the world seem familiar, and keeps names sounding of the people who bear them.
**On the page.** "better then strange names that imply a cliched world" (447)
**Rows.** 447, 1079 · **Sources.** 2 (Gus L; Perkins) · **Strength.** MODERATE — 447 is the section's own ONE SOURCE flag for the principle; 1079's western-European-roots limb is flagged by the critic as one tendency among several, so only the sound-human limb is used here.
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · chronicle-line · herald-pools
**Anti-AI risk.** None. Names are where generated worlds are most obviously generated, and this is the corpus's only stated cure.
**Conflicts.** Feature 45 leaves the Gygaxian diction while feature 43 admires Gygax's names; Reconciliation 8 separates them — the name is a noun of the world, the diction around it is the archivist's.
**Transferable.** YES.

### 30. The epithet that names a thing at a glance
**What it does.** Gives a person or a thing a compressed phrase that identifies it before any description does.
**On the page.** "tentacled subterranean trapper" (91)
**Rows.** 91, 1075 · **Sources.** 2 (the 2025 bestiary via an EN World reviewer; Perkins) · **Strength.** MODERATE
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · compendium-docent · herald-pools · chrome
**Anti-AI risk.** HIGH: an epithet per person is a slot, and the two-word adjective-noun epithet is the most imitable shape in the corpus. The section itself notes that the template's two examples happening to be two words is NOT a rule — a caution worth carrying verbatim into our design.
**Conflicts.** Against 19's economy only in the sense that an epithet is an extra; against 27, which already carries the distinguishing thing.
**Transferable.** PARTIAL — valuable for the compendium and for a roster line, dangerous as a universal slot.

## E. The number, the document frame, the withholding

### 31. Numbers as words in prose; numerals only in labelled fields, tables and dated line-heads
**What it does.** Spells every count, span and measure in running prose and confines numerals to the places where a number is a label rather than a statement.
**On the page.** "we spell out whole numbers from zero to one hundred" (266)
**Rows.** 266, 276, 873, 902, 1004, 1048, 1038, 1181, 1153, 1160 · **Sources.** 6 (the house guide, Alexander quoting Rime, the publisher's own encounter column, the in-house SRD/Basic-Rules measurements, the Phandalin boxed text, the Settlement Tracker) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · herald-pools · chronicle-line (numerals in the dated line-head only) · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** None from the rule. One caution: spelled numbers invite spelled-number cadence ("forty households, six mills, three and a half leagues") which is a tricolon waiting to happen.
**Conflicts.** Measured and honest: the house rule stops at one hundred and exempts dates; the publisher's own free column prints "Icehaven is a village of 5,000 souls" in running prose (1166) against the Phandalin boxed text spelling its buildings (1181). **On lawful routes the publisher has no single rule, and the section says the no-digit law is the chair's, not the author's.**
**Transferable.** YES, and already owner law — but transferred as OUR rule with the author's practice as partial support, never as an inherited rule. That distinction is the section's own and must survive into S12.

### 32. The dated registry number: a census with the year it was taken
**What it does.** Gives a population as a dated observation rather than as a timeless truth, so the record shows its own age.
**On the page.** "the official census of 50.000 was taken 15 years ago" (809)
**Rows.** 809, 560 · **Sources.** 2 (the GAZ1 writeup; the Yawning Portal founding year via Alexander) · **Strength.** MODERATE
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · chronicle-line
**Anti-AI risk.** None; it is a cure for the omniscient-present number. The tell it removes is real: a generated dossier states populations as facts of nature.
**Conflicts.** The digit law: the year and the count are numerals in a labelled field and words in the prose.
**Transferable.** YES — and it is the single most useful number-handling idea in the author for a living-history product, because it makes a count a fact ABOUT the record.

### 33. Cross-references by title, never by page
**What it does.** Points to another entry by its quoted title, to a chapter by number and to a table by name, and never cites a page, because pagination moves between printings.
**On the page.** "We avoid page references as much as possible." (10)
**Rows.** 10, 268, 270 · **Sources.** 1 (the publisher's style guide, two versions) · **Strength.** SINGLE
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · compendium-docent · chrome
**Anti-AI risk.** None.
**Conflicts.** Feature 41's endorsement of "See Durnan, p. 17" as the shape an earliest entry may take (456); Reconciliation 5 keeps the pointer and drops the page.
**Transferable.** YES, and it generalises: in a generated dossier every reference must be a resolved identity, never a positional one. (The program has been bitten by exactly this — a verdict keyed by position breaking when the list changed.)

### 34. The pointer entry: the earliest record of a place may be a reference to its keeper
**What it does.** Admits an entry that consists of a name, a class and a pointer to the person who keeps it, as the honest first state of a record that has not yet been written up.
**On the page.** "4. The Yawning Portal (inn) - See Durnan" (456)
**Rows.** 456, 555 · **Sources.** 1 (Alexander's textual history) · **Strength.** SINGLE
**Registers.** dossier-archivist
**Anti-AI risk.** None; it is an anti-manufacture device — a record may point instead of inventing.
**Conflicts.** LIVE (critic §6, sixth): feature 60 bans noun-phrase slot entries and the pointer is a noun phrase with a full stop. Either the pointer is the one licensed fragment or it takes a verb. Open.
**Transferable.** PARTIAL — the shape is exactly what our least-documented places need, but it sits against the full-form ruling and must be reconciled with it before use.

### 35. In-world documents as objects: the clue, not the conclusion; described before they are read
**What it does.** Treats a charter, ledger, lorebook or broadsheet as a physical object in the record — described first, then read — that supplies a clue rather than a conclusion, hedges its own source's boasts, and is set apart from the record's own voice.
**On the page.** "Instead of writing the conclusion, you are writing the clue" (549)
**Rows.** 549, 550, 551, 553, 563, 564, 1188, 522, 523 · **Sources.** 3 (Alexander's two lorebook pages — flagged ONE SOURCE by the section — Appelcline on the Volo device, the publisher's own Phandalin Post) · **Strength.** STRONG on the count; the four lorebook rules are one author.
**Registers.** dossier-archivist (embedded documents) · compendium-docent · dm-page · herald-pools (a broadsheet line)
**Anti-AI risk.** A document frame per entry is a template, and a dateline-plus-verbless-list broadsheet repeated is a metronome. The publisher's own specimen is one article, once (1188), not a form.
**Conflicts.** Cook leaves the solution unsaid (149); Baur states the expected arc plainly and says don't be coy (835). A record is not a puzzle: the section chooses Baur for the record and Cook for the DM page.
**Transferable.** YES — and it is where first and second person are legitimately admitted, inside a quoted object, which is the only licensed breach of rules 1 and 4.

### 36. The broadsheet's dateline: the world's own month and the place, then the damage
**What it does.** Opens an in-world news item on a dateline of the setting's month, day and settlement, and follows it with a verbless list of what happened.
**On the page.** "Ches 25, Phandalin - Ambushed wagons, dead travelers, and a town" (1188)
**Rows.** 1188, 541 · **Sources.** 2 (the Phandalin Post handout; the CBR account of the 2024 gazetteer's calendar) · **Strength.** MODERATE
**Registers.** herald-pools · chronicle-line · dm-page
**Anti-AI risk.** The dateline is a fixed opening move by nature; used for every Herald receipt it is the metronome. It is a FORM for one kind of artefact, not a grammar for a register.
**Conflicts.** The digit law (a numeral day in the dateline) — admitted, because a dateline is a label.
**Transferable.** PARTIAL — good for a quoted broadsheet inside the dossier; not the Herald's default shape.

### 37. Local time kept in the world's own units, with a calendar of its own names
**What it does.** Names the local unit of time and keeps it — loose times in speech, a named clock unit, the world's own months and festival days — instead of importing weeks and o'clock.
**On the page.** "People use loose times; clock hours are called bells." (353)
**Rows.** 353, 355, 286, 541 · **Sources.** 3 (the Realms guide, the house guide, CBR) · **Strength.** STRONG on the count; the tenday/bells vocabulary is ONE WORK by nature and the section says the dossier borrows the SHAPE, not the words.
**Registers.** chronicle-line · dossier-archivist · herald-pools
**Anti-AI risk.** The fantasy-calendar tic: invented month names deployed for their own sake. The transferable is the method (name the unit, keep it, never import ours), not a calendar.
**Conflicts.** Readers note the publisher's own hardcovers still print "week" (381), so the rule is aspirational even at its source; and the register map assigns the rules-side of feature 12 to the DM page while giving the archivist only the method (the critic's tenth, minor, §6).
**Transferable.** YES as method.

### 38. A short house lexicon with its reasons — the method, not the words
**What it does.** Fixes a small list of house words and their capitalisation with a stated reason for each, so that the register has vocabulary law rather than vocabulary habit.
**On the page.** "goodsir (a form of address, not capped)" (289)
**Rows.** 289, 291, 356, 358, 310, 288 · **Sources.** 2 (the house style guide; the Realms guide) · **Strength.** MODERATE
**Registers.** all six
**Anti-AI risk.** None. This is, mechanically, the ban-list plus its rationale — the instrument the surface-text program already plans.
**Conflicts.** None.
**Transferable.** YES as method: a list of our house words with a reason each, letter-by-letter ordered, and never the D&D words themselves.

### 39. Terminology fixity: define a term once and use it the same way everywhere
**What it does.** Uses one word for one thing across a whole document set, and treats a glossary that contradicts its own body as a defect.
**On the page.** "main text and glossary define the Influence action differently" (475)
**Rows.** 475, 310, 288, 1164 · **Sources.** 3 (Alexander on the 2024 glossary, the house guide's word list and alphabetisation, the publisher's own replacement statement) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** all six, chrome and compendium-docent especially
**Anti-AI risk.** None.
**Conflicts.** None.
**Transferable.** YES — and it is checkable by walker, which makes it one of the few style rules here that can be enforced rather than intended.

## F. The voiced line (the Herald's pools), the history, and the measured profile

### 40. The spoken line's budget: one or two sentences, rarely three, never past four, never a paragraph break
**What it does.** Caps anything meant to be delivered rather than read, with a target and a ceiling instead of a single number, and treats a paragraph break inside a spoken block as a fault.
**On the page.** "no more than one or two brief and descriptive sentences" (314)
**Rows.** 314, 900, 1207, 97, 96, 98, 100, 852 · **Sources.** 7 (the AL template, the publisher's own encounter specimen, the 2024 Free Rules, Merwin — ONE SOURCE for the four-sentence ceiling — an EN World thread, Revivify, DM's Workshop, Decker & Noonan) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** herald-pools · chrome (microcopy)
**Anti-AI risk.** HIGH and obvious: a fixed sentence count is a metronome, and every pool line arriving at exactly two sentences is audible within four lines. The corpus's own spread — one to a paragraph, no two witnesses agreeing — is the honest signal that the number must be a band with a distribution, not a target.
**Conflicts.** Explicit and the widest in the corpus: one sentence to a paragraph, ordered by authority in feature 27. The publisher's own three (1207) is a hedged observation of attention, not a cap, and the section says so.
**Transferable.** YES as a band; NO as a number.

### 41. The reaction point: never carry the reader past where they could act
**What it does.** Stops a voiced block at the moment the audience would respond, and breaks longer matter into chunks each of which ends where action is possible.
**On the page.** "What we've identified here is the reaction point" (660)
**Rows.** 660, 377, 659, 875 · **Sources.** 1 (Alexander, one essay across four rows) · **Strength.** SINGLE
**Registers.** herald-pools · chrome
**Anti-AI risk.** None.
**Conflicts.** None internal; it is the reason behind the length rules rather than a rival to them.
**Transferable.** YES — the Herald's receipts are exactly the register this governs, and "actionable chunks" is a directly implementable segmentation rule.

### 42. The material before the creature; the living kept out of the fixed description
**What it does.** Opens on the stone, the streets and the roofs before any inhabitant, and leaves the living out of a description meant to hold across occasions, because where they stand changes.
**On the page.** "mostly made up of steep-roofed cottages, separated by twisting streets" (1168)
**Rows.** 1168, 874, 1089, 174 · **Sources.** 3 (the publisher's own Icehaven arrival, Alexander quoting Rime, the DUNGEON guidelines) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** herald-pools · dossier-archivist (the fixed description) · chronicle-line
**Anti-AI risk.** Applied as an order rule it is a fixed move order: material, then people, every time. It conflicts head-on with 14 (the ogre first) and the conflict is the useful part — salience decides, and the material-first rule holds only for a description that must survive many occasions.
**Conflicts.** Explicit and unresolved across the corpus: 1089 and 174 keep creatures out entirely; 635 puts the conspicuous creature first; 139 starts with inhabitants; 138 does both at different stages.
**Transferable.** YES for the archivist's fixed description (a settlement's built fabric is what persists), with salience governing the Herald.

### 43. Rumours pooled and set apart: some true, some false, each adding local colour
**What it does.** Keeps rumour, hook and quest out of the record's body and pools them after it, with the record's own facts true and the pool's items mixed.
**On the page.** "When in doubt, tell the players things that are true." (1078)
**Rows.** 1077, 1078, 514, 503, 1104, 887, 878, 882 · **Sources.** 6 (Perkins, Blackman, Gus L, the DUNGEON staff, Seed of Worlds, Alexander, Power Score) · **Strength.** STRONG — and note that Winninger's rumour mill (1083) is **excluded** as route-questioned; the rule survives without it.
**Registers.** herald-pools · dm-page · dossier-archivist (placement only)
**Anti-AI risk.** A rumour table is a generator, and Gus L names the failure exactly: rumours that add no local colour or evocative detail (503). Ten interchangeable rumours per settlement is the metronome at the pool level.
**Conflicts.** Reviewers agree such matter does not belong in the record's body and disagree on whether it sits at the entry's end or the chapter's (882 against 887).
**Transferable.** YES — the placement rule and the true/false mixture both, with 503 as the acceptance test for each generated rumour.

### 44. The past enters as a thing standing in the town, not as a paragraph of annals
**What it does.** Shows history through what it left behind — what stands, what is defaced, what is still observed — rather than narrating it, and keeps the unmet history in the record while keeping it out of the entry a reader must use.
**On the page.** "Each adventure implied a cosmology, a history, and a mood" (815)
**Rows.** 815, 1143, 411, 655, 218, 217, 588 · **Sources.** 5 (Ionita on Moldvay, Perkins & Bilsland, Nelson, Alexander, Baur) · **Strength.** STRONG — the crater-and-defaced-statue wording itself is on the questioned route and is **not** cited; its substance stands on 1143 and 815.
**Registers.** chronicle-line · dossier-archivist · compendium-docent
**Anti-AI risk.** None; it is the cure for the history-essay failure. The one risk is the object-of-history slot — every settlement getting exactly one ruin.
**Conflicts.** Explicit: Nelson praises the iceberg of history the players never meet (411); Lynch and Baur call unmet history waste (395, 217). The section resolves by placement — the record may hold the iceberg, the entry may not — which is exactly our dossier/DM-page split.
**Transferable.** YES, and it is directly aligned with the owner's living-history product: the chronicle line holds the annals; the dossier's entries hold what the annals left standing.

### 45. Specificity as the source of authority: a place that could exist nowhere else
**What it does.** Earns a record's authority by exactness rather than by elevated diction, and tests a place by whether its description could be transplanted to any other place.
**On the page.** "an adventure set in Sigil has an entirely different feel" (1126)
**Rows.** 1126, 815, 465, 451 · **Sources.** 4 (Perkins, Ionita on Moldvay, Prince, Gus L) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** all six
**Anti-AI risk.** None. This is the acceptance test for the generic-place tell, and it is measurable: swap the proper nouns between two settlements and see whether either entry breaks.
**Conflicts.** Against 45's diction rule only in appearance; Perkins admits he almost always overwrites (1125) while prizing the nowhere-else place.
**Transferable.** YES — adopt as a gate alongside 7.

### 46. The measured profile, with the SPREAD as the anti-metronome instrument
**What it does.** Supplies an actual measurement of the publisher's own lore prose — mean about twenty-one words with a real standard deviation, about one sentence in nine over thirty, no sentence under eight, no digit, no second person, no semicolon, question or exclamation, contractions rare — as a target profile rather than a rule.
**On the page.** "from the first layer to the ninth" (1005)
**Rows.** 1001, 1003, 1004, 1005, 1009, 1010, 1011, 1012, 1046, 1047, 1048, 1037, 1038, 1039 · **Sources.** 3 documents (SRD 5.1 planes appendix, the SRD pantheon prose, the 2018 Basic Rules specimen), one in-house instrument, one second-seat re-derivation · **Strength.** STRONG on the count of documents; **the section marks the whole profile PROVISIONAL** because the measured text is cosmology, not settlement prose, and names the measurement that would settle it.
**Registers.** dossier-archivist · herald-pools (the Basic Rules specimen's shape)
**Anti-AI risk.** THE central one. A mean sentence length is a metronome by construction; the transferable part is the DISPERSION — a population SD of 7.5 words on a mean of 21, a floor at eight and a tail past thirty at one in nine. A generator that hits the mean and misses the spread produces exactly the flat, even prose the reconstruction exists to cure.
**Conflicts.** The critic's own rough pass over the questioned Greyhawk settlement text read a mean of about sixteen, a floorless distribution and heavy digits — shorter and rougher than the appendix. The profile may be re-cut when the lawful settlement specimens are measured (critic §7, the cheapest open work in the sweep).
**Transferable.** PARTIAL — the method transfers completely and the numbers only provisionally; adopt the instrument, hold the figures loosely, and re-measure against the lawful specimens before pinning any generator to them.

### 47. Tone declared once, and gated
**What it does.** Declares a setting's tone as a property of the whole (hopeful, cynical, tragic) and gates every line against it, admitting humour and tragedy only while they do not overtake the register.
**On the page.** "ring wraiths aren't used for gags" (72)
**Rows.** 72, 245, 348, 333 · **Sources.** 2 (the house style guide, the Realms guide; the Standards for the villainy limb) · **Strength.** MODERATE
**Registers.** chronicle-line · dossier-archivist · compendium-docent · chrome
**Anti-AI risk.** None. A declared tone is a constraint on variation, not a template for it.
**Conflicts.** LIVE (critic §6, eighth): feature 44's "first page states what the place is for" rests on a campaign-purpose row (834), which the owner's whole-setting ruling contradicts; the declared tone is the candidate replacement and this row is the evidence for it.
**Transferable.** YES.

### 48. The content-standards floor: no trait attached to all members, no living faith as an evil analogue, evil named as wrong, facts from the record and not from a search
**What it does.** Sets hard content rules that operate below style: no group attribution, no whitewashing, villainy called wrong, PG, the world left restorable, and setting facts taken from the setting's own record.
**On the page.** "Don't attach specific traits or attributes to all members" (332)
**Rows.** 332, 333, 348, 349, 350, 352, 244, 330, 584, 577, 578, 388, 390 · **Sources.** 7 (the 2020 Standards, the Realms guide, the house guide, Trammell, Garcia quoting the PHB, the 2020 statement via Wikipedia, Gizmodo) · **Strength.** STRONG
**Registers.** all six
**Anti-AI risk.** None. The material-culture-varies-by-region clause (578) is the positive form and it is generative: show the variation by naming the things.
**Conflicts.** None; it supersedes the 2004 PG-13 (Reconciliation 7) and the earlier Realms-only rules.
**Transferable.** YES, and it is the closest thing in the corpus to our own deity doctrine and product-scope laws stated by someone else: faith described as culture, no people contained by one text, no evil drawn from a living religion.

## G. Admired in this author, but NOT transferable to a record of a place

| # | What it is | Why it does not transfer |
|---|---|---|
| N1 | **Boxed text itself — the recital.** The tournament instrument that makes every table's experience the same (the 1979–1982 sequence, features 28–29). | The dossier is read, not spoken. What the box guarantees — that a fact is not omitted — is given instead by the entry's fixed slots. The Herald's pools inherit the device's LAW; the record does not inherit the device. |
| N2 | **The two-sentence spoken budget as a length rule for the record** (157, 851). | It is an observation about oration, hedged by its own authors as qualitative and possibly bogus (671). An entry that is read has a different budget; only the reason transfers — the fact that matters sits first. |

## H. The four contradictions this author leaves open, which are OUR design decisions

These are carried from the critic's §6 as live decisions, not as the section's errors. Each blocks a generator rule.

1. **The empty slot.** Silent omission (1090, 1091 — an area with nothing to say is not an area) or the honest-absence sentence (the owner's ruling)? Our reading above: the DUNGEON rule binds the DM page; the record writes the absence, because the record's silence is a fact about the record. Needs the chair's ruling before the walker can check it.
2. **Where secrets live.** Not on the record (feature 35), on the record but off the public page (41), or on the DM page (56)? Three placements, one case.
3. **The person's full form.** A sentence (39, 57) or a paragraph plus a want plus an epithet plus a quirk (50)? Under the full-form ruling the answer cannot be "a sentence where the entry is a roster" without saying which entries are rosters.
4. **The tiers against the full form.** The three tiers (55) size locales inside a settlement in the section's own examples, and also whole settlements (923). Under the ruling every settlement carries the full slot set, so the tiers must be declared to size locales only — otherwise a hamlet is both a full-form element and a one-to-three-sentence entry.

Two smaller ones: the pointer entry is a noun phrase while feature 60 bans noun-phrase slot entries (technique 34); and feature 44's "first page states what the place is for" rests on a campaign-purpose row and needs replacing with the declared tone (technique 47).
