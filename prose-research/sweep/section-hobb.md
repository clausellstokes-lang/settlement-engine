## A. The record register (what the archivist sounds like)

Section A rests on one article: Matthew Oliver, "History in the Margins", Mythlore 41.1 (2022), pp. 45 to 66, which supplies 23 of the 363 kept rows (40 of 346 before the r7 regrade marked its cross-angle duplicates PARTIAL) and is the only peer-reviewed criticism of Hobb's prose style in the sweep; everything else in the academic angle is student theses (Duits BA, Kok MA, Lagus MA, Moon MA by Research, Jeanneret MA, Freeman DCA), relays (Mandala and Borowska-Szerszun through Wikipedia; Mendlesohn, Shropshire, Crane and Fletcher, Bruening through Elliott's bibliography), one-passage uses (Garnes, Slavova, Bruening), onomastic and thematic papers (Tkach and Volkova, Tkach, Srsic, Hodunok) and corpus papers that measure nothing about her prose (Waumans, Dekker).

**1. Chapter-opening documents composed inside the world, in the voice of a formal, distant history.** Nine sources: Oliver [77, 78, 225, 226, 359] (Mendlesohn's Bede comparison [237] counts with Oliver); Wikipedia's Farseer style section [113]; Duits [137, 397, 408]; Elliott's rereading series [183, 184, 344, 346, 667]; Outcalt [198, 199, 200, 203, 360]; Hobb herself to Moher [3]; the Quaint Book Nook reader [363]; Heck in Asimov's, 1997 [520]; Landon on the later journal-headed chapters [492]. Wording: "largely a formal history written in a distant, scholarly voice" [78]; "opens with the authoritative voice of History" [226]; "short memoirs prefacing each chapter that narrate a fictional history" [113]; "reads exactly like how a textbook of a real world country" [360]; "a brief historical memoir" [520]; "journal entries that he writes of days long past" [492]; and Hobb's account of why, "little prologues at the beginning of each chapter" [3] as her compensation for the first-person limit. Rule: the settlement dossier is this document. It is a public record composed inside the world by an office, in present tense, with the calm of a history that does not know it is being read as a story.

**2. The record cites testimony and public information, never the writer's own relation to the subject.** SINGLE SOURCE (Oliver, one kept row [81], on the Patience epigraph of Assassin's Apprentice chapter thirteen; [80] and [227] are PARTIAL). Wording: "evidentiary quotes from her nursemaids and publicly available information" [81]; "no hint of the author's relationship to her" [80, PARTIAL]. Census: the finder's tally of Elliott's descriptions (a majority of chapter-opening documents untitled and unattributed) is unverified and is not cited as evidence; the verified census rows are listed under rule 4. Rule: when the dossier characterises a person or a house it attributes the fact to a class of witness (the millers say; the roll of the ward records) and never to the archivist's acquaintance.

**3. The record is dry by design, and the personal text outranks it in warmth.** Three sources for the dryness: Oliver [225, 359], Outcalt [199, 362], Duits [137]. Wording: "presumably the primary one, the authoritative one" [225]; "official history and personal myth-making" [359]; "A pretty informative, yet boring, introduction epigraph" [199]; "instead of focusing fully on the facts" [362]; "almost reads like an encyclopaedia, offering factual information" [137]; and for the wording only, "the dry, impersonal, but objective history" [79, PARTIAL] and "The main text is the marginalia" [355, PARTIAL]. DISAGREEMENT: two readers value the documents rather than tolerate them; Elliott calls the device "something I appreciate" [184] and the Quaint Book Nook reader writes "I loved the excerpts at the start of each chapter" [363]. Rule: the archivist stays dry and objective on purpose; warmth is supplied by the chronicle line and the Herald pools beside the record, never by loosening the record.

**4. The kind of document is named, and the kinds are many.** Six Hobb-specific sources: Elliott's rereading series [341, 344, 346, 645, 648, 652, 667]; Hobb to Moher [3]; Wikipedia [113]; Duits [397]; Heck [520]; Landon [492]; plus the Ekman and Taylor mechanism [98, 100, 101] (not Hobb). Two of Hobb's own gazetteer-register openings survive only as quotations inside Tkach: "is to start out with a basic misunderstanding of the area" [443, PARTIAL] and "A more ancient tradition attributes such names to accident, at least initially" [444, PARTIAL]; the article never calls them epigraphs, so they are wording only. Census: the five verified census rows are the ones cited above ([639] gives the corpus as 541 posts); the kind-by-kind tally is unverified. Rule: every dossier block declares what kind of document it is (roll, ledger, minute, letter, saying, reflection), because the reader learns the settlement's literacy from the heading before reading the entry.

**5. Two registers, not one disagreement: the record opens encyclopaedic and slightly archaic; the narrating sentence is clear and to the point.** Rewritten to its pages (chair's note 3). The record register: Duits on the encyclopaedic chapter-one opening [137, 397] and, for the wording only, "Even in English, the language seems a bit archaic, and long" [138, PARTIAL], scoped by the verifier to that opening. The narrating register: Duits comparing Fitz's own sentence with its Dutch rendering, "The English sentence is clear and to the point" [139, PARTIAL]; Hobb on the length of her own sentences, "sentence length is very different from language to language" [386] and "Often Arnaud has to join several English sentences together" [387]; her French translator, "un style très fluide, très clair et très imagé" [415] and "avec un style posé, réfléchi" [425]. The archaic-long-sentence claim about the record register is SINGLE SOURCE (Duits) and rests on a PARTIAL row; the clear-short-sentence claim about the narration has four sources (Duits, Hobb at Phantastik-Couch, Mousnier-Lompré at ActuSF and at Elbakin, the last two one witness). Rule: the archivist's register is the record's, plain and a little formal, with the old-fashioned cadence and none of the ornament; its sentences stay short and additive, so that a reader joining them would be doing the translator's work, and it never borrows the narrating voice's intimacy.

**6. Names are plain native words for the home culture's nobility, sound-foreign for neighbours, plain and unglossed for the low-born; the dossier never explains a name.** Eight sources: Tkach and Volkova [124, 125, 127]; Kok [141, 142, 143]; Duits [140, 406, 407]; Jeanneret [437, 439, 440, 441, 442]; Cartoceti [430, 431, 433, 434]; Tkach 2021 [445, 446, 447]; Mousnier-Lompré at Elbakin [420, 424]; Bock in the New Statesman [312]. Wording: "the names are not fantastical-sounding but English words" [142]; "have the custom to name their children after virtues or traits" [141]; "phonetic foreignness and an absolute lack of transparent semantics" [125]; "tools of direct psychological characterization" [127]; "Les noms des gens de la noblesse" [439]; "ont des noms aux sonorités différentes, qui n'ont pas de sens dénotatif" [440]; "Ho adottato il sistema di non tradurre i nomi palesemente" [431]; "names can embody a character’s virtues" [312]; "the virtue becomes unrecognisable for most Dutch readers" [406]. Withholding a name is itself a move: Pulley on Hobb declining the "narratively efficient thing to do" [285]. The former limb, an office may replace a name (Srsic [128]), is a plot fact about one Liveship character and is parked on the DM page (chair's note 8). Rule: founders and their heirs carry plain words of their own tongue as names (Patience, Steady); neighbouring peoples carry names with no readable meaning; the low-born carry ordinary names; the dossier never explains a name and never lets a foreign name look native.

**7. Kin are described in the same terms as strangers.** SINGLE SOURCE (Mandala relayed by Wikipedia [112]). Wording: "describes his immediate family in the same terms" [112]. Rule: the archivist grants no warmth gradient by relation; the founder's brother is a tall man of the river ward exactly as a stranger would be.

## D. Pace, length, compression

**17. Wording: "both leisurely and intimate" [9]; "construct the ramps up to that exciting scene" [11]; "Sometimes excitement and a quick pace have to be sacrificed" [14]; "usually a lot slower than towards the end of the book" [171]; "so many words are given over to character construction" [172]; "I tend to write longer, more detailed stories" [162]; "revving the engine in the hundred pages before the end" [60]; "the slow pacing here isn’t a weak point to overcome" [282]; "closer the slice-of-life style than to action" [45]; "It is, without question, a slow novel" [489]; "Comparing it to more pastoral family dramas would be more appropriate" [490]; "Hobb gives us months" [480]; "even when they are performing the most routine tasks" [71]; "entertaining after a slow start, sporadically surprising, sometimes subtle" [516]; and, wording only, "may not directly move the plot forward but are very important" [12, PARTIAL]. DISAGREEMENT, five critics counting the cost: Whitehead names "a tendency to meander" [295] as her greatest weakness; Goldstein found one book "tired and repetitious in the earlier book" [264]; Craig, "you wish for more action and less introspection" [487]; Dray reports that "the pacing of the series seems to be the most frequent complaint" [496]; Langford, "although there are a few too many pages" [582]; and Elliott reports the opposite fault, "A sense of being rushed is something I have noted" [347]. Rule: the dossier includes routine entries that move nothing (the tally of the well, the mending of a fence) and lets a large event arrive by accumulation; but each routine entry must be short and exact, or the critics' meander is earned.

**18. Compression is a bridge, not a law: a dull span becomes one sentence; a block may be one sentence; but the cut-everything rule is a short-story rule, and in the novel Hobb sprawls.** Rewritten to its pages (chair's note 4). The bridge: SINGLE SOURCE (Hobb's craft post [255, 256]). The scope: Hobb to the OF Blog, "In a novel, I sprawl, and I know it" [152], and the same interview's short-story sentence, "cut out everything that does not describe the setting" [150, PARTIAL], which the verifier scoped to short stories. Dray on the cost when the bridge is used on an event: "the outcome of the civil insurrection is summarized briefly" [502, PARTIAL]. ## E. Register control, speakers, restraint

## G. Feeling, restraint, and what the record refuses

**32. What is not said carries the weight; a quarrel over a small detail carries standing.** Four sources: Teitelbaum [61]; Vacuous Wastrel [191]; Pulley [285, 288]; Craig [484]. Wording: "what is most important is what is not said" [61]; "So much can be accomplished with only glances" [191]; "but it has these pin-sharp moments too" [288]; "her emphasis on internal drama" [484]; and, wording only, "It says volumes about both of them" [287, PARTIAL]. Rule: the Herald pools carry small disputes over concrete things (whose straw, whose trousers) and let standing be inferred; the dossier records no feelings, only acts and objects.

**33. Reaction is characterisation; the story is how events affect people.** Four sources: Hobb on Writing Excuses [304] and to Pat's Fantasy Hotlist [24] (one witness); Vacuous Wastrel [66]; a reader on Landon's page [495]. **34. No judgment; no themes planned; no moral drawn.** Rewritten: the HuffPost row [48] is now PARTIAL because the page retracts the sentence in the next breath. Two sources, one witness each: Mousnier-Lompré, "pas de jugement chez elle, uniquement un exposé des faits" [416]; Hobb to Orullian, "I don't think about foreshadowing or symbolism or themes" [511] and "the author has to stop making judgments and let the" [509]; and, wording only, "I do not care for 'message' stories, regardless of genre." **35. Repercussions cross the map, and events set off events.** Two sources, one witness: Hobb to HuffPost [49] and to Flood [478]. Wording: "inevitably have repercussions in the Six Duchies" [49]; "You set off events, and then you see coming into" [478]. Rule: the chronicle line lets an event elsewhere echo in this settlement's ledger within a season.

**36. Pratfalls lessen impact; silly is worse than impossible.** Two sources, one witness: Hobb to the Arched Doorway [322] and to Flood [475]. Wording: "which is fun but it lessens the impact" [322]; "silly is more dangerous than impossible" [475]. Rule: no comic business in any register; humour, where it appears in the Herald pools, is deadpan.

**37. Violence and trauma are rendered through their psychology and their bodily cost, not their brutality.** Five sources: Teitelbaum [63]; Borowska-Szerszun relayed by Wikipedia [133]; Langford [65]; Dray [498]; Craig [485]. **38. ## The contemporary record (reception)

The earliest reception in the sweep dissents. Chris Morgan, reviewing Assassin's Apprentice in Interzone 98 (August 1995) under the belief that the author was a man and a historical novelist, found "repetitions, awkwardnesses and bad habits creep in" [514], classed the book as "historically-based fiction with relatively small amounts of fantasy" [517], granted it "an exquisitely poetical phrase which says much of human nature" [515], called it "entertaining after a slow start, sporadically surprising, sometimes subtle" [516] and faulted the ending as "a sudden descent into melodrama of an almost farcical nature" [519]; the row calling this the only contemporary review is PARTIAL for that limb, "Stylistically, the novel is patchy" [513, PARTIAL]. Peter Heck in Asimov's (February 1997) named the chapter-heading device "a brief historical memoir" [520]. Langford in SFX (1997) felt "every blow, sword-cut and fever" [65] and closed with "although there are a few too many pages" [582]. Clute's SF Encyclopedia entry reads the early volumes as "a painfully confessional memoir written and presented as a manuscript" [64], and the 1997 Encyclopedia of Fantasy calls the author "A restless and varied fantasist, ML possesses real talents" [562]. Dray in 2001 recorded "the general glum realism of all of her works" [540] and that "the pacing of the series seems to be the most frequent complaint" [496]. Goldstein in 2010 found the writing "tired and repetitious in the earlier book" [264]. Landon's 2014 verdict on Fool's Assassin, "It is, without question, a slow novel" [489], better compared to "pastoral family dramas" [490]; Flood the same year, "Hobb gives us months" [480]; Craig in 2015, "you wish for more action and less introspection" [487] against "as familiar as those in a novel by Jonathan Franzen" [488]; Whitehead in 2016, "a tendency to meander" [295] against "a gifted communicator of emotions and atmosphere" [296]. The praise that survives is for the household detail, the consequence and the voice, never for the sentence as an object; the two witnesses who praise the sentence itself are her French translator ([415], [425]) and Whitehead ([296]).
