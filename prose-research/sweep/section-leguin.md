The verbless record syntax, described here because features 1 and 21 meet on it. Three verified forms: the filing header with no finite verb [717, 712], the survey line of label, year and director [786], and her own travel prose rendering a stretch of country as bare noun phrases on a repeated frame, "Sagebrush at its intervals. Power poles at their intervals." [884, 'Places Names', Dancing at the Edge of the World, p. 52]. Two handbook strings the critic named as verbless are PARTIAL and cited for their quotations only: "Highly intelligent, fully hominoid nocturnal troglodytes, 120-135 cm. in height" [776], which is verbless but does not close on the height, and "Technology enhanced to Industrial, Point C, during League Mission of 252-254" [777], which the verifier found is not verbless at all but a reduced clause on the participle. So the verified verbless line is a label-and-value string (a heading, a survey entry, a landscape at intervals), never a clause with a suppressed verb.

The shortest record. Le Guin's own account of the minimal record is one line of runes in Carlisle Cathedral, "Tolfink carved these runes in this stone." [876], which she presents as a whole story; it is "pretty close to Barbara Herrnstein Smith's earliest form of historiography-notch-cutting" [877], "it does not really meet the requirement of Minimal Connexity" [878] and has little beginning or end, "Yet I would say Tolfink was a reliable narrator" [879], and "The material was obdurate, and life is short." [880] ('It Was a Dark and Stormy Night', Dancing at the Edge of the World, p. 29; one essay, five rows, single source). Galt's annals, her model annalist, are "kept and dated year by year for 50 years" [906].

Critics on the form: because the Left Hand narrator is a professional cultural analyst, his concern with a thorough account "provides the novel with the characteristic features of an anthropological report" [400] (Theall); fantastic narratives include documents "to create an air of factuality" [642], the narrator of 'Winter's King' is "a historian, telling a familiar story" [643], and Ai interleaves "extracts from Estraven's journal, an anthropological report, and Gethenian legends" [645] (Bittner); Bloom reads the field-notes chapter's narrator as a field investigator "wryly cataloging a weird matter" [652] whose "field notes add a number of sharper observations" [655].

Rule: A block carries the apparatus of a report in words. It dates itself against the record and against landmarks (as of this entry, in the year the mill burned, in the third year of the Reeve) because the contract bans digits; Le Guin's own records date by numbered years and ordinal days, and the ban is the program's, not hers. It names a settlement institution by the settlement's own capitalised term (the Reeve's Court, the Ash Tithe, the Widow's Portion) and does not gloss it in the block that first uses it. It may state a rule of the people as a plain present-tense generalisation with a generic plural subject, a social fact as a rule of the settlement's own grammar or custom, a prohibition as a dated passive naming the authority, and a policy as an agentless passive with the number in words. It states what it holds and does not hold (the archive has only the steward's tally, of which two entries follow) and may name who gave a thing to the record and who keeps it. It cross-refers to another block rather than repeating that block. A block may open on a verbless label-and-value line (a heading, a survey entry, a landscape at intervals), never on a clause with its verb suppressed. The record's precision is a register marker, so the archivist writes a span and a single figure in the same sentence in words, exactly as the field note does (a gestation of eight months and a fortnight, and a range of six to eight). Status codes, numbered maps, percentages and the bare imperative to the reader (note this) are DM-page voice and never enter a block.

### 2. The encyclopedic form: reports, essays, the back of the book, and what it costs a reader

The author's own first-page note routes readers: the explanatory pieces are set apart "where those who want narrative can ignore them" [503]. 'A Description of Earthsea' is "a text that is neither story nor afterword" [658; 752 is the same snippet], "almost thirty pages of Earthsea lore and history" [659], "is told from outside the world of Earthsea" [660] rather than by the historian within (all Cadden, snippet route, now graded at three rows), built "with a scholar's seriousness and eye for detail" [756] (Weissman), covering "history, language, royal dynasties, and dragons" [755] (Weissman), and, in the estate's words, "an account of Earthsea's history, people, languages, literature, and magic" [790]; Wikipedia calls it a reference narrative [750, relay]. Theall on why the form suits her: the outsider is observer as well as participant, which "allows for the particularly descriptive approach" [401]. The cost, from the same readers: the ethnographic sections are "intellectually intriguing, but, really, often a struggle to read" [306, 494]; the book reads "like a writer deciding to include all her notes" [495; 307 is the fullSentence form], and is "more impressive than it is involving" [308]; Eldridge says it should be "read in very small pieces" [316, fullSentence: review body]. Yee's contrast, "Tolkien's world has almost no ethnographic detail" [735], while Le Guin's has no history, is taken up under feature 10.

Disagreement, marked, inside the reception: Eldridge [315, 316], Bonnell [317, 318], Keeley [500, 502] and Attebery [499] take the archival form as its own satisfaction; Horton [306 to 308, 493 to 495] finds it a struggle and a writer's notes left in, though he is certain she did it on purpose. Both verdicts are verified; the dossier inherits both.

Rule: The settlement dossier is this form: a compendium of reports kept by an archivist, with the explanatory matter set apart so that a reader who wants the story can pass it by, and with the reference matter written so that a reader who wants the world may begin there. It therefore inherits Horton's risk, an archive that reads like all the notes. The cure on the evidence is Eldridge's, blocks short enough to be read in very small pieces, and the sentence disciplines of features seven, eight, nine and thirteen applied inside every report. The reference matter (the gazetteer of the settlement's history, people, languages, and customs) is its own document, neither story nor afterword, told from outside the world, and the DM page is where it lives.

### 3. Many voices, one plain frame: the register of an embedded voice is its own, the frame's is not

Support: 17 sources, 20 quotations, 20 rows [310, 320, 386, 388, 416, 417, 483, 491, 586, 637, 645, 660, 662, 677, 716, 719, 725, 728, 789, 905]; three rows are Le Guin's own fiction, the rest critics. The rung-8 flag "each limb is one source" falls on the polyphony limb (Slusser on Cummins [386], Jameson [388], Cummins herself [637], Cadden [662], Wade [716], Maslen [905], six distinct documents) and stands on the dialect limb (Mitchell [416] alone, with Amenta [586] as the only second voice). Inside 'The Matter of Seggri' the captain's log is archaic, "They are ordinary drudges, living in towns which belong" [491], while the frame narrator writes plain modern prose and states its holding in the first person plural, "We have only her report, from which three excerpts follow." [483]. The Left Hand of Darkness is, in Jameson's reading, "a virtual anthology of narrative strands of different kinds" [388]; Wade counts "three different voices" on its first page, of which the archival header is the second [716]; the narrator concedes "The story is not all mine, nor told by me alone" [719]; Bittner names the interleaved materials [645]. Slusser, reading Cummins on Always Coming Home, felt "a strong sense of identity speaking through a controlled polyphony" [386]; Cummins's own words are that the ethnograph model admits "both the voices of the anthropologist and the people being studied" [637]; Cadden finds that its narrative relationships "keep us from fixing on any one narrating voice" [662] and that 'A Description of Earthsea' "is told from outside the world of Earthsea" [660]; Pandora is "a stand in for the author or the future anthropologist" [310, fullSentence: Horton, review body] and holds "a dialogue between herself-as-narrator and a Kesh village archivist" [320]. An editorial note on a neighbouring people "dryly explains how" [725]; Le Guin "purposefully left the accents off Kesh names until the Back" of the Book [728] (Saleem reporting her). Maslen names recording "a diversity of different understandings and ways of speaking" inside one language as "the principal problem that confronts a writer" [905] for her as for her mother. The frame's own steadiness: in A Wizard of Earthsea, per Mitchell, accents and dialects are "referred to but not reproduced on the page" [416] and "Le Guin eschews wide variations of tone or register" [417]; Amenta finds the Polish translator "generally respects and reproduces the different linguistic registers" [586], which attests that the novel employs several; Bucknall: her style "varies from book to book and from character to character" [677], one register per document and per speaker. One reader of Rocannon's World marks the seam between an embedded tale and its frame: the rest of the novel is "cast in much more factual, sci fi style" [789] than the opening Semley story, whose manner the same reader calls a fake folk-tale style (that phrase is in the verifier's note, not a verified string). Three PARTIAL rows are cited for their quotations only: Slusser's "intermingled with interpolated tales, documents, diaries" [648] (his word for the framework is editorial, which is the sharper fact), Cummins's "use the textual model of the ethnograph" [636], and Cadden's "the voice of the anthropologist examining the ways" [661], which the page says of the Back of the Book only.

Rule: The archivist's frame keeps one plain modern register throughout the dossier and states its own holdings in the first person plural of the archive. An embedded voice (a founder's charter, a priest's ruling, a quoted resident, a Herald line, an old tale) may carry its own register, archaic or rough, and stays inside its quotation marks or its labelled block; the frame never borrows it, and the seam between a tale and the record is left visible. Dialect and accent are named (she speaks with the fen vowels) and never spelled. A note in the archivist's own voice is dry. The record admits both the archivist's voice and the people's, and never lets the reader fix on one narrator as the author.

### 4. How the record is made: the story is told first, the chart is drawn, the facts are got right, and the gaps are declared

### 5. Tense: the ethnographic present is a distancing device; narrative keeps the past

Support: 11 sources, 21 quotations, 21 rows [70, 126, 127, 235, 244, 400, 404, 456, 480, 635, 721, 722, 766, 769, 869, 870, 871, 872, 873, 874, 875]; fifteen are Le Guin's own words or her own record sentences, the rest Walton, Theall, Erlich, Cummins and Saleem. The rung-8 flag "4 sources" falls: the tense doctrine now has her two essays and the workshop answer, her own present-tense record sentences at two documents, and three critics. In 'Some Thoughts on Narrative' (1980, in Dancing at the Edge of the World, p. 38) she observes that anthropological reports about people long dead, "whose societies no longer exist, are written in the present tense" [869], that "The present tense takes the story out of time." [870], and, the second source the critic asked for, that the present tense used to make the telling "more actual," actually distances the story" [871]; narrative, by contrast, "does not normally use the present tense except for special effect" or affectation [872], "locates itself in the past" so as "to allow itself forward movement" [873], because "narrative is language used to connect events in time" [874] and "Narrative is a stratagem of mortality." [875] (p. 39). In 'On Rules of Writing' (2003) she faults writers who "abandon narrative past for the inflexible present tense" [126, the verifier's compression] since the "narrative past tense involves and includes past, present, and future" [127], and answers the fear of distance with "But distance lends enchantment" [235, fullSentence: the fourth paragraph]; limited third person as now practised is, like the present tense, "a kind of flashlight beam" [244, fullSentence: answer to Patricia, Navigating the Ocean of Story, Session 1]. Her record sentences do what the essay describes: "It is always the Year One here" [721], "Ki'O society is divided into two halves or moieties, called" [766], "In the stony uplands of the Deka Mountains the farmholds are" [769], all present tense, all standing facts; and the frame narrative of the same forms is past, "The first recorded contact with Seggri was in year 242" [480]. Theall: the analyst's concern gives the novel "the characteristic features of an anthropological report" [400], and its narrator will "make my report as if I told a story" [404]. Erlich, with a linguist, reads the opening sentence of Always Coming Home as past conditional and "emphatic past perfect, implying futurity" [456], which is the ethnographic present's own trick, a past that is our future. Walton on A Wizard of Earthsea: the story is "told entirely within the world, but after the story" [70].

Dissent, marked (the critic's Cummins note, met here): Cummins says Always Coming Home "lacks the objective tone of most anthropological writing" [635], is not organised by topic and holds mainly primary material; Saleem reads the Kesh notes' modal as the opposite of a distancing report, "Again and again, Le Guin used the word "may"" [722] when describing a custom, and the ritual song may be sung four times or five or nine "or as many as you like, or not at all" [723, PARTIAL, quotation only]. So the present tense of her Kesh record is not the objective present of the field report; it is permissive, and the objectivity lives in the form (the note, the attribution, the Back of the Book) rather than in the tone.

Reconciliation (the chair's tense contradiction, and Cummins). The dossier's present-tense contract is not the workshop present Le Guin attacks, a flashlight on a single actor; it is the anthropologist's present she describes as the tense of a report on a society, which takes the report out of time and is itself a distancing device. So the archivist's blocks are present tense by her own account of the form, and that present is a form of the distance she prizes. Cummins and Saleem add the correction: her own ethnographic present is not cold, and it admits "may" where a custom is a custom and not a law. The chronicle line is narrative, language that connects events in time, and it is written in the narrative past, told within the world after the event, as Walton reads Earthsea and as her own frames do. The earlier draft's feature 5 wrote "told within the world, after the story" as a rule for the whole dossier and thereby contradicted its own contract; the rule now belongs to the chronicle line only. One caution: [869] and [870] are her observation and her gloss; [872] opens with "This might be why", a hedged causal claim; the observation is exact and the causal story is hers.

Rule: The archivist writes in the ethnographic present. The present is the tense of a report on a people, and it holds the settlement out of time; it is a distance, not an intimacy, and the archivist does not use it to make a scene feel immediate. A custom is stated with the modal a custom deserves (the households may keep a second hearth) and a law without it (the gate is shut at dusk); the objectivity of the record is in its form, not in a cold tone. The chronicle line, which connects events in time, is written in the narrative past and told from inside the world after the event. A block never switches tense to make a moment vivid.

### 7. Plain is not flat: the journalism trap

Support: 6 sources, 14 quotations, 14 rows [2, 3, 106, 107, 108, 294, 295, 423, 514, 572, 597, 799, 800, 801]; eleven are Le Guin's own words (Elfland p. 8; blog 95, 2015; the Paris Review), and the rung-8 flag "single-author doctrine" falls: Swanwick summarises her four don'ts, Mitchell names the neutral style as a hazard in his own voice, and Marino reads the same distance the other way. Reconciliation with feature 25 (the chair's "telling" contradiction), unchanged. The earlier draft's rule, "any sentence that merely reports is rewritten", pulled against feature 25's "the dossier tells". The fault Le Guin names is not that journalism reports; it is that it is toneless, inexact, and suppresses sensibility. So the walker's target is the toneless sentence, not the reporting sentence. A told sentence with an exact noun and a heard cadence passes; a report with a vague noun and no cadence fails.

Rule: The record register is the dossier's own danger, and it has two walls. An archivist who suppresses all sensibility writes journalism, and journalism cannot make a landscape; an archivist who reaches for the elaborate word to sound old writes a theme park. Every block keeps at least one exact sensory cue (the wet thatch, the cracked ice on the cup) and one exact verb; a sentence that is toneless or inexact is rewritten until the thing it names is felt. Reporting itself is not the fault.
