## I. Word stock

**1. Plain, concrete, current vocabulary; very few actually archaic words.**
Support: 14 rows, 7 works. Le Guin 244, 247, 314, 512, 513, 809; Walker via Wikipedia 108, 322, 866; Rosebury via Wikipedia 51, 265, 324; Simon 104; Alden reporting Shippey 340; Shippey 484.
True wording: "he has no ichor; everything is direct, concrete, and simple" (247); "only a few actually archaic words" (866); "Tolkien's actual vocabulary is not obsolete" (340); "coupling archaic syntax with deliberately ordinary vocabulary" (104).
Rule: The archivist's words are the common words of the present tongue. The antique air of an entry comes from sentence shape and rhythm, never from a rare word.

**2. A nineteenth-century lexical stratum: landscape, position and direction, warfare, descriptive verbs and adjectives, much of it obsolescent when written.**
Support: 9 rows, 4 works. Siepmann 25, 26, 287, 494, 495, 689; Lee reporting Kullmann and Siepmann 35; Kullmann and Siepmann 502; Bolding reviewing them 458.
True wording: "whither, thither, thence, whence, upwards, yonder, afar" (26); "around twice as many key words with nineteenth-century fiction" (35); "common English words are rendered strange" (458); "partly due to three simple lexical causes" (25).
Rule: Words of position and direction from the older stratum (yonder, afar, thence) are admitted to the dossier's roads and lands, at most one to an entry; the warfare stratum stays in the chronicle line. Note the hedge the source keeps: the archaic impression is only partly lexical.

**3. Old words put to new uses: heroic borrowings stretched beyond their old range.**
Support: 8 rows, ONE WORK (Siepmann's corpus paper, repeated across four angles): 27, 33, 111, 116, 288, 333, 496, 501.
True wording: "Branched lightning smote down upon the eastward hills" (27); "he is a linguistic postmodernist" (33).
Rule: A short stock of heroic nouns and verbs (valour, corslet, smite) may be stretched to civic matter, a smitten wall or the valour of a bridge-warden, provided the sentence around them stays plain. The archivist keeps one register while borrowing from several centuries.

**4. Modern core words set in older grammatical patterns.**
Support: 6 rows, 2 works. Siepmann 28, 112, 289, 497, 689; Bolding 458.
True wording: "a black smoke swirled in the air" (28); "modern words placed within 19th-century structural patterns" (458).
Rule: Mass nouns of weather and air take the indefinite article (a smoke, a mist, a wind rises). The strangeness of the record comes from pattern, not from word.

**5. Rare native words that are not archaic, together producing otherworldliness.**
Support: 2 rows, ONE WORK: Siepmann 30, 499 (114 P for the same quotation).
True wording: "atmosphere of medievalism or otherworldliness" (30).
Rule: Terrain and office words are native and rare (fastness, fen, dingle, lore-master, wolf-rider, yesteryear) and never Latinate.

**6. Homely collocations, one heavy intensifier, and hyperbole in a few domains.**
Support: 3 rows, ONE WORK: Siepmann 31, 334, 500 (115 P and 32 P for the same quotation).
True wording: "marked preference for the most homely collocations available" (31).
Rule: The archivist pairs a noun with its homeliest adjective and uses "great" as its one intensifier (great peril, great halls, a great smoke). Size, speed and loudness may run to hyperbole; nothing else may.

**7. Manner-laden descriptive verbs; overused emphasisers.**
Support: 6 rows, 2 works. Siepmann 29, 113, 289, 333, 498; Kullmann and Siepmann 686.
True wording: "abundant use of descriptive verbs" (29); "around seven times more common than in modern fiction" (686, of the adverb "even").
Rule: The verb carries the manner (loom, gleam, plod, stride, cower); adverbs of manner are struck. The emphasisers very, so, too and even are the author's tics: permitted in the Herald's pools, rationed in the dossier.

**8. Precise technical rare words with a job, against ornamental archaism.**
Support: 3 rows, ONE SOURCE (Drout): 165, 462, 467. Commenters on the same post dispute the example ("thrawn" is current Scots, note to 462).
True wording: "merely an ornamental archaism" (165); "precise technical terms" (467).
Rule: Every rare word in the dossier names a thing exactly (eyot, louver, laving); a rare word that only colours is struck.

**9. Monosyllabic simplicity; native coinages before Latinate words; no colloquialism, no false modernity.**
Support: 4 rows, 3 works. Shippey via Lee 44; Tolkien on Beowulf via Wikipedia 239, 568; a Goodreads reader 310 (amateur, weight accordingly).
True wording: "a simple, monosyllabic style" (44); "colloquialism and false modernity" (239); "\"unlooked-for\" instead of \"unexpectedly.\"" (310).
Rule: Prefer the short native word and the native compound to the Latinate one, striking to hitting, unlooked-for to unexpected, but only where the native word is still current. Flag: the monosyllable claim rests on ONE scholar and one amateur count.

**10. Terseness: the older English deletes the little empty words and puts the weighted word first.**
Support: 10 rows, 4 works. Tolkien, Letter 171, via 17, 18, 339, 591, 594, 595; Simon 105; Gordon 118; Shippey 485; TM&M 810.
True wording: "modern English is regrettably looser, more full of little 'empty' words" (17); "Real archaic English is far more terse than modern" (105); "a lot of little 'empty' words" (594); "a choice of styles" (595).
Rule: The archivist strikes the empty words (there is, it was, very, that) and lets the emphasised noun stand first. English leaves a choice of styles without any risk of unintelligibility; refusing the choice is a parochialism of time.

## II. Sentence shape

**11. Fronting and inversion as the archaic signal, in syntax rather than vocabulary.**
Support: 15 rows, 8 works. Lee 37, 38, 39; Rosebury via Wikipedia 53, 96, 691; Turner via Wikipedia 329, 760; Tolkien 594; Kullmann and Siepmann 657, 678; Simon 98, 99, 100; Shippey 476, 484.
True wording: "the object is moved to the beginning" (37); "In rode the Lord of the Nazgûl" (38); "Weapons they have laid at your doors" (329); "fronted subject complements followed by copular be and the subject" (678); "the inverted syntax creates delay and suspense" (39).
Disagreement: Holmes reports the corpus data of Kullmann and Siepmann as tending to refute "the prevalence and significance of archaic inversions" (666), and Turner notes the two authors use the word differently, Siepmann distinguishing inversion from fronting (679). The close readers (Lee, Rosebury, Simon) find inversion at every climax; the corpus finds it rarer than criticism assumes.
Rule: The dossier fronts the emphasised noun once in an entry ("Helms too they chose"); fronting marks a turn and is never the default order. Fronting cannot survive into French and is unmarked in German (329, 760), so it is a device of this tongue only.

**12. Parataxis: clauses chained by "and" without causal connectives at marked moments; loose semantic fit.**
Support: 14 rows, 6 works. Turner via Wikipedia 54, 55, 158, 323, 690; Shippey 159, 254, 482; McIntosh citing Garth 157, 692; Halbrooks 295; Kullmann and Siepmann 656, 657, 683; Bolding 663.
True wording: "loose semantic fit" (159); "stylistically marked" (158); "breathless excitement" (157); "sentences without subordinate clauses or causal conjunctions" (54); "there are few hypotactical sentences" (657); "Clauses and phrases coordinated by and feature prominently" (683).
Rule: At the moment of an event the chronicle line chains short clauses with "and" and withholds the causal word ("by stock or by stone"); the archivist's settled description may subordinate. Bolding's row (663) gives parataxis to the riders and hypotaxis to the city as cultural registers: a rural settlement's pools run paratactic, a chartered town's hypotactic.

**13. Short stark sentences at crisis.**
Support: 3 rows, 3 works. Shippey via Lee 44; Fandomentals 354; Kullmann and Siepmann 656 (41 P for the quotation "Tolkien deliberately condensed the prose").
True wording: "pairs nicely with a simplicity of prose" (354); "short sentences, parataxis, inversion and repetition" (656); "There was a crack." (354).
Rule: When the chronicle records a death, a fire or a flight, the sentences shorten to five words and lose their subordination.

**14. Clause-level habits measured by corpus.**
Support: 15 rows, ONE WORK (Kullmann and Siepmann 2021, the narrative-syntax chapter, reached through Google Books search-inside): 668, 670, 671, 672, 673, 674, 675, 677, 678, 680, 681, 682, 683, 684, 685. The reviewers (Holmes 665, 666; Turner 662, 679; Bolding 663, 664) corroborate the chapter and its headline findings, not each micro-habit.
True wording: "underuse of personal pronouns in sentence-initial position" (672); "reference by name becomes a frequent necessity" (673); "the overuse of then, presently or suddenly in clause-initial position" (677); "the stative passive is far more common than the eventive" (680); "existential clauses with a wide variety of verbs" (682); "underuse of modal verbs" (674); "short dialogue-framing sentences (said Frodo)" (684); "It seemed that he had made some decision" (671).
Rule set for the archivist: begin a sentence with a name or a noun, not a pronoun; repeat the name rather than reach for the pronoun; sequence with "then" and "presently"; prefer the stative passive (the wall is built of stone) and the passive with a prepositional complement (the gate was shivered in many pieces); write "there came" and "there was held" for events and never "there is" for mere presence; place a participial clause first, sometimes bare (he, bowing low, bids them farewell); report a thought only by a that-clause (the reeve holds that the ford is safe) and never by free indirect speech; open a paragraph with "at last" and a short verb; ration the modal verbs.

**15. Repetition, variation, the delayed adjective, apo koinou, and alliteration crossing from verse into prose.**
Support: 11 rows, 8 works. Lee 38, 40, 42; Drout 464, 465; Brauer 69, 309; Halbrooks 305; Venkatesh 308; Hall 142; MCS 307.
True wording: "Horns, horns, horns" (42); "seeing the further opportunity for alliteration" (40); "Fey he seemed, or the battle-fury of his fathers" (305); "tiny re-constructions of sentence meaning" (464); "the recurrent image of the rain as a curtain is significant" (465); "makes its way into Tolkien's prose" (69).
Rule: Triple repetition and the delayed adjective ("the orcs innumerable") belong to the chronicle line at a battle or a founding, with the identity withheld to the last sentence. Alliteration crosses into prose only where a people's verse has just been heard, and a martial passage may run on an iambic base and shift to four stresses (308). A recurring image is justified by its recurrence across the settlement's entries, not by novelty.

## III. Rhythm

**16. The two-beat rhythm of stress and release; the walking gait; cadence that follows the breath.**
Support: 20 rows, 7 works. Le Guin 58, 59, 60, 61, 62, 65, 149, 150, 152, 250, 348, 352, 518, 521, 522; Drout via Wikipedia 325; Zimmer via Wikipedia 66; Ephraim 311; Nepveu 345; re-read commenters 346.

**17. Polarities that reverse; directionality; a structure both journeylike and architectural.**

## V. Narrator and point of view

**28. The self-effacing, impartial narrator; no asides, no talking down, written for no class of reader.**
Support: 22 rows, 10 works. Thomas via Nepveu 70, 508, 693; Thomas via Kullmann and Siepmann 507; Litschko 71, 169, 697; Mambrol 168, 358; Poveda 694; Tolkien 211, 214, 549, 572, 574, 575, 577, 579; Drout 163, 461; Shippey via a summary 257; a forum 360.

## VI. Depth and description

## VII. The civic record: frame, prologue, appendices
