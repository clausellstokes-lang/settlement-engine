## Features

### 1. The strange words are real, old and rare; none is coined; each stands in the noun slot of a plain sentence whose context makes its sense plain

Support: 23 distinct sources. Wolfe himself six ways: the Person 1998 interview (19, 230, 231, 232, 233), the appendix to The Shadow of the Torturer relayed by Wikipedia (239, 240, 241, 1121, 1122), Keeley (297) and Wright (454, 1144); The Castle of the Otter via Wright (453, 988, 987); the Thrust 1983 interview, asked whether any term was invented: "None. Some of the terms are Latin" (952); the Jordan 1992 interview, "No, but some of them are typos" (1107); Wolfe's essay on the library, whose catalogue lists bindings by material without a figurative comparison (1016). Critics: Ewing, "cut back and simplified, almost Hemmingwayesque" with exotic nouns only (107); Gerwel (101); Auerbach (139, 1090, 1091); Khanna (131); Keeley (295, 296); Wowra (250, 251, 348, 349, 350); Andre-Driussi (330, partial); Macdonald (367, 368, 369); Attebery, "Wolfe generally invents by reusing" what is forgotten (792); Wright on archaisms as "agents of estrangement to render the fictional Urth alien" (986, 990, 991); Ipsifendus, "Everything archaic to Severian is rendered as Latin." (379); Hunt (320); the schicksalgemeinschaft reread (340); Gordon on The Devil in a Forest, where Wolfe "tactfully explains them in the course of furthering" plot or character (802).

Wording: "in no case have I done so" (239, 1121); "intended to be suggestive rather than definitive" (240, 297, 454, 1144); "every word in the book appears in a dictionary" (295); "convey the flavour of an odd place at an odd time" (453, 988); "it will be clear to the reader what is meant" (232).

Disagreement: Sucharitkul has "neologisms mingle with resurrected antique words" (548), against Wolfe's flat "None." (952); Macdonald hears the vocabulary as new, formed "by changing a vowel or suffix" (367); Gerlach's Long Sun article (159, partial, quotation only) records ordinary words "applied in unusual, usually comical ways" and, per the verifier, coinages in that series. The counter angle carries the cost: Auerbach, "What it doesn't do is make the writing beautiful" (1092); Roberts via Aramini, readers who find the "archaic idiom a deterrent" (1145); Keller, "dense descriptions and slowing adjectives" (524). The defence, from four independent readers, is that the ornament sits in the noun slot and the sentence around it is plain (107, 367, 368, 1091).

Chair's note applied (feature 1 against feature 36, which Wolfe's diction): the principle is the New Sun's and Wolfe never relaxed it in his own words (952 in 1983, 1107 in 1992, 239 in 1980); what changes after 1980 is the density, not the rule. The measure angle's rare-word rate outside the top ten thousand English words runs 10.7 (1972), 13.0 (1980), 8.3 (2013), 6.1 (2015) per cent over all sentences, and the out-of-dictionary rate 4.2, 5.8, 3.8, 3.3 (metrics-wolfe-measure.json; the individual figures are PARTIAL rows 738 to 749 because the verifier's replication moved the decimals, but the ordering replicated and 746 is kept). So the dossier takes the New Sun's kind of word at the later Wolfe's rate: the Severian density is the ceiling, the Smithe density the floor.

Rule (archivist): the archivist names the settlement's offices, tools, garments and beasts with real, old and uncommon words and invents none; each such word is a noun, and it stands in a short plain sentence whose other nouns make its sense clear; the word suggests the thing rather than defines it; the dossier carries few such words to a page, and never two in one sentence.

### 2. The dictionary method: from the Latin or Greek word to the form an earlier hand anglicised

Support: 1 source, the Person 1998 interview, found at two hosts (19, 233). Flagged: single source. Wolfe's Qabbalistic reading as "a place that I could steal ideas and names from" (1108) is the same method for names.

Wording: "someone will have anglicised this term" (19).

Rule (DM page, word-pool construction): to name an office or object, the builder finds the Latin or Greek word for it and then the English form some earlier hand made of it; the pool holds the anglicised form, never the raw Latin; a name may be lifted from an old book of lore the builder does not believe in.

Rule (archivist, Herald): the archivist's sentence runs a dozen words or so, and a third of his sentences are under eight; no sentence passes forty; one sentence in ten at most carries a bracket or an aside, and most pages carry none; the sentences are uneven in length, a run of short ones broken by one long one, never a steady march; no word can be struck without loss; the surface is clear and the depth is elsewhere.

### 4. Show, never state: a person or an institution is known by what it does

Support: 3 distinct speakers, one of them Wolfe relayed four ways. Wolfe in Clarkesworld 2008 (23), his numbered advice in Black Gate 2010 (35) relayed by Stewart (345), and on the novice's paragon whose virtues "are only talked about. Never shown." (698); Dirda, independently, on the method, "what a character does, but not what that action means" (1196); Clute on Silk (203, partial, quotation only: "that goodness is in the details").

Chair's note applied: the prior draft rested this on one speaker; Dirda's 1989 review (1193 to 1198) is a second, and Clute's clause a third in substance though its limb failed. It is no longer single-speaker, but two of three are the same sentence said twice.

Wording: "You simply show it and shut up." (35); "I know I can't say it at all" (23).

Rule (archivist): the dossier never writes that a family is cruel or a guild is honest; it records what the family does, what the guild's ledgers show, and what the deed cost, and stops there.

### 5. Fiction demands the concrete; the ship, the cargo and the shoal are named; and the plan of a building exists from the moment two entries could disagree about it

Wording: "Like poetry and song, fiction demands the concrete." (27); "Could you see the street from this window" (263); "virtually everyone is interesting at work" (830).

Chair's note applied (feature 5 against 37): Wolfe drew the plan mid-draft, when he "found myself getting tangled up" (262), and does "almost none" of his research before writing (37); the plan is not a pre-planning law but the method's repair. Rule (archivist, Herald): every entry names the street, the building, the office and the object rather than the category; a wreck is a named ship carrying a named cargo on a named shoal; the settlement's people are seen at their work, the tanner at his pit and the clerk at his desk; the plan of a house or a hall is drawn the first time two entries need the same wall, and after that no entry contradicts it.

### 7. Context over exposition; and the later hand may gloss only in the apparatus, never inside an entry

Support: 12 distinct sources. Khanna (128); Noel (341); Elliot (315, 316, 317, 319); Gerlach (157); Kelly (361, 363); Macdonald, "the mysteries of his trade" (366); Wolfe's letter to Cooney, "lumps of prose like uncooked dumplings" (355); Wolfe in Thrust, "As long as you stick to explaining, you're going to" have a lump somewhere (949); Sini (286); Dirda, "he gives you the names of things, but not what they are" (1195); Gordon on the tactful in-narrative gloss (802); Clute on Wolfe's "refusal to unpack in easy terms" (202, 1126); Gerlach on inhabiting the world "much as the characters do" (156, partial, quotation only).

Exception the set records: an outsider narrator licenses explanation; Wolfe made The Knight's narrator an American who "would not have heard about the Aelf" (69). Chair's note applied (feature 7 against 15, may the later hand gloss): the dossier is a document with a stated origin, often a translation by a later hand (feature 15), and Wolfe's own translating hand does gloss, but only in a paratext: the appendix that names peltast and exultant as substitutions (241, 297), the foreword that explains how place names were rendered (332, 427), the preface that warns of "possible errors of translation" (923), the glossary that defines arete (929). Inside the entries the hand never glosses; the one in-text explanation Gordon finds is spoken by a character in the course of the action (802). Decision: the later hand may gloss in a foreword, an appendix or a glossary set apart from the entries, and nowhere else; an outsider's voice inside the record explains only what an outsider would ask.

Rule (archivist): nothing inside an entry is explained that the settlement's own clerk would pause to explain; an office is named and its purpose is inferred from what it does; a rite is recorded, not annotated; a gloss, if the record must carry one, lives in a note set apart from the entries, in the voice of the hand that copied the record and not of the clerk who kept it.

So the sentence "a dossier that hides its central fact from every reader has failed" is not a Wolfe finding and is rewritten as a product rule, owner-signed under the legibility law, and marked as such on the DM page. Andre-Driussi's "probably not even recognized as a mystery by most readers" (1111) is the measured cost.

Rule (archivist): every fact the dossier withholds leaves its evidence in the record, a married name in a later entry, a book shelved under a letter, pork on a menu, a maid calm in a rite; the evidence is entered before the reader needs it, in the plain manner of everything around it, and is never marked as evidence; the evidence is objective fact a second witness could check, never a mood. Product rule (DM page, not Wolfe's): at least one reader in three must be able to recover a dossier's central hidden fact from the record alone; Wolfe himself accepted less, and the product does not.

### 10. A clue is given once; a world-fact may recur, and its recurrence is itself a signal (single speaker for the first half)

Support for once-only: one speaker, Wolfe, five ways: the Jordan 1992 interview, "I try not to leave a clue more than once" (1098, and 0 partial), "you are writing for somebody that is a lot dumber" (1099), Auerbach quoting the same passage (505), WolfeWiki relaying it (287), Barach relaying it (312). Chair's note applied: flagged single-speaker; all five rows are Wolfe. Support for recurrence as signal: Aramini, the tunnel dogs called gods, "as repeating an unrelated fact in the text will objectively state" the truth (145), and late Wolfe playing less fair for want of "metatextual repetitions" (142, partial, quotation only); Gevers's hologram, "any fragment contains the essence of the whole" (329, 1072); Beck, "Names, objects, and moments are revisited, over and over" (1219); Lackey counting one ordinary noun "ten times in about three pages" and reading the count as planted (1180); nastler's recurring tableau (598); Lycanthrosaurus, a load-bearing name that "occurs only once" at the end of a long sentence (1167, 1168).

Chair's note applied (feature 10 against Aramini 142 and 145): the two are not in conflict once the objects differ. Wolfe's "once" is about a clue to the puzzle, a fact whose only job is to point at the hidden solution; Aramini's, Beck's and Lackey's repetition is of world-facts and motifs, whose recurrence is how the record states a truth it never asserts. A dossier does both.

Rule (archivist, chronicle line): a fact that points at a hidden answer appears once in the dossier and is not restated in a summary; a fact about the settlement itself, a chapel still used, a name always spelled one way, a maid called a maid, may recur across entries, and when it does the recurrence is the record's way of stating what no entry says; the reader's work is finding more traces, not reasoning harder from one.

### 11. Small details presage large revelations, and yet not everything means very much

Chair's note applied (feature 12 sided with the minority): the rule is kept, as a DESIGN CHOICE and not a finding, for a reason the sources do not supply: the dossier is the ground truth the game state is reconciled against, so the archivist's own voice may omit, misread and misjudge but may not enter an event that did not happen, or the record could not be checked against the world. What Wolfe does that the design permits is the lying witness: a testimony, petition or confession quoted inside the record may lie outright, in the manner of a character in dialogue (118, 1133), and the archivist enters it without comment. The choice is recorded on the DM page with the tally above.

Rule (archivist, design choice): the dossier is an account kept by a named office or hand, never an omniscient view; its errors are the errors of a clerk trying to be accurate; the archivist's own sentences omit, misread and misjudge but never invent an event; a voice quoted inside the record may lie, and the record does not say so; a reader who asks who keeps the record, when, why and for whom is asking the right question.
