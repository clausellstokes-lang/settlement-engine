# check-leguin.md — adversarial check of six characterizations against the VERIFIED Le Guin section

Inputs read in full: `sweep/section-leguin.md` (346 lines) and `sweep/kept-leguin.json` (550,702 bytes,
454 rows). Cross-read: `sweep/partial-leguin.json` (87 rows) to confirm that nothing I credit is a
PARTIAL row wearing a verified coat.

Integrity check first, because a characterization can only be as good as the rows under it:

- All 454 kept rows carry a verified verdict: 450 VERIFIED_VERBATIM + 4 VERIFIED_SUBSTANCE. No
  CONTRADICTED verdict exists anywhere in the sweep (Coverage table, total row).
- Every claim index I cite below was looked up in `kept-leguin.json` by index. All present, all
  verified. The one index I probed that is NOT kept is [167] (PARTIAL, "merely counting stresses is
  not going to give us any solid") — it is not used as support anywhere below.
- Two structural caveats from the section that bear on every row below:
  1. **232 of 454 kept rows (51.1%) are Le Guin's own words.** Half the evidence is self-description.
  2. **No primary-measure lane has landed.** `primary/PROVENANCE.md` records no fingerprint of her own
     sentences, so every quantitative feature (14, 15, 16) is her doctrine derived from counts of
     *other* writers, and the section sets no numeric target on her authority. Nothing below may be
     turned into a threshold.
- Source concentration that bites: 'From Elfland to Poughkeepsie' (one document, one host) and
  chapter one of *Steering the Craft* (one document, reached at lithub) carry a large share of the
  doctrine rows. Where that matters I say so per row.

## 1. Economy — **SUPPORTED** (21 distinct documents, 18 hosts, 37 verified rows)

Her doctrine, 6 documents: "Listing is not describing. Only the relevant belongs." [207]; "what you
leave out is infinitely more" [258]; "white space around the word, silence around the voice" [206];
"Overcrowded descriptions clog the story and suffocate themselves." [261]; never "ten vague words
where two exact words will do" [260, 208]; revise to "cut and recombine until what's left is what
counts" [210]; "the leaving-out is half the art" [285]; "if you don't have to mention the doorknob you
don't" [620]; on O'Brian, "Everything the reader needs to know is included, but nothing more." [262],
"The sensory details are intense, brief, precise." [263]; the halving exercise [211]; "And every word
counts." [10]. Rows [262, 263] are her praise of another author and are doctrine, not measured practice.

Independent confirmation on her own sentences, 13+ documents: "There's never a wasted word, and all
the words are right" [71] (Walton); "not a single word being used unnecessarily" [153] (Eichner);
"every word of Le Guin's is carefully weighed" [145] (Mahon); "She can say more in twenty words than
almost anyone" [340]; "Earthsea leaves a lot up to the imagination" [146]; laws "more hinted on than
explained" [372]; "you can do successful worldbuilding without going into detail" [373]; "neither long
nor wordy" [370]; the antithesis of video-game verisimilitude [74] (Plotz via Burt); "dialogue is
sparing" [415] (Mitchell); a closing "all the weightier for consisting of only three sentences" [397]
(Suvin); "a sense of depth, of unspoken further implication" [300]; details "doled out so skillfully"
[363]. Her own regret over *The Lathe of Heaven*, "They talk too much. They explain things too much."
[623, 893] — one sentence, two rows, counted once.

**Marked exception, adversarial.** Economy is not universal across her books in the verified record.
Horton on *Always Coming Home*: the ethnographic sections are "intellectually intriguing, but, really,
often a struggle to read" [306, 494] and the book reads "like a writer deciding to include all her
notes" [495], "more impressive than it is involving" [308]; Eldridge says read it "in very small
pieces" [316]. That is a verified critic charging the opposite of economy at book scale. Her own
caution against the other ditch stands too: a narrative can be "over-elliptic and over-written" [240].
Economy is verified as a sentence-level and Earthsea-level property, contested at the *Always Coming
Home* book level. The section already carries both verdicts (feature 2, "Disagreement, marked").

## 2. Rhythm — **SUPPORTED** (24 distinct documents, 15 hosts, 76 verified rows; 11 documents are critics reading her own sentences)

Her own, verbatim: "The sound of the language is where it all begins." [85]; "The test of a sentence
is, Does it sound right?" [118]; "A good writer, like a good reader, has a mind's ear." [87]; "Pace and
movement depend above all on rhythm" [281]; "the rhythms of words are bodily rhythms" [236]; "It's a
very long beat, the rhythms of a prose work" [305]; "changing sentence rhythms embody and express the
characters' emotions" [122]; judged in "the movement of the syntax, and the rhythm of the sentences"
[9, 110]; "Rhythm is repetition." [172]; coherence by "inner references and backward-looking or
forward-looking" semirepetitions [173]; "If the rhythms become predictable, the coherence of the story"
goes mechanical [174].

Critics on *her* prose, 11 documents: "carefully balanced and musical" [36, 133] (NEA); "the musical
cadence of an oral storyteller" [137]; "one of the best books for reading aloud" [71, 200] (Walton);
"spare and lyrical, just begging to be read aloud" [358]; "her ear for rhythm turns a description of
the midsummer Long" Dance into a dance [360]; "an almost biblical rhythm" [220]; a "gift for
alliteration" [361]; Wyman on Omelas, the "long lines of the first paragraph mimic the festival parade"
[326] and a chiastic sonic flip [327]; Kelso on Tehanu and Lathe — "almost somnambulistically smooth"
[54], "no rhythmic superfluities" [59], variations for pause and emphasis [53], vocabulary as
"rhythm's Siamese twin" [56], "the very large, long wingbeats" of the dragon's flight [55]; Bell on the
Rocannon's World sentence, "the parallel construction of the two rhyming sets" [213, 214/336].

**Two adversarial limits.** (a) Feature 14's mechanics are near single-source — 16 of 17 rows are one
book (*The Wave in the Mind*, two essays) — and **every count in it is of another author** (Tolkien,
Twain, Woolf, Austen, a textbook), never of her own prose; she disclaims them herself: "too subjective
for any conclusions at all to be drawn" [166]. (b) Feature 18's repetition engine is 15 of 28 rows on
one Tolkien chapter, labelled doctrine, not measured practice. So *that rhythm governs* is supported
from many independent readers of her sentences; *any numeric rhythm target* is not supported by
anything, and the section explicitly withdraws the one target an earlier draft set.

## 3. Ordinary vocabulary carrying worldbuilding weight — **PARTLY** (conjunction: 3 documents; limb (a): 13 documents; limb (b): 3 documents)

The characterization is a conjunction and the two limbs verify separately while the *link between them*
does not.

Limb (a), that the vocabulary is plain/ordinary — well sourced, but the sources say **plain and
exact**, not *ordinary*: "plain, strong, and exact" [35, 131] (Dirda/NEA); "plain, strong, and direct,
without obvious flourish" [139]; "elegant diction" [41]; "austere but vivid" [43]; "clarity and
evocative precision" [45] (SFE); "clear syntax, precise word choice, natural emphasis, and unobtrusive
grammar" [154]; "The language is precise and descriptive" [371]; "her spare, clarifying prose" [379].
The one row that actually measures ordinariness of word stock in her own prose is **Brogan alone**:
she "relies heavily on words with Germanic roots to convey the rawness of her windswept world" [142] —
and the section itself flags "Brogan is the single source for that limb" (feature 16). Every other
vocabulary row is her count or reading of **another** author: Tolkien's "flexibility, its variety" [8];
"the vocabulary is simple and familiar" of a **Hurston** passage [121, 282]; the Tolkien sample's 45
monosyllables of 72 [165]; the textbook [168]; Twain [169]; Eliot's "all broken monosyllables" [889].

Limb (b), that words carry the world — supported but thin and almost entirely self-description:
3 documents, 8 of 10 rows her own words. "Exact and vivid words make an exact and vivid world" [226];
"The imaginary must be imagined, accurately and with scrupulous consistency." [296]; "A fantastic
setting requires vivid and specific description" [294]; a generic landscape "must be brought to life by
the language of the telling" [297]; in fantasy every seam is exposed so "every word counts" [10]; the
glimpse of "a much greater landscape, a long history" [223, 224].

**Why the conjunction is only PARTLY, and where it is actively cut against.** No verified row names
*ordinariness* as the world-carrying property. Where the section's own rows assign the load, they
assign it elsewhere:

- To **exactness and vividness**, not plainness: [226]'s true wording is "The more realistic, exact,
  'factual' detail … the more plausible the world will be. … Exact and vivid words make an exact and
  vivid world."
- To **rhythm**, explicitly, in the one place she comments on simple familiar vocabulary: [121, 282] —
  vocabulary "simple and familiar", and "above all the rhythm is powerful".
- And plainness *without* exactness is named by her as a **failure mode**, in four rows: "It is a fake
  plainness. It is not really simple, but flat." [2]; "the scenery is cardboard, or plastic" [3]; "the
  rocks, the wind, the trees are not there, are not felt" [106]; "It is journalistic prose." [107,
  799], whose "suppression of the author's personality and sensibility is deliberate" [108, 800]; "A
  toneless, inexact language is incapable of creating landscape" [295]. Feature 7 exists precisely to
  name this trap.

What does support the conjunction, at 3 documents, is demonstration rather than assertion: verified
verbatim specimens of her own ordinary-word sentences doing world-work — "rock, ice, sky, and silence:
nothing else" [339] and the Gethenian "common table implement … with which you crack the ice that has
formed on your drink" [216, 338] (Bell); "It was built of uncut rocks roughly mortared" [346]
(Tomlinson quoting *The Dispossessed*); plus Brogan [142] naming it. Three documents, one of which
names the mechanism.

Verdict: PARTLY. Adopt as "plain **and exact** vocabulary, with the load shared by exactness and
rhythm". Adopting "ordinary vocabulary is what carries the world" as stated would be adopting an
inference the corpus does not make and which [2, 3, 106, 107, 121, 282, 226, 295] point away from.

## 4. Distance without costume — **SUPPORTED** (distance: 10 documents, 24 rows; costume-refusal: 8 documents, 20 rows)

Distance, verified: "But distance lends enchantment" [235]; limited third as now practised is "a kind
of flashlight beam" [244]; "Distrust anybody who says fiction must use only limited third person."
[245]; the detached-author mode — "values and judgments can only be implied indirectly" [274], "The
author never enters a character's mind" [275], "the least overtly, most covertly manipulative of the
points of view" [276]; "The present tense takes the story out of time." [870], anthropological reports
on the long dead "are written in the present tense" [869]; the narrative past "involves and includes
past, present, and future" [126, 127]. Critics: she passes from "the distance of the teller of
fairy-tales to the very close" [69] (Walton); Ged "is also treated with detachment and clear-sightedness"
[151] (Eichner); tone "grave and slightly formal" [35, 132] and "even more gravely austere" [39, 135]
(Dirda); "a controlled polyphony" [386] (Slusser); she reports "just as an anthropologist reports on an
indigenous people" [136]. The distance is explicitly **not** coldness: Homer "truly doesn't take sides"
yet "His impartiality is far from dispassionate" [302, 303], and journalism's suppression of sensibility
[108, 800] is named as the *wrong* kind of detachment.

No costume, verified: the archaic manner is "a trap into which almost all very young fantasy writers"
walk [112]; Vance's achieved style "contains no archaisms at all" [113, 5]; Tolkien's "an English
extraordinary for its simple timelessness" [13]; the colloquial/formal lurch means "the characters lose
coherence in my mind" [6, 115]; plain English "ranges easily from the commonplace to the stately" [105,
8]; pastiches "tone-deaf to the morals, the manners, the ideas" and the language of their period [248];
does an unusual or archaic word intensify or distract [284]; Hemingway's accented English "a little
affected and foolish" [625]; the rider at the palace gate so that "Nobody has to do any thinking about
it at all" [848]. Her own measured practice on this side: Mitchell — "dialogue is sparing" [415],
accents "referred to but not reproduced on the page" [416], "warmth and humour, but no knowing
wisecracks" [418]; Amenta — the Polish translator "inclined to elevate the tone of the phrase" [585],
i.e. her register sits lower than its translation, while the novel's several registers are respected
[586].

**Adversarial notes, both marked in the section already.** (i) The costume half is doctrine-heavy: 10 of
its 20 rows are one document ('From Elfland to Poughkeepsie'), and the doctrine rows are her readings
of Vance, Tolkien, Eddison, Dunsany and Hemingway — the section labels feature 12 "her doctrine, not
her measured practice". Independent attestation of her own no-costume practice is **2 documents**
(Mitchell, Amenta). (ii) One verified reader perceives the opposite: the prose was "very archaic and
flowed in a unique style" [341]. The section's reconciliation — timeless plainness reads as archaic to
readers used to colloquial prose — is a reading, not a verified claim. (iii) The related "sentences
beginning with 'And'" / biblical-costume row is PARTIAL [11] and may not be cited for the limb.
Verdict stands: SUPPORTED, with the practice half thinner than the doctrine half.

## 5. Her stated rules on sentence sound and adjectives — **SUPPORTED** (sound: 5 documents, 15 rows; adjectives: the rule proper is 1 work at 2 copies, 5 documents counting corroboration) — with two direction flags

Sound, her own words, 5 distinct documents: "The sound of the language is where it all begins." [85];
"The test of a sentence is, Does it sound right?" [118]; "A good writer, like a good reader, has a
mind's ear." [87] (dull, choppy, droning, jerky, feeble are the faults of sound); "Pace and movement
depend above all on rhythm" [281]; "the rhythms of words are bodily rhythms" [236]; "It's a very long
beat" [305]; "Not whispering, not muttering, OUT LOUD." [252]; on Tolkien, the flow "follows the breath"
[83, 128] and "punctuation comes just where you need to pause" [129]. Note the concentration: [85, 87,
118, 121, 122, 281, 282] are all one document, chapter one of *Steering the Craft* as excerpted at
Literary Hub.

Adjectives, her own words: "Adjectives and adverbs are rich and good and fattening." [195]; they cause
obesity in prose "only when used lazily or overused" [269]; narrative going a long distance "needs more
muscle than fat" [270]; the Chastity exercise writes "descriptive narrative prose without adjectives or
adverbs" [271]; "Suddenly seldom means anything at all" [272]. Corroboration on her practice: "without
obvious flourish" [139] (NEA); "Metaphors and similes are scarce enough to burn vividly" [414]
(Mitchell). Her epic models, doctrine on other texts: the Song of Roland's "one simile and no metaphors"
in four thousand lines [111, 808]; Homer's metaphors "neither static nor ornate" [809].

**Flag 1 — direction. The adjective rule RATIONS; it does not BAN.** [195, 269, 270] are affirmative
about adjectives and adverbs; only laziness and overuse are faulted. [271] is a *workshop exercise*, not
the doctrine, and the section records that an earlier draft wrongly turned it into "adverbs near zero"
and has withdrawn that (feature 17, Reconciliation). Any characterization that imports a prohibition is
**CONTRADICTED** by [195, 269, 270]. Reception cuts the same way: Kirkus praises Tehanu's "philosophy
expressed in thoughtful, potent metaphor" [436]; Kuts and Uholkova find *The Left Hand of Darkness*
"extremely rich in means of artistic expression, among which metaphors" [708, 709]; Barbour reads one
governing image, the wall [409]; Watson a "neural simile" [412].

**Flag 2 — the word "rules".** She disavows rules as a category in verified rows: "Most such rules are
hogwash" [833]; "Distrust anybody who says fiction must use only limited third person." [245]; her
counts are "too subjective for any conclusions at all to be drawn" [166]; the sound rule is phrased as a
**test** ("Does it sound right?" [118]), not a prescription, and the essay carrying much of this is
titled 'On Rules of Writing' and attacks them. "Stated rules" is a fair shorthand for the imperative
sentences she does write, but it inverts her own framing, and the corpus will not support presenting
them to a generator as hard gates.

**Flag 3 — textual instability.** [195] (annkroeker, quoting the 1998 edition) reads "rich and good and
**fattening**"; [269] (archive.org OCR of the 2015 Mariner revision) reads "rich and good and
**nourishing** … cause obesity in prose only when used lazily or overused". The adjective rule proper
is one work reached at two copies that disagree on a word, and one of those copies is an unofficial
scan the section forbids citing downstream (feature 29).

## 6. Proposed as "the discipline" (weight 20) — **UNSUPPORTED** (0 sources)

Zero verified rows support this. It is a program decision, not a finding, and nothing in the 454 kept
rows licenses a weight or a primacy.

- A regex sweep of all 454 kept rows for exemplar language (`model for`, `emulate`, `learn from her`,
  `exemplar`) returns **0 hits**. No verified row ranks her prose against another writer's as a model
  to follow. The nearest thing is an article *title* — Mahon, '5 Things Every Fantasy Writer Can Learn
  from Earthsea' [145, 146] — one document, and the verified rows from it are about weighed words and
  leaving things out, not about primacy.
- The corpus supplies no basis for a relative weight of any size. A number like 20 cannot be
  CONTRADICTED by sources, but it also cannot be sourced; it must be recorded as the program's own
  judgment call, vetoable, not as a research finding.
- Verified counter-evidence to leaning the whole discipline on her prose, which should be stated
  wherever the weight is recorded:
  - **Her own**: an irresistible story can be told "in the most conventional, banal prose" if the writer
    has the narrative gift [896]. That is Le Guin denying that prose style is the source of narrative
    power.
  - **Dissent, verified**: "the style left me cold" [204]; "It just feels all surface and no depth"
    [364]; "It has a lot of narrative that tells instead of showing" [365]; after 1974 *The Left Hand of
    Darkness* "seemed to some readers too oblique and metaphorical" [451]; Dirda in 1990 reporting
    critics who find the writing "windy and soft, too feminist" [460]; Cadden, "Le Guin does not seem to
    value dialectic, however" [666] against Bloom's tag; Horton's struggle-to-read rows [306, 494, 495,
    308].
  - **Structural**: 51.1% of the kept rows are her self-description, and no primary-measure lane has
    landed a fingerprint of her sentences (Coverage). A discipline weighted 20 would rest, for its
    quantitative half, on counts she took of Tolkien, Twain, Woolf and Austen and then called too
    subjective for conclusions [166].
