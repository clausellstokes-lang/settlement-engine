 lepusdomesticus 😦 tired Blizzards of Still Winter 

 March 31 2014, 08:00 
 Listens: Kathy Mattea: "Black Waters" 

 Tigana: Head Smashing 
 Dear Readers! This is my first post dissecting Guy Gavriel Kay’s 1990 novel Tigana ! All posts about the novel will be categorized under the new tag “dissecting tigana,” so as to a) be easy to find and b) not clutter up my “books” tag. This post will cover the Prologue.

What these posts will be:
1. A little snarky and a little flippant and just a tiny bit serious, to keep moving through this book fairly quickly.
2. As I get further in and have a better picture of the whole story, I will start to intersperse the commentary posts with essays of meta-analysis of what I’ve read so far.
3. When I finish the novel, I’ll do some wrap-up essays in greater detail, which will take into account the story as a whole.
4. There will be unmarked spoilers for each chapter discussed in the post, as well as all previous chapters.
5. Spoilers marked in brackets [like so] refer to my skimming ahead in the book and may or may not be accurate.
6. I’ll include very brief summaries of the action for those who want to read these posts but not the book, but I’ll only quote things I’m directly commenting on.
7. Since Tigana is about war and contains just about every kind of bigotry, there will be a note before the cut on each post telling you what disturbing material the post includes examples and detailed discussion of.

 But Susie, why are you doing this? Don’t you hate Tigana ? 

I can’t tell you whether I hate Tigana or not. I expect that I will hate Tigana by the time I’m done, because when I started reading the book straight through, I found that the more I read the angrier I got. And I thought I really wasn’t being fair to the author, because I really do feel that Kay has some really good topics and ideas here, and it’s just the execution that’s ham-handed, and I think I really shouldn’t accuse him of having an evil agenda until I’ve read the whole thing. Besides which, he starts at the very beginning (of chapter one) with unreliable narration, so it’s reasonable to assume that he knows more than his characters do, intentionally.

So I thought I would do better taking it a little bit at a time, and letting my reactions stay purely in-the-moment, because I fully expect my perspective to change as I read along. Also, this way I won’t get bogged down in rage that makes me want to tear the book into tiny pieces and stuff it down the garbage disposer. And yes, Dear Readers, this is why I don’t do e-books. ;)

On the upside, reading Tigana is making me feel really great about L&W. I know it’s not the best-written thing ever, and I know when I get it published (when!) a lot of people will hate it, but I look at it and think, “Oh God , that’s good!” One imagines Mr. Kay felt the same way about his book. *smiles ruefully*

All page numbers [in brackets] are from the 1999 Roc (Penguin) trade paperback (Tenth Anniversary edition with an afterword by the author).

I. PROLOGUE
 [Content Note: Warfare]

 The novel’s epigraph is two quotes: the first from the John Ciardi translation of Dante’s Paradiso, about the pain of exile, and the second from the Edmund Keeley translation of George Seferis’s “Stratis the Sailor Describes a Man,” about the importance of remembering neither too much nor too little, but just the right amount. I’m going to save discussion of them for the meta-analysis essay in which I discuss the Afterward to the Tenth Anniversary edition. Suffice it to say, the book is about exile and memory.

The prologue follows a Tiganan sculptor-turned-soldier named Saevar as he chats with Prince Valentin, ruler of Tigana (a small nation on a peninsula called The Palm*), the night before the final battle in their war against King Brandin of Ygrath. [SPOILER: They lose.]

I want to talk about three things in the prologue: world-building, authors unintentionally doing things that undermine what they’re trying to do, and the use (and abuse) of prologues in general.

WORLD-BUILDING
The first clause of the entire book is: “Both moons were high.” Some readers of fantasy, I have found, like to have an “explanation” of how this story might be “true,” how they can fit a belief that the events of the novel might have actually happened within the realm of known reality. This is purely a matter of taste, so I don’t think it’s fair to use it as an example of what’s wrong with a story, just because I personally don’t like this conceit—I can accept perfectly well that my favorite stories were made up by some regular person sitting in her pajamas in front of a computer (or typewriter, or pile of paper). However, I think it is right to point out when this conceit actually makes the story less plausible because of inadequate research.

 Tigana , like The Dark Crystal or Sherwood Smith’s Inda series, takes place on another planet, in this case one which is exactly like earth with the hemispheres reversed (and small changes to geography), plus magic, fairies, and two satellites. All well and good, except that the geography (cosmography?**) of the moons is not plausible. Assuming the moons, like our moon, have phases due to their position relative to their planet and the single sun***, then I think it’s just possible that one could be rising half-empty while the other sets half-full at midnight, thus making them both visible at a time when they look as though they could fit together “the two moons together [making] one whole” [2], but later [5] Kay seems to have the moons setting at the same time, which should be impossible if their phases are exactly opposite. (I realize I’m a lunatic, but getting the behavior of the moon wrong is a big pet peeve of mine, so having two moons to get doubly wrong just demands a mention. *g*)

“voices were threading in song to the tune a syrenya played” [2]
“To the east […] a trialla suddenly began to sing” [3]

 Here be smeerps ! We have trialla and grele^ [which is actually in Chapter 2] instead of nightingales and partridges, and I actually have no idea if a syrenya is an instrument or the person who plays it. Now, Tigana was published 24 years ago, and was undoubtedly one of the spate of secondary world fantasies that inspired all those authors of how-to guides to first, coin the term smeerp, and second, decry the use of smeerps, so I don’t fault Kay for being a progenitor of something that later became a trope. But I do understand now why it’s not a very good trick for an author to employ. It’s of a piece with setting the story on another planet: on the surface it makes sense that a different world (especially one with magic) would have endemic species too, but in practice it is distracting, since a fantasy world by its very nature will not mesh with the rules of accepted Reality, so no amount of this kind of invention will actually increase its verisimilitude, but more importantly than that, in a book like this where it seems that these changes will have no bearing on the plot or the rest of the story^^—since otherwise the setting is essentially late-medieval/early-renaissance Italy with slight changes in culture—it feels completely arbitrary, especially since horses are horses and people are people, etc.… Unless I’ve totally misunderstood and they are actually fairies of some kind… which makes eating them seem kind of gross.

But, you know, fantasy authors who make up words for existing technology in glass houses should not throw nitpicky stones. ;)

UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCES

“He had no illusions; none of them did. Not since the battle at this same river five days ago. All they had was courage, and a leader whose defiant gallantry was almost matched by the two young sons who were here with him.” [1-2]

All they had was a lousy leader. So, here we have an army sitting on the south side of a river which is the northern border of their country, while the enemy army camps on the north side of the river. We are told they just fought a battle in the same location five days before, but are not told how that battle was conducted (only a hint that the Tiganans didn’t do so well in it), nor are we given any sense of how large this river is. I make the assumption that the river must be fairly wide and strong, or else the opposing army probably would’ve crossed it already. Which means I really don’t think much of the Tiganans’ military strategy.

The thing about rivers is that people sometimes find they have a need to cross them, and even to cross them while transporting goods that are either heavy, easy to lose, and/or not waterproof. They (or beasts of burden) can walk across, if the river is shallow, slow, narrow, and with fairly flat banks. Otherwise, people need some ingenuity, usually ferries or bridges. Bridges are better. The importance of bridges in warfare cannot be overestimated. I can think of three well-known movies off the top of my head— A Bridge Too Far , The Bridge At Remagen , and The Bridge on the River Kwai —that are about military strategy and bridges in WWII, a conflict in which all combatants had planes, bombs, and guns, and still needed reliable ways to cross major rivers. Less technologically advanced societies can rely even more on rivers to protect them from invasion, or provide a defensible barrier to utilize on a battlefield (or also to provide an uncrossable boundary against which to be massacred should their opponents surround them…). An army with any kind of projectile weapon can fight very effectively against an army crossing a river in boats, and when bridges are involved it’s even easier, because the defending army can choose between destroying the bridges so as to force the opposing army to make boats (or give up the fight), or leaving them intact so as to funnel the opposing army into an easily-fought bottleneck, or best of all, if it’s a very large bridge, destroying it while the opposing army is on it, thus killing a large number of enemy soldiers while also preventing the survivors from crossing the river.

Actually, you know what this reminds me of? Horrible Histories ’ take on the Battle of Maldon .

“the terrible King of Ygrath and his sorcery” [3]

OK, so this war has an added variable: the invading army has a superior technology—magic—that the Tiganans don’t. We don’t know yet just what magic can do in the world of the novel, but assuming it allows the Ygrathens to easily cross the river safely, then the smart thing for Valentin to do is retreat and try to lead Brandin to a battlefield of his choosing, one which presumably could give the Tiganans an overwhelming advantage—something that might work given Brandin’s strong desire for revenge, belief in his vast military superiority, and unfamiliarity with the ground. Or to retreat long enough to gain magic of their own and/or greatly increase their numbers so as to have a more equal battle. Instead Valentin seems to be throwing every able-bodied man in the country into this battle here, probably out of a desire to keep the Ygrathens out of Tigana entirely.

I’m not saying it would be good to let Brandin invade Tigana on purpose (and perhaps there’s some magical reason to be desperate to prevent that also, but we’re not told); Tiganan civilians would likely be killed, and much destruction to property and years of decline in food production, etc., would follow. But it might not be worse than what actually happens, [SPOILER!] which is them being all killed, leaving the country to be conquered by Brandin, who uses magic to literally erase Tigana from the world’s memory so that it basically never existed at all.

Of course, the Tiganans could just surrender.

“I would have gone home from that surrender,” [Saevar] said, with resolution, “and […] smashed every sculpture I ever made of you.” […]
The Prince said […] ‘Oh, our pride. Our terrible pride. Will they remember that most about us, do you think, after we are gone?” [4]

Heh.

I’m thinking, it is sometimes difficult to separate authors from their characters—if a character is clearly meant to be likeable, but isn’t, then we assume the things we hate about the character are things the authors thinks are virtues, and thus think less of the author. Yet, I know from personal experience that authors often enjoy writing characters who have some pretty serious flaws, and that they intend to show those flaws, even if the character is meant to be mostly likeable, or to grow more likeable as the story goes on. But I do believe that how well the author can pull off an Unlikeable Protagonist, or a Villain Who Grows On You, or even a Misguided Hero Who Finds Enlightenment At The End Of The Book, is a skill that can be assessed through the work.

Kay makes the choice to put us in Saevar’s head for this scene, and Saevar doesn’t think their pride is any bad thing, indeed he believes their futile attempt to defend Tigana here is an act of great virtue. I can’t know if Kay intends us to agree with Saevar. What I do know is that this whole incident makes me think Valentin is a total nincompoop, and if he can’t defend his people effectively then he doesn’t deserve to rule over them. And the question of whether or not I should care about the goal of our protagonists in the rest of the book—to restore Tigana as an independent nation, with a name—is one that I will be asking for many more of these posts. Right now, I’m starting the book with zero investment in the achievement of their goal, and therefore in the entire story itself.

PROLOGUES
Which brings me to prologues in general. Those aforementioned how-to guides that council against smeerps also generally council against prologues, and this one is a good example why. The only reasons to have this prologue at all is this exchange, which is only theme-related, and not plot-related:

Saevar said […] “We will leave a name.”
“And we leave our children,” Valentin said. “The younger ones. Sons and daughters who will remember us. Babes in arms our wives and grandfathers will teach when they grow up to know the story of the River Deisa, what happened here, and, even more—what we were in this province before the fall. Brandin of Ygrath can destroy us tomorrow, he can overrun our home, but he cannot take away our name, or the memory of what we have been.” [5]

Which is rather more irony than is strictly necessary. ;P 

Indeed, both the Prologue and Chapter One of the novel are false beginnings, in the sense that the events that happen in them are important to the story, but we are introduced by name to a lot of characters who are never going to show up again [Spoiler! Until a couple of them turn out to be Not Really Dead After All!], and the important points could have been included elsewhere as exposition to better effect. (And are reiterated later anyway!) The prologue especially, as a practical matter, serves only to make me dislike Valentin, whom Saevar at least sees as a great leader, and since [SPOILER!] Valentin’s and Saevar’s surviving children^^^ are our Heroes for the rest of the book, I really think Kay means us to see him that way too. Certainly his walking among the troops like this was used to show the king’s greatness in the works that set the precedent, most notably Shakespeare’s Henry V ^^^^. Additionally, he is presented as a benevolent father figure in the way Saevar thinks of him, and in the way Valentin kisses Saevar and says goodbye to him with the equality of the hand gesture when they part. [5]

Then again, there’s also this, which makes me think maybe Kay is doing it all on purpose: Saevar thinks, “There was singing on the other side of the river too […] It was curiously hard to impute any absolute sense of evil to those harmonizing voices, or to hate them quite as blindly as being a soldier seemed to require. He wasn’t really a soldier, though, and he had never been any good at hating.” [1]

But then there’s this: “Our children’s children will remember us, and will not lie tamely under the yoke.” [said Saevar]
“And if any of them seem inclined to,” Valentin added in a different tone, “there will be the children or grandchildren of a certain sculptor who will smash their heads for them, of stone or otherwise.” [5]

Love, Susie

*I learned that from the map opposite page 1.
**I’m amazed that that’s a word.

***Doubling the star wouldn’t solve this problem. It might give the moons not just dark sides and bright sides but also dim sides. And it might also mess up day and night, which just wouldn’t do for a story where the alternate planet setting is just mood atmosphere anyway and the author really wants to tell a story about humans on Earth with magic.

^Another of my peeves with smeerps like these, is you don’t know the singular or plural of the word.
^^There are two moons apparently for purposes of the novel’s made-up religion, which I will be discussing in great horrible detail, probably in the posts on Chapter 2.
^^^Ah, separately. Much more on that to come, alas.
