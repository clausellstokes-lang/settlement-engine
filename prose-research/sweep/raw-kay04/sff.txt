 I've heard such praise for Kay, and from the view of an outsider looking in, I adore his concepts and what he strives for in his stories. A couple days ago I started reading what is perhaps is seminal work, Tigana. I'm really enjoying it so far but I have a couple issues with it. I want to know if these will continue to pop up in Tigana as I continue reading and if they present themselves in his other books.

*Spoilers of the first ~150 pages of Tigana*

1) In the first 150 pages we are given revelation after revelation and coincidence after coincidence. To the point that it's distracting. All the characters seem to know each other or are related and in an effort to make the story more interesting, Kay doesn't reveal it to us from the get go. A useful storytelling technique but one I find incredibly overused already. Is this sort of thing common his books?

2) The pacing is a little odd. The book is almost written like a play. It's almost all long conversation of pure dialogue after another with little of anything else. It's a bit jarring to me and I'd like to see more movement in the story through action and events instead of all the action and events being placed within the conversations. There's nothing wrong with this, of course, it's just my personal preference. Does this continue throughout Tigana and his other books?

 #2

 This is how GGK writes his works...what you see is what you get, and it isn't going to change in Tigana. If the first 150 pages includes Dianora's first section then the majority of the characters are introduced.

The relationships in this book, and the relationship between them and memory, are the core elements of the whole novel.

Also, it's important to consider that the whole plot is one that is twenty years in the making. No relationship, or event, is a coincidence - except that between Devin and Alessan et al. 

I've gone straight from a reread of Tigana to a reread of The Last Light of the Sun and there are lovely, subtle differences in his tone and manner of writing.

 It's been too many years since I've read Tigana, but I'll chime in on Kay's style.

Kay writes in a very controlled, orchestrated way. I suppose most authors do, but Kay works in a very epic style where everything that happens is somehow important or represented. This can come across as contrived if you're looking for more traditional fantasy storytelling.

What I love about his work is that there aren't really any extras. The stories have to do with all the characters...either how their presence affects history at a given point or how history affects them at a given point. His books all take place at points which are perfect storms of history, present, personality, and coincidence. For some readers, it's too pat. But for many of his readers who have come to trust Kay, we know that he'll take disparate elements and weave something very special out of them (time and time again, book after book).

 In Tigana the overuse of dialogue is largely due to the nature of the plot. A large portion of the plot is past tense, and the characters are nearing a goal that they've been working on for a long time. This isn't so in his other works. 

As far as coincidental revelations, that's a device GGK uses in all his works. After reading a few you'll start to see it coming ahead of time. 

He also tends to use flashbacks and internal monologues a lot, but in my opinion, he does this better than any other author I've read. The way he blends history into the narrative sometimes flat out amazes me.

Personally I thought Tigana was the least impressive work of GGK that I've read (though it has the best ending). Lions of al Rassan and Sarantine Mosaic are among the best speculative fiction, and A Song for Arbonne is my favorite book regardless of genre.

Man this thread makes me want to pick up Under Heaven . Been putting it off too long.
