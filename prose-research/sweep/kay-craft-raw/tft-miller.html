I Drove 12 Hours to Meet Guy Gavriel Kay. What He Taught Me About Fantasy

The One Where a Fantasy Grandmaster Changed How I Read Stories

Joel Miller
May 03, 2026

Guy Gavriel Kay nearly fell out of his chair when I told him I’d driven twelve hours to see him.

I’d come from Pennsylvania to Quebec City in Canada to hear one of the most acclaimed living fantasy authors speak—a writer Christopher Tolkien once trusted to help edit J.J.R. Tolkien’s The Silmarillion, and who has since spent sixteen books quietly redefining what fantasy can do. What I didn’t expect was how much Kay would say, in a single evening, about why his characters feel so unmistakably alive, why he refuses to put real historical figures into his novels, and how we as readers change over the years.

If you’ve read Kay’s œuvre, consider this the peek behind the curtain—the philosophy, craft, and confessions behind books you already love. If you haven’t read him yet, fair warning: this might be the piece that finally hooks you.

The Drive

The sun had already set when we crossed into Old Town Quebec City. The temperature had dropped from Pennsylvania’s lovely 75°F to a nippy 40°F, and streetlights pooled across the old stone buildings, painting them warm gold. Twelve hours behind the wheel—my first time in Quebec and my first time driving that far in a single stretch—wound up through northern Pennsylvania, into the Adirondacks, across the border, past Montreal, and finally here. Any other time the cold would’ve flattened me. Instead it lit something inside.

We had made it.

Old Town Quebec City is the kind of place that feels like you traveled to Europe without the transatlantic flight. History oozes out of the cobblestone streets. The years echo with every step. You can’t help but sense you’re somewhere special. And it is! Hell, the venue we’d come for matched the city’s gravity perfectly.

Our hotel—designed, like the rest of the buildings around it, in a 17th-century French colonial style—was less than a minute’s walk from an old prison. Spooky. Built over 200 years ago, the giant stone building has an ominous quality that matches the dark jail cells still preserved underground. But the prison was eventually transformed into the Morrin Centre, a cultural site and library that hosts historic, educational, and cultural events.

That night it would be the site of a speaking engagement by World Fantasy Award-winner Guy Gavriel Kay.

Who Is This Guy (Gavriel Kay)?

Kay cut his teeth in fantasy in an unusual way: as a young man, he was invited to work alongside Christopher Tolkien as a consulting editor on J.R.R. Tolkien’s The Silmarillion. Chosen through family connections, he spent a year in Oxford editing the notes, materials, and other documents J.R.R. Tolkien had left behind—lots of painstaking work on a manuscript the world hadn’t yet seen.

In hindsight, it feels like the passing of the torch, foreshadowing Kay’s own destiny to leave his mark on the fantasy genre.

After that, Kay published his own trilogy, The Fionavar Tapestry, drawing from some of the same mythic wellsprings that had inspired Tolkien. But he didn’t stay there. He found his real creative footing in his own flavor of historical fantasy, and the description he uses for his own work—“near-historical fiction with a quarter turn to the fantastic”—has shifted what fantasy can do.

Sixteen books later, he’s still doing it.

Kay, who’s now 71, speaks and lectures perhaps a handful of times a year, primarily in Canada—and he’s been slowing down, especially on off-years when he isn’t promoting a new book. Catching him in-person is rarer every year, which is why we made the twelve-hour trip.

In-person, Kay has a deep, resonant voice with a Shakespearean cadence to it. It kind of reminds me of Christopher Lee. Kay’s exactly as well-spoken and articulate as you’d hope.

Why Not Just Write Straight Historical Fiction?

This is the question I’ve always wondered. Why rewrite historical figures and turn them into fictional characters? Why turn the real world a quarter-turn into the fantastic?

Kay’s rationale was nuanced and thought-provoking:

“If it stems from a single thing, it was a realization decades ago that I didn’t want to pretend that I knew the thoughts, conversations, and feelings of people who actually lived. I actually dislike the idea of writers and filmmakers feeling that they can do their thing when lives were actually lived. And it doesn’t matter if you’re making something up because you have to hook the reader or viewer.

“If it is characters clearly inspired by real people—but just as clearly not them—then I can make my characters live, think, act in ways that seem strong and meaningful to me and for the reader without having co-opted, taken advantage of, and used real people.”

It’s a deeply ethical and empathic stance to take. Once you hear that, it completely reframes Kay’s work to be nuanced and honest. You can see how Kay crafts his characters while wearing his inspirations on his sleeve.

On Craft: There Is No System

If you’re a writer hoping Kay would offer some tidy writing advice or methodology, brace yourself. Across sixteen books, he hasn’t found a single way to do it.

“I wish I did, but I don’t have a pattern, a template, or a system. I’m jealous of people who do.

“I remember when I wrote Tigana. It started with an image of a cabin in the woods and some people gathered in that cabin. Somebody coming up to it from outside. And I had no idea who any of them were! But I knew that I could—would—needed to—write something that worked toward that cabin in the woods.”

For his most recent book, Written on the Dark, inspiration and ideas collided: the medieval French poet François Villon, and the unsettling notion of someone so powerful that laws don’t apply to them.

“Villon was the most famous—the best known at his time—of the French poets. And when you read about his life, something extraordinary emerges… we know a fair bit about Villon’s life until he’s about 30 years old, and then he disappears. He’s gone. Absolutely no record of his dying, of moving. No records of what happens to him after a certain point. And that interested me. Why did someone disappear?”

His other idea was more contemporary yet timeless:

“I wanted to work with the idea of people too powerful to be brought to justice. You want to say that might be a bit of an issue in the modern world! History doesn’t repeat itself. But it rhymes, as somebody once said. It rhymes because echoes and associations of the past can be found in things that are happening now.”

That last quote is the key to everything Kay writes. It adds the context, the meaning, and the pathos to everything he creates.

“You Don’t Need a Course in the 100 Years War”

One of the things I most admire about Kay is that you don’t need a history degree to read him. I’ve researched the reign of Justinian I and read the novels Kay built around that era—and I’ve come away convinced that he gives readers everything they need without ever lecturing them. He confirmed this is intentional:

“One of the things that’s really important to me is that readers who know nothing about the actual history don’t ever feel they’re behind the curve or missing something. I don’t ever want readers feeling that they need to take a course in the 100 Years War before they can read Kay’s novel. My editor and my agent would fight each other to kill me [if that were the case]!”

He’s inspired by history, not restricted by it. He’ll diverge from the record on purpose if that’s what the story needs. The result are novels that feel deeply researched and wholly alive at the same time.

The Past Isn’t Even Past

Kay’s reason for writing this way isn’t just aesthetic. It’s philosophical:

“I’m interested in the way the past rhymes with the present; the way the past—so the great phrase goes—isn’t even past. We’re still affected by, still shaped by, events that happened 50, 100, 400 years ago. Because of that, if I can write books where the characters in the books are aware of their own past and the past of their country and culture, I can do things with that, with that awareness of history.”

It’s why his characters feel weighted. They’re standing on the foundation of centuries of their history. And that history is built as an extension of our own, magnified by the imagination and skill of a writer well-versed in his craft.

The Best Part of Writing Is Not Writing

If you’re a writer (or a reader who’s curious about how the proverbial sausage gets made), Kay spoke about his research process:

“My very favorite part of writing a book is the research. Because, think about this: I’m just learning things! I don’t have any responsibility to do anything with it yet. I haven’t got to write anything, but I’m just learning some neat things that I tell my wife, my friends. I say: ‘Do you know about such and such?’ That’s fun! And I make notes about it, and I’m having a good time.

“And then, at some point, this nagging voice at the back of my head says: ‘You know, you’re supposed to be an author. You’re not in university anymore.’ And there comes a point where the research—which has been going on for a year, at least, for every book—starts to feel like stalling. I start to feel that I’m avoiding what I’m supposed to be doing this for. And then I get to it.”

Every writer I know can relate or recognize themselves in that description. I know that I fall into the same traps and pitfalls with my own writing, finding myself striving for perfection where there can be none.

What Actually Influences a Writer

Perhaps the most common question any author is asked is who their influences are. It’s a fair question. And really, if you think about it, it’s asking who shaped the author’s prose, narrative, and worldbuilding. But Kay gave a completely disarming answer that changed the way I think about literary influence:

“The truth is our lives are the greatest influence on us. The deaths among people you know, getting married, having children, getting older, new friends, travels—the experiences of your life are the greatest influence. But it’s not anything I read or have read. It’s what I’ve experienced living that is the greatest influence.

“And because of that, characters will not have the same interests, orientations, defining approaches to life. Because I don’t! I don’t think the same way now, in all respects, that I did when I started out in my 20s writing The Fionavar Tapestry. Something would be wrong if I were the same person.”

Then he flipped the lens onto you and I, the readers:

“We change as readers too. What we need and what we want from books will change as we change. When we reread books we loved at fourteen or twenty, sometimes we say, ‘Damn it! That’s still great!’

“And sometimes we say, ‘Yes, that was a good book for a fourteen-year-old.’ We change as writers and we change as readers. And I think that’s wonderful. I think that’s a strength.”

That single observation reframed how I think about the books that line my shelves. There are books I’ve read that I wasn’t ready for. It wasn’t that they weren’t right; they just weren’t right then. When I’d matured and grown into them, they awakened something in me I couldn’t have experienced before.

Perhaps you too can think of a book that’s done the same for you.

This shaping of the past into the present is at the core of Kay’s philosophy. Legacy and artistry drive his worlds the same way they drive ours. And as we gain more experience and wisdom, our perspective shifts.

The Signing

After the talk, my wife and I hung back. Most people in line had a single book to be signed—only one man had three—and we were carrying a bag of Kay’s books. Each. And these aren’t small books, mind you.

So we stepped aside and let everyone else go first—it felt only right not to make a room full of fans wait while Kay worked through a library of books. He must’ve noticed because later told us that he appreciated our courtesy.

While we waited, the event manager noticed us and, in passing, remarked on the number of books. When I explained we’d driven twelve hours from the US to be there, she was floored.

A few minutes later she came back and asked if we’d be willing to be filmed for the Centre’s social media. Abashedly, I said yes.

Eventually the line dwindled until we were the only ones left.

Kay called us over and laughed when he saw the books. “A nice selection of books you have there!”

He was kind, cordial, humble. He asked what part of Canada we were from. My wife and I exchanged a look and said, “Funny you should ask.” When we told him how far we’d driven, he stopped signing, slumped back, and nearly tumbled out of his chair. You could see, plainly, how humbled he was.

As he signed, we chatted. Among the things we talked about, he spoke about The Lord of Emperors, which I recently reviewed. He told us that after the chariot race in Sailing to Sarantium was so well-received, he knew the second book needed a bigger, better chariot race as part of its climax—and that’s exactly what he delivered. (If you want to read more about that duology masterpiece, check out my spoiler-free review.)

The event staff took a picture of Kay and us. He thanked us for coming. And before we left, he looked at me very sternly and all but commanded: “Please drive safely.”

No arguments here! I wasn’t planning otherwise.

So, as we stepped out into the crisp air of Quebec’s Old Town, the icy wind cut through us. This wasn’t the first author I’ve met and found myself deeply changed by. Not because I’d added a stack of eight (!) signed books to my shelf, mind you. (Though that was nice!)

Kay gave me a lot to muse on. He had reminded me of something I think every reader and writer needs to hear now and then:

The past isn’t past, the books we love are alive, and we’re allowed to keep changing alongside them.
