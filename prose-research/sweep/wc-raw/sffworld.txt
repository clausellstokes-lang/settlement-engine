 Actually, I think The Book of the New Sun is *the* greatest fantasy/sci-fi novel since The Lord of the Rings. 
 
It is a hard read. But that's because there is so much packed into it--a tangled plot, a pseudo-invented vocabulary, many layers of religious and literary allusions, puzzles of character identity and, underlying it all, an apocalyptic vision of the latter days of an aging planet. 
 
The Book of the New Sun has so much more to offer, in terms of literary merit, than other contemporary fantasies that it's almost laughable. It's not for everyone, for sure, especially those who prefer conventional epic fantasy. But it is absolutely a seminal work in the genre and, for me, a powerfully moving statement about faith. 
 
By the way, there is a coherent plot--it's just not always linear. Each of the waystations along Severian's journey is important to the overall development of the story. It is by no means a series of short stories!

 cgw: Your reference to Joyce is quite a propos--I think there are a number of ways The Book of the New Sun can be compared to Ulysses. I'm not saying that Wolfe is as good as Joyce, only that on a literary level Wolfe's intentions for his novel were comparable to Joyce's. 
 
Anyway, I read Wolfe both for his literary merit and because I think his novels function well as fantasy. Severian's Urth is described evocatively and intriguingly, and the various fantasy and sci-fi elements of the story feel like they belong in that world. 
 
I can see why Wolfe's style in these books could turn some readers off. I think the style is one of the key selling-points of the Book of the New Sun, however. And in Wolfe's other series (The Book of the Long Sun and The Book of the Short Sun) he employs radically different styles to match the nature of the stories and narrators. Wolfe's a versatile author.
