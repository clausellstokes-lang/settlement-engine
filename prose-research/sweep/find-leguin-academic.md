## PRIMARY — Le Guin, "From Elfland to Poughkeepsie" (1973) — full text read (digginganddeepening PDF; local copy leguin1973.txt)
- Style IS the book (p. 9): "The style, of course, is the book." Remove style → synopsis. "absolutely true of fantasy."
- Plain noble style (p. 8): epics keep oral directness; Song of Roland: one simile, no metaphors; sagas plainspoken. "A plain language is the noblest of all." + "It is also the most difficult."
- Tolkien as model (p. 8): plain clear English; virtue = flexibility/variety; commonplace→stately; slides into metre (Bombadil) unnoticed; "no ichor"; direct, concrete, simple.
- Fake plainness (p. 8): Poughkeepsie prose "not really simple, but flat"; "not really clear, but inexact"; sensory cues vague; "the scenery is cardboard, or plastic"; = journalistic prose, suppression of sensibility; "written fast, and read faster"; wrong for novel, "dead wrong for a fantasy".
- Archaism (pp. 5–6): "perfect distancer, but you have to do it perfectly"; "It's a high wire: one slip spoils all." Failure markers: thee/thou -est, subjunctive was→were, She-To-Whom trap, Eldritch/Tenebrous/smaragds/mayhap/perchance, "Ichor" as touchstone of the seventh-rate. Eddison did it perfectly: "totally artificial, but it is never faked".
- Eddison passage judged (p. 6): "exact, clear, powerful"; visually precise; "musically—that is, in the sound of the words, the movement of the syntax, and the rhythm of the sentences—it is subtle and very strong."
- Vance (p. 7): aloof, restrained dialogue "very like his own narrative prose"; "an achieved style. And it contains no archaisms at all." → distance without costume.
- Register consistency (p. 7): Leiber/Zelazny alternate colloquial American and old formal usage → "jerked back and forth between Elfland and Poughkeepsie; the characters lose coherence in my mind". Cabell's tone "perfectly consistent: elegant, arrogant, ironic."
- Dialogue as test (pp. 2–4): four-word substitution trick; "Speech expresses character."; "Nobody who says, 'I told you so' has ever been… a hero." Eddison/Morris invest trivial acts (reading a book, dividing rabbit) with "importance… emotion… vitality". Tolkien's speakers: "an English extraordinary for its simple timelessness… sobriety, wit, and force".
- Dunsany imitation markers (p. 5): made-up names, vague gorgeous cities, "a great many sentences beginning with 'And.'" Dunsany's sources: KJV + Irish speech; "delicate ear for speech rhythms".
- Every word counts (p. 10): "a construct built in a void, with every joint and seam and nail exposed"; "The only voice that speaks there is the creator's voice. And every word counts."
- Style = how you see (p. 9): "Style is how you as a writer see and speak. It is how you see: your vision… your voice."

## Kelso, "Boldly to Re-Venture", Paradoxa 21 (2008) 7–22 — PDF read (pp. 14–18 on rhythm)
- Rhythm "eludes most contemporary critical frames"; Kristeva semiotic; two Wave in the Mind essays.
- Lathe jellyfish sentence: "four opposing blocks, two compound adjectives, two clauses, echo the rhythm of ocean on beach: Here—and-back" → "rhythm at sentence level foreshadows the tenor of the work as a whole"; vocabulary = "rhythm's Siamese twin"; "tugged hugely"/"tidal abyss" embroidered vs "made, like bread" homely.
- Always Coming Home paragraph: long opening clause → shortening phrases → colon brake at "see" → triple of lengthening units → "singing polysyllable"; "Here there are no rhythmic superfluities."
- "Le Guin does seem to move toward simplicity, or at least austerity, in both the rhythm and content of her later work" (Yeats parallel); Tehanu "making less do more".
- Tenar/Moss rush-splitting: business as "a suite of variations to indicate pause and emphasis"; "They split rushes for a while in silence" → "neatly, quickly, with her nail" → "They split rushes." (brevity cuts scene).
- Kalessin: "almost somnambulistically smooth", "second sentence extraordinarily long, commas again weighting the critical words: 'sinuous,' 'claws,' 'fire'"; "classic triple repetition" ("Straight to Gont… straight to her") linked by assonance her/air — "closely approaches actual poetry".
- Le Guin quoted ("Collectors, Rhymesters and Drummers" 183–84): "If I could hold to the rhythms of the dragon's flight, the very large, long wingbeats, then the story told itself… When I lost the beat, I fell off"; rhythm = "writing's enabler, or even its source".
- p. 8: Language of the Night "establishing Le Guin's lyrical, unruly, and individual critical voice".
