## The author's theory, condensed (what the prose must DO)

1. **Consistency earns belief.** Art is what gives the Secondary World "the inner consistency of reality"; inside it what is related is "true" by its own laws; "The moment disbelief arises, the spell is broken" (OFS). The harder the material departs from the Primary World the harder consistency is — "easier to produce this kind of 'reality' with more 'sober' material". No frame or machinery may hint the world is a figment (Wikipedia OFS).
2. **Strangeness + consistency, not strangeness alone.** Fantasy's advantage is "arresting strangeness"; "the green sun" is cheap to say and costly to make credible — "a kind of elvish craft".
3. **The adjective is the root spell**; incantations are "a part of speech in a mythical grammar". Recovery = "regaining of a clear view", freeing things "from the drab blur of triteness or familiarity". Fantasy "is a thing best left to words"; Drama is "naturally hostile to Fantasy".
4. **Sound is a value in itself.** "pleasure in articulate sound"; the maker's instinct is "the fitting of notion to oral symbol"; "word-form in relation to meaning (so-called phonetic fitness)"; the language's "final fruition: verse". Pleasure peaks "in the contemplation of the association of form and sense" (E&W). Fine words are "beautiful words before they are understood" and must be met "in a living context, and not in a dictionary" (L234) — argent ≠ silver.
5. **Language first, world to fit it.** "your language construction will breed a mythology" (SV); "The invention of languages is the foundation" and the book is "largely an essay in 'linguistic aesthetic'" (L165); "a language requires a suitable habitation, and a history" (L294); legends must be of "the same 'taste'" as the language (L180). Names must have "verisimilitude" that assists "literary belief in the story as historical" (L297).
6. **Depth is made by dark, unexplained background.** The illusion of historical perspective "is largely a product of art"; the impression of depth is "an effect and a justification" of allusions to old tales "mostly darker, more pagan, and desperate than the foreground" (Beowulf). "it is good that there should be a lot of things unexplained" (L144); the attraction is "the glimpses of a large history in the background", destroyed "unless new unattainable vistas are again revealed" (L247). Structure as "a balance, an opposition of ends and beginnings"; "more like masonry than music"; "heroic-elegiac", "an echo of an echo".
7. **Register per people, by diction and idiom, not accent.** Westron → English "with some differentiation of style to represent dialectal differences"; related tongues → Old English forms (Rohan) / Scandinavian (Dale); Gondor speaks "nobler and rather more antique style" (L144). "I paid great attention to such linguistic differentiation ... in diction, idiom"; no accent-marking; Orcs do NOT drop aitches — make them nasty by "intonation, articulation, and tempo"; Minas Tirith is "as London is to modern English"; Rohirrim have "a slower tempo and more sonorous articulation" (L193). LotR's style is to span "the colloquialism and vulgarity of Hobbits, poetry and the highest style of prose" (L131). Learned hobbits "quick to note and adopt the style of those whom they met" (App. F).
8. **Real archaism, not tushery.** Tushery = bogus expletives ("tush, pish, zounds, marry"); "a real archaic English is far more terse than modern"; a modern idiom for Théoden would be "a disunion of word and meaning"; heroic scenes need the ancient style as they need helms not "modern uniforms"; fronting ("Helms too they chose") puts the emphasised word "into prominent first place" without "a lot of little 'empty' words"; use "the wealth of English which leaves us a choice of styles"; reject the "parochialism of time" (L171). For translation: avoid "colloquialism and false modernity", be "literary and traditional" because the original was "poetical, archaic, artificial" in its own day; "avoid both the colloquial and the obscure"; "modern literary, not present-day colloquial" (OTB). Verse "bursts into ... an archaic style"; "avoid any modern rusticity" where dialect marks place not rank (L152).
9. **No talking down.** The children's convention "had some unfortunate effects on the mode of expression and narrative method"; intelligent children single out the addresses to children "as blemishes"; The Hobbit moves from a "whimsy", "facetious" mode to "more consistent and historical"; LotR was addressed to no class (L215). "in style or manner, I regret it. So do the children" (L165). Talking down is "useless ... or pernicious"; limiting vocabulary "cut[s] off the gifted ones" (L234). The film treatment's "Old scamp!" lowers "the tone towards that of a more childish fairy-tale" (L210).
10. **Literal geography and season.** "miles are miles, days are days, and weather is weather"; seasons "are pictorial" and "basic to the purport and tone"; "not in 'fairy-land', but in real river-lands in autumn"; neither "contes des fees" nor "scientification" ("food concentrate"): "Both modes are alien to my story"; characters kept "in style and sentiment"; anticipation and assimilation of scenes have a "flattening effect"; the two story-branches are "totally different in tone and scenery" (L210).
11. **Delivery.** The book wants "the older art of the reading 'mime'", passages read "as a necklace upon a thread of narration"; it is "a tale not conceived dramatically but ... epically" (L194). Gandalf must not "splutter": "high and noble authority, and great dignity" (L210).
12. **Nomenclature.** Shire names are "not 'meaningless'" — English elements "obsolete or dialectal, or ... worn-down"; Common-Speech names should be "a euphonious name of familiar English style" (Rivendell); untranslated ones would break "the feigned linguistic history of the period" (Nomenclature). 'hobbit' must stay unaltered (L190). Anglo-Saxon is "the sole field" for the Mark's names "as a device of 'translation'" (L297).

## Negation lane
- Searched for critics holding that the essays are poor guides to his practice or that his archaism is inconsistent: two rounds returned nothing new beyond Shippey's observation (via search summary, not read direct) that late in life Tolkien doubted his own work's "inner consistency of reality"; Shippey and Le Guin hold the archaism consistent and technically controlled.
- The strongest live counter-claim to Letters 215/234/165 is Moorcock, 'Epic Pooh' (1978; READ): "the prose of the nursery-room. It is a lullaby"; "It is the predominant tone of The Lord of the Rings". Wikipedia records Bratman ("one wonders what book Moorcock read") and Williams dismissing it.
- Tolkien himself concedes a gap in L144: "I have perhaps ... erred in trying to explain too much" (readers "stuck at the Council of Elrond"); and in L131 "Some of the details of tone and treatment [of The Hobbit] are ... mistaken".
