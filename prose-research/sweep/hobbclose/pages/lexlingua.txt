 Listens: Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan- Sangam 

 Book Review: The Liveship Traders by Robin Hobb 

 I had heard many people commend Robin Hobb as a brilliant SFF author, so I finally decided to start off her booklist with The Liveship Traders Trilogy . 

 Robin Hobb & her Liveship Traders Trilogy Book-Set 

 This set of books is about the Vestrit trading family of Bingtown, whose head, in his will, bestowed the family ship— The Vivacia – on his eldest daughter Keffria, and by proxy, her husband, Kyle. This was a terrible shock to Althea, his younger daughter, who is a spirited, independent and highly competent woman who has been trained since childhood to become a sailor. 

 The inheritance of a liveship is no trivial matter, because it basically is a ship with a soul, memory and even the five senses. The figurehead of the ship, carved usually as a man or woman, can talk, guide the crew, and brave bad weather at sea—so such magical liveships are very conducive to trade. These ships are carved out of some special kind of wood (“wizardwood”) by the people of the Rain Wilds, and are loaned to Bingtown traders. 

 The Vivacia is such a ship, and once the Vestrit family has seen three deaths in three generations, she will acquire the souls of the Vestrict deceased and will transform into a ‘liveship’. Althea, who was the apple of her father’s eye, and is an excellent sailor, has loved the Vivacia and takes its loss really hard. I was sometimes irritated by how desperately she wanted her ship back, but I could also sympathize with her. 

 Kyle, her brother-in-law, to put it mildly, is a male chauvinist pig, and a complete tool. Everytime he comes on the page, you want to hate him and hit him. He is an autocrat, who takes over the reins of the house, the property and its people with an iron fist. He hurts Keffria with his cruel words, spoils his eldest daughter Malta, and treats his son Wintrow most abominably. 

 In fact, Wintrow’s relationship with his father is a major highlight of the book. Wintrow was planning to become a monk (to the religious order of Sa) and has no inkling of the harsh life at sea, yet his father cruelly pulls him out of his training as a priest, and thrusts him on The Vivacia to replace Althea. 

 Priest or Pirate? 

 No matter what Wintrow does, Kyle simply won’t acknowledge him as a ‘man’ or as a worthy ‘son’. Wintrow’s entanglement in the bonds of duty, God and liveship are vividly described by Hobb, and nearly drove me to tears at several points. 

 “I am not afraid. I simply don't want this. Why don't you offer it to the person who truly hungers for it? Why don't you make this offer to Althea?” Wintrow asked quietly. The very softness of his words cut through his father's diatribe. His father's eyes glinted like blue stone. He pointed his finger at Wintrow as if it were a weapon. “It's simple. She's a woman. And you, damn you, are going to be a man. For years it stuck in my craw to see Ephron Vestrit dragging his daughter after him, treating her like a son. And when you came back and stood before me in those brown skirts with your soft voice and softer body, with your mild manners and rabbitty ways, I had to ask myself, am I any better? For here before me stands my son, acting more like a woman than Althea ever has.” 

 Then there is the pirate Kennit, who wants to be the King of the Pirate Isles, whose character is so contradictory that it is simply incomprehensible. He is a man of many masks, and can suit his words to the situation. From start to finish, his motives are impossible to gather. All his actions have ugly ulterior motives, yet all his actions have positive results! How do you judge a man—by the good he intends, or the good he actually does? Is he a good man, or an evil man, is he to be pitied or is he just a charming thug? Moore often than not, fantasy novels portray villains as monochrome black, with no redeemable features; but Kennit is such a complex gray that you won’t know the difference between redeemable and culpable traits. His aim is to get a liveship of his own, and he captures The Vivacia for just this purpose. 

 Althea needs to get her ship back (and to rescue Wintrow as well) so she engages the help of Brashen Trell, her deceased father’s first mate, who is perhaps one of the most endearing heroes I have ever read about. He is the beta guy, the ‘nice’ guy on the sidelines, not flashy at all— more like the good reliable guy on the sidelines. He has fallen on bad times since his aristocratic family disowned him for his disreputable acts as a rakish youth. Since the Vestrit patriarch gave him a position on board, he is extremely grateful. He has always had a soft spot for Althea, so he looks after her. Each mention of him in the books warmed the cockles of my heart immeasurably. 

 [Amber] seized his shoulder in her good hand and tried to shake him. “Brashen, they are alive! Give yourself a moment to savor that! Once you take a breath and say, Althea lives,’ all the other obstacles are reduced to annoyances. Say it.” Her gold-brown eyes were compelling. Somehow, he could not refuse her. “Althea lives.” He tried the words aloud. Amber grinned at him, and Clef cut an uneven caper about the deck. “Althea is alive!” the boy repeated. 

 “Believe it,” Paragon encouraged. “The serpent has no reason to lie.” 

 Something dead inside him stirred to life. Perhaps, despite his defeat, she still lived. He had accepted the burden of her death due to his failures. Trying to live with that dereliction had baffled him. This reprieve unmanned him. Something very like a sob shook him, and despite Clef’s astonished look, the weeping he had refused at her death suddenly clawed its way out of him. He dashed tears from his eyes, but could not control the shaking that overtook him. 

 Clef was bold enough to seize his wrist and tug at him. “Cap’n, don’t yer unnerstan’? She’s alive. Ya don’ need ter cry now.” 

 He laughed suddenly, the sound as painful as a sob. “I know. I know. It’s just-” His words abandoned him. How could he explain to a boy the rush of feelings that accompanied the restoration of his world? 

 There are numerous plotlines running through the book. Kyle wants to use slaves for profiting from trade; Althea must get her ship back; Wintrow wants to go back to being a monk but must brave the rigours of sea life; Kennit wants a liveship; spoilt Malta is to be married off to a Rain Wilds fellow as debt repayment; the cruel and dissipated Satrap (king) has imposed heavy taxes on the traders; the slaves are angry at the cruelty of their masters, the traders; customs of new immigrants to Bingtown are changing the social fabric; and the liveships… The Liveships are not what they seem. They used to be more than wood, once upon a time, and the secret of their origin is frightening. If you’ve read the poem Encroachment by A nonymous maybe you can guess what I’m hinting. 

 There is also a rape scene in the book. 

 [ Spoiler (click to open) ] 
I’ve been told that SFF works regularly employ rape scenes— (really, I had no idea!) and I can’t fathom why Hobb used it. Is it because Althea reminded Kennit of Wintrow; is it because he wanted to subjugate her instead of the liveship; is it because she reminded him of himself; is it because he was jealous of her; is it because he wanted to feel powerful? Is it a mixture of all these motives, and more? It was a painful scene, though Hobb did not dwell too much on its aftermath. We studied in law school how in earlier days, women who were raped were themselves seen to be at fault for provoking the rapist—Althea’s reaction to Kennit comes surprisingly close to that theory, i.e. she believes herself guilty for instigating her own rape.
 
 The books are written in multiple POV, with Althea, Brashen, Malta, Wintrow and Kennit being the primary ones. I was understandably more interested in Brashen (<3 <3 <3) and Althea, but Wintrow came a close second. I was just too appalled by Kennit to welcome his passages. Kyle, though, was one man I loved to hate. 

 Hobb gives a powerful voice to all its characters, all of them grey and noticeably grim. Her use of sailor’s cant and slangs is illuminating too. The Liveship Traders is gut-wrenching and addictive in a way that you wouldn’t want to stop while reading even though you’ll feel emotionally drained out. The most astounding part about the books, is that no matter which century you read it in, the trilogy will never feel antiquated to you. Not many SFF books written in the last decade can claim that, I think. 

 There’s so much more going on in these books that I could write a novella about it myself. As it is, my review is once more leaning towards the verbose side. I’ll end with a short excerpt, where Wintrow’s finger which has become septic must be amputated before the infection spreads further: 

 He looked around himself. Slowly he called his awareness back into his body. He clenched his teeth against the white wave of pain from his hand. Black dots swam for an instant before his eyes. He blinked them away, focusing for a time on the two bloody handprints he had left on the Vivacia's deck. The blood had soaked deep into the wizardwood. They both knew that no amount of sanding would ever erase those twin marks. Slowly he lifted his gaze and looked around. Gantry was cleaning the knife on a rag. He returned the boy's gaze, his brow furrowed but a small smile on his face. He gave him the smallest of nods. Mild's face was still pale, his eyes huge. Kyle gazed out over the rail. “I'm not a coward.” He didn't speak loud, but his voice carried. His father turned slowly to the challenging words. “I'm not a coward,” Wintrow repeated more loudly. “I'm not big. I don't claim to be strong. But I'm neither a weakling nor a coward. I can accept pain. When it's necessary.” 

 A strange odd light had come into Kyle's eyes. The beginnings of a smile hovered at the corners of his mouth. “You are a Haven,” he pointed out with quiet pride. 

 Wintrow met his gaze. There was neither defiance nor the will to injure, but the words were clear. “I'm a Vestrit.” He looked down to the bloody handprints on Vivacia's deck, to the severed forefinger that still rested there. “You've made me a Vestrit.” He smiled without joy or mirth. “What did my grandmother say to me? 'Blood will tell.' Yes.” He stooped to the deck and picked up his own severed finger. He considered it carefully for a moment, then held it out to his father. “This finger will never wear a priest's signet,” he said. To some he might have sounded drunken, but to Vivacia his voice was broken with sorrow. “Will you take it, sir? As a token of your victory?” 

 Captain Kyle's fair face darkened with the blood of anger. Vivacia suspected he was close to hating his own flesh and blood at that moment. Wintrow stepped lightly toward him, a very strange light in his eyes. Vivacia tried to understand what was happening to the boy. 

 Something was changing inside him, an uncoiling of strength was filling him. He met his father's gaze squarely, yet in his own voice was nothing of anger, nor even pain as he stepped forward boldly, to a place close enough to invite his father to strike him. Or embrace him. 

 But Kyle Haven moved not at all. His stillness was a denial, of all the boy was, of all he did. Wintrow knew in that instant that he would never please his father, that his father had never even desired to be pleased by him. He had only wanted to master him. And now he knew he would not.
