## Features

### 1. Plainness is the noblest register and the hardest

Support: 14 sources, 31 rows [1, 100, 101, 102, 16, 35, 131, 139, 140, 8, 105, 13, 21, 24, 23, 25, 27, 32, 40, 41, 43, 45, 75, 77, 154, 358, 367, 370, 371, 379, 380]. Le Guin's own doctrine (Elfland, p. 8): "A plain language is the noblest of all." [1, 100] and "It is also the most difficult." [101]; "Clarity and simplicity are permanent virtues in a narrative." [102]; fantasy must be clear and direct "because there is no known framework for the reader's mind" [16]. Independent critics agree in near-identical words: "plain, strong, and exact" [35, 131] (Dirda, NEA); "plain, strong, and direct, without obvious flourish" [139] (NEA Teacher's Guide); "in prose as taut and clean as a ship's sail" [21] (Craig, Guardian); "a lean but lyrical style" [24] (NYT); "Every word was exactly in place" [23] (Bloom), who also calls the Left Hand prose "precise, dialectical" and evocative in its "restrained pathos" [27]; "as elegant and beautiful as any written" [25] (Zadie Smith); "often quietly poetic" [40] (NEA); a copy-editor's definition of the plain style she models, "clear syntax, precise word choice, natural emphasis, and unobtrusive grammar" [154] (Carey); "austere but vivid" [43] and "a prose notable for its clarity and evocative precision" [45] (SFE); "Her clear, spare prose reminds Plotz of Willa Cather's." [75]; "clear, patient" [77] (Burt); readers: "spare and lyrical, just begging to be read aloud" [358], "the spare, beautiful prose and the inevitable, mythic feel" [367], "the novels in Earthsea Cycle are neither long nor wordy" [370], "The language is precise and descriptive" [371], "too taboo for her spare, clarifying prose" [379], "vivid prose, straight talk and revolutionary engagement" [380].

Dissent, marked: 3 sources, 5 rows. A reader on Walton's post: "the style left me cold" [204]; a rereading blogger: "It just feels all surface and no depth" [364]; a commenter: "It has a lot of narrative that tells instead of showing" [365]; Horton on Always Coming Home: "more impressive than it is involving" [308] and the ethnographic sections "intellectually intriguing, but, really, often a struggle to read" [306]. The dissent is not about ornament; it is that spareness plus telling can read as cold or flat. Features 2, 3 and 8 are the sources' own answer.

Rule: The archivist writes short plain declaratives in familiar words, in a grave and even tone. Plainness is the chosen register, not a fallback from a richer one. The dossier accepts that spareness can read as cold to some readers and answers that with an exact sensory cue and a heard cadence in every block, never with ornament.

### 2. Plain is not flat: the journalism trap

Support: 2 sources, 7 rows [2, 103, 107, 108, 106, 3, 295]; both sources are Le Guin herself (Elfland; blog 95), so this is single-author doctrine, though [139] and [371] echo "precise" from outside. The failed style "is not really simple, but flat." [2, 103]; "To journalism. It is journalistic prose." [107]; "the suppression of the author's personality and sensibility is deliberate" [108]; its sensory cues are vague, so "the scenery is cardboard, or plastic" [3] and "the rocks, the wind, the trees are not there" [106]; "A toneless, inexact language is incapable of creating landscape" [295].

Rule: The record register is the dossier's own danger. An archivist who suppresses all sensibility writes journalism, and journalism cannot make a landscape. Each block keeps at least one exact sensory cue (the rock, the wind, the wet thatch) and one exact verb, and any sentence that merely reports is rewritten until the thing it names is felt.

### 3. Sensory detail: exact, brief, precise, and civic

Support: 12 sources, 21 rows [3, 106, 226, 294, 296, 297, 263, 262, 211, 216, 338, 339, 346, 348, 355, 369, 149, 184, 251, 208, 260]. "Exact and vivid words make an exact and vivid world." [226]; "A fantastic setting requires vivid and specific description" [294]; the imaginary must be imagined "accurately and with scrupulous consistency" [296]; a generic landscape "must be brought to life by the language of the telling" [297]. On O'Brian: "The sensory details are intense, brief, precise." [263] and "Everything the reader needs to know is included, but nothing more." [262]; the craft exercise halves a passage while "keeping the narrative clear and the sensory impact vivid" [211]. The close readings show what kind of detail: the Gethenian implement "with which you crack the ice that has formed" on a drink [216, 338]; a landscape as four bare nouns, "rock, ice, sky, and silence: nothing else" [339]; a wall "built of uncut rocks roughly mortared" [346]; "Street lights were broken, windows shuttered, shop fronts gutted by fire" [348]; "the rough and improvised nature of a key setting" [355]; Tehanu's matter as "the harvest, cooking and cleaning, conversations amongst women" [369]; recurrent phrases for "the scent of turf, the quality of the light" [184]; "Specific features or mannerisms" fix a minor character [251]; the goat-charming spell's "evocative detail" [149].

Rule: Each block names one or two concrete civic objects with plain exact nouns: the mortared wall, the shuttered shop front, the cracked ice on a cup, the split rushes. The object is chosen because a resident handles it, not because it decorates. Detail is brief; nothing the reader does not need is included.

### 4. Crowding and leaping: only the relevant belongs

Support: 19 sources, 31 rows [205, 206, 259, 207, 257, 208, 260, 209, 210, 258, 261, 285, 286, 198, 146, 372, 373, 74, 19, 300, 357, 345, 330, 366, 363, 153, 71, 199, 145, 340, 240]. The problem "has to do with what details are included, what omitted" [205]; "Listing is not describing. Only the relevant belongs." [207, 257]; "what you leave out is infinitely more" than what you leave in [258]; "white space around the word, silence around the voice" [206, 259]; "Overcrowded descriptions clog the story and suffocate themselves." [261]; crowding means "ten vague words where two exact words will do" is refused [208, 260] and the story is kept "interconnected with itself, rich with echoes forward and backward" [209]; revision: "cut and recombine until what's left is what counts" [210]. In interview: "you have to describe more than a realist does" [286] yet "the leaving-out is half the art" [285]. Critics confirm the effect: "There's never a wasted word, and all the words are right." [71, 199]; "not a single word being used unnecessarily" [153]; "every word of Le Guin's is carefully weighed" [145]; "She can say more in twenty words than almost anyone" [340]; "Earthsea leaves a lot up to the imagination" [146]; its laws and myths are "more hinted on than explained" [372]; "you can do successful worldbuilding without going into detail" [373]; Plotz: the prose is "the antithesis of the well-rendered verisimilitude" of a video game, leaving blanks readers fill [74]; Craig: depth "without his tiresome back-stories" [19]; incidental characters "hint at the full lives they have lived off the page" [357]; the reader can "rely on her explaining that difference in due course" [345]; detail is doled out so that "the flow of the narrative is never interrupted" [363]; a sense "of unspoken further implication" [300]. Her own caution against over-leaping: narrative by a poet is sometimes "over-elliptic and over-written", too aware of itself [240].

Rule: The dossier is not an inventory. A block lists nothing it cannot make relevant; a generated roll of every trade, every household, every field is the exact failure named here. Gaps are deliberate. A named thing may stay unexplained in one block if another block explains it later, and some things stay unexplained for good.

### 5. The glimpse of a larger world: told within the world, after the story

Support: 9 sources, 13 rows [70, 223, 224, 225, 304, 291, 319, 321, 73, 19, 74, 323, 287]. Walton: the story is "told entirely within the world, but after the story" so the implied reader is assumed to know the great deeds [70]; it is "a world with a clear line drawn around it" [73]. Le Guin on Tolkien: references to places, people and events outside the immediate story give "a conviction of the reality of the immediate scene" [223] because the scene is shown as "part of a much greater landscape, a long history" of which it is a glimpse [224]; she herself often named events and places that were "when I wrote them, merely words" [225] and "put clues into the book, without knowing it" [304]. Of her whole invented universe: "its history consists largely of gaps" [291]. In Always Coming Home the present day is "remembered only in stray fragments of folklore" [319], and the reader is asked to "bear with some unfamiliar terms" until they come clear [321]. Of Tolkien in interview: "You know where you are, you know what the weather is" and the rest is up to the reader [287]. The Omelas narrator "confesses to lacking detailed knowledge of the laws" of the city [323].

Rule: The archivist writes as one who lives inside the settlement's world and assumes the reader does too. Places, families and events outside the block are named without explanation, as glimpses of a longer history. The settlement's record has gaps and says so plainly; a gap is a fact of the archive, not a defect of the generator.

### 6. The legend voice: grave, slightly formal, and spoken

Support: 13 sources, 18 rows [68, 202, 97, 359, 137, 138, 132, 35, 39, 135, 152, 220, 67, 76, 181, 180, 341, 289]. A Wizard of Earthsea "succeeds in feeling like a legend" [68, 202] (Walton); it "reads like the retelling of a tale first told centuries ago" [97] (Mitchell); it has "the feel of an old legend, told and retold around hearth" fires [359]. The sentences are "formal, clear, and exact, with the musical cadence" of an oral storyteller [137]; "her tone grave and slightly formal" [35, 132]; The Left Hand of Darkness has "a tone even more gravely austere" [39, 135]; "a heightened and at the same time tightened prose style" [152]; "The voice she uses gives it an almost biblical rhythm." [220]. The reader hears "a spoken voice in the reader's head" [67] and is made one of the "co-creators, collaborators, listeners, as if to an oral tale" [76]; oral narrative's repetitions each build the foundation and advance the story [181], in triads in European story [180]. Of storyteller's stories she says they are "very simple, pure Spanish, because they're storyteller's stories" [289]. The NEA Teacher's Guide paraphrases her as holding that high fantasy demands a slightly formal, elevated style [138].

Disagreement, marked: a forum reader calls the prose "very archaic and flowed in a unique style" [341], while Le Guin's own doctrine (feature 7) forbids archaism and praises "simple timelessness". On the evidence the reconciliation is that timeless plainness is perceived as archaic by readers used to colloquial prose; the perception is real and the method is not archaism.

Rule: The archivist's register is grave, slightly formal, and speakable, as if read to a listener at a hearth. Formality comes from plainness, steadiness and rhythm, never from old words. A block that cannot be read aloud in that voice is rewritten.

### 7. Archaism is a trap; the register never lurches

Support: 4 sources, 14 rows [112, 113, 5, 13, 6, 115, 114, 248, 284, 178, 7, 12, 105, 8]; eleven rows are one essay (Elfland), so this is near single-source doctrine, independently reapplied by Le Guin in 2015 [248, 284]. The archaic manner is "a trap into which almost all very young fantasy writers walk" [112]; Vance's achieved style "contains no archaisms at all" [113, 5]; Tolkien's dialogue is "an English extraordinary for its simple timelessness" [13]; imitation Dunsany is made-up names and 'a great many sentences beginning with "And."' [114]; alternating colloquial American with formal usage means "the characters lose coherence in my mind" [6, 115]; historical pastiche tone-deaf to "above all the language of the period" [248]; a novelist "using unusual or archaic words" may distract rather than intensify [284]. Elvish speech has "a dignified, often stately gait, but not a regular stress-beat" [178]; Tolkien's plain English "ranges easily from the commonplace to the stately" [105, 8]. "Speech expresses character." [7] whether the author knows it or not, and Eddison's speakers show "with what importance they invest these trivial acts" [12].

Rule: No thee, thou, mayhap, ichor, or ye olde; no sentence-initial And as a mannerism; no lurch between colloquial and formal inside a block or between blocks of one dossier. The register may range from commonplace to stately, but it ranges by subject, not by mood. When the dossier quotes a resident, the speech expresses that speaker and stays in one register.

### 8. Sound first: the sentence is tested by ear

Support: 11 sources, 24 rows [85, 117, 280, 118, 87, 281, 9, 110, 36, 133, 71, 200, 358, 252, 83, 128, 129, 236, 305, 121, 282, 122, 55, 56]. "The sound of the language is where it all begins." [85, 117, 280]; "The test of a sentence is, Does it sound right?" [118]; "A good writer, like a good reader, has a mind's ear." [87], and dull, choppy, droning, jerky, feeble are faults of sound; "Pace and movement depend above all on rhythm" [281]. Prose is judged musically in "the movement of the syntax, and the rhythm of the sentences" [9, 110]; her own rhythm is "carefully balanced and musical" [36, 133]; of Tolkien even long sentences have "their flow is perfectly clear, and follows the breath" [83, 128] and "punctuation comes just where you need to pause" [129]; "the rhythms of words are bodily rhythms" [236]; prose rhythm is "a very long beat, the rhythms of a prose work" [305]. Where "the vocabulary is simple and familiar" the rhythm carries the power [121, 282]; "changing sentence rhythms embody and express the characters' emotions" [122]. Walton: "This is one of the best books for reading aloud" [71, 200]; a reader: "spare and lyrical, just begging to be read aloud" [358]; her advice on dialogue: "Not whispering, not muttering, OUT LOUD." [252]. Kelso quotes her on writing Tehanu by holding to "the very large, long wingbeats" of the dragon's flight, so that the story told itself [55], and calls vocabulary "rhythm's Siamese twin" [56].

Rule: Every generated sentence is speakable in one breath or punctuated where the breath falls. The walker that checks a block reads it for cadence as well as content. Feeling that the plain vocabulary does not state is carried by the rhythm of the sentence, and a block whose rhythm is dull, choppy or droning fails on that ground alone.

### 9. Prose rhythm is irregular stress (mechanics)

Support: 3 sources, 19 rows [157, 158, 159, 160, 161, 162, 163, 164, 165, 166, 168, 169, 175, 176, 177, 179, 219, 218, 95]; sixteen rows are one book (The Wave in the Mind), so flagged as near single-source; Saunders [218] and a reader's paraphrase [219] are the only outside voices, and a 1992 article title [95] attests rhythm as a narrative technique in Left Hand. "Prose often has three or even four unstressed syllables" between stressed ones [157]; the absence of noticeable metrical regularity is "the only difference between prose and poetry" she is sure of [158]; prose rhythm has stresses and bars, word groups "following syntax, punctuation, sense, stress, and breath" [159, 160]; "good prose does have a stress-rhythm" [161]; "Dull prose, clunky narrative, hard-to-read textbook stuff, lacks the rhythm" [162]; narrative prose runs "one beat in two to four" syllables [176, 177]; textbook prose hobbles "clogged by a superfluity of egregiously unnecessary and understressed polysyllables" [175]; epic action moves in balanced cadences "but it remains pure prose" [179]; her counts: "My samples of prose run higher than that." [163], with the honest caveat "too subjective for any conclusions at all to be drawn" [166]. "The rhythms are so subtle and have to be irregular." [219]; Saunders: "rhythm is something that appears (and surprises me) by way" of editing [218].

Rule: A dossier sentence never settles into a scannable meter. A walker may count stress ratio (roughly one beat in two to four syllables) as a diagnostic, never as a target. The failure to catch is the polysyllabic administrative string: Latinate nouns stacked without a beat.

### 10. Sentence length: variety, against Macho Staccato

Support: 7 sources, 13 rows [264, 265, 266, 237, 238, 143, 340, 382, 339, 54, 59, 120, 267]. "There is no optimum sentence length. The optimum is variety." [264]; she rejects the notion that "the only good sentence is a short sentence" [265] and calls it a myth "that short-sentence prose is" more like speech [266]; contemporary writers "avoid complex sentences and write what I call Macho Staccato" [237]; "The ideal is flexibility, a balance of different sentence lengths" [238]. Her exercise writes narrative "in sentences of seven or fewer words" [143] as training, not as a style. The close readings show both poles: "He is done with doing." [382]; the four-noun landscape line [339]; Kelso's "almost somnambulistically smooth" long sentence [54] and a paragraph with "no rhythmic superfluities" [59]. "The chief duty of a narrative sentence is to lead" to the next [120, 267].

Rule: The dossier mixes long complex sentences with very short ones inside each block. The grammar walker refuses a block whose sentences are all short or all long. Each sentence must lead into the next; a sentence that ends the thought and the breath together, every time, is the staccato fault.

### 11. Diction: native, monosyllabic, familiar; ornament distracts

Support: 5 sources, 11 rows [142, 165, 168, 169, 175, 121, 282, 8, 144, 284, 241]. One critic asserts the root: she "relies heavily on words with Germanic roots" [142] (Brogan, single source for that limb). Le Guin's own counts: the Tolkien sample has forty-five one-syllable words of seventy-two [165]; "in even a readable textbook more than half the words" are polysyllables [168]; "that Mark Twain uses more one-syllable words than a folktale" [169]; textbook prose is clogged by polysyllables [175]. Tolkien's vocabulary is not striking; "Its outstanding virtue is its flexibility, its variety." [8]; where "the vocabulary is simple and familiar" the rhythm does the work [121, 282]. Ornate prose she finds "difficult to get through because it's always stopping to be admired" [144]; she asks whether "using unusual or archaic words" intensifies or distracts [284]. On the passive voice: "Where it does belong, we ought to use it freely." [241], one of the lovely versatilities of the verb.

Rule: Prefer the native word (hearth, ward, toll, kin, byre, well) over the Latinate (residence, jurisdiction, remuneration, facility). More than half the words in a block are one syllable. No word that stops the reader to admire it.

### 12. Adjectives and adverbs are rationed; metaphor is rare

Support: 5 sources, 8 rows [195, 269, 270, 271, 272, 139, 111, 212]. "Adjectives and adverbs are rich and good and fattening." [195, 269] and cause obesity only when used lazily; narrative going a long distance "needs more muscle than fat" [270]; the Chastity exercise writes "descriptive narrative prose without adjectives or adverbs" [271]; "Suddenly seldom means anything at all" [272]. The NEA guide: "plain, strong, and direct, without obvious flourish" [139]. On the epic model: the Song of Roland has "one simile and no metaphors" in four thousand lines [111].

Disagreement, marked: Kirkus praises Tehanu's "thoughtful, potent metaphor" [32] beside its "flawless, poetic prose"; the doctrine rations metaphor, the reception credits it. On the evidence a rare exact figure is admired; a habit of figures is not.

Rule: A block carries few adjectives, and those it carries are exact (uncut, roughly mortared, shuttered). Adverbs are near zero. Suddenly and its kin are banned. A simile appears rarely and is homely (bread, rope, a byre door), never grand.

### 13. Repetition with variation is the coherence engine

Support: 7 sources, 23 rows [171, 172, 173, 174, 209, 246, 273, 180, 181, 188, 189, 190, 191, 80, 79, 182, 194, 53, 54, 59, 81, 183, 187]. "Rhythm is repetition." [172]; "deliberate repetition in a narrative is both great and legitimate" [171]; to forbid it is to "go right against the nature of narrative prose" [273]; a narrative's "coherence is established by inner references and backward-looking or forward-looking" semirepetitions [173], but "If the rhythms become predictable, the coherence of the story" is mechanical [174]; "keeping it interconnected with itself, rich with echoes forward and backward" [209]; indulge the resonances, for "A novel is a living architecture." [246]. In Tolkien she finds "a positive/negative binary pattern of alternation or reversal" [188] whose "reversals are not simple binary flips; each yang contains its yin." [80]; the master rhythm is two beats, stress and release [79, 182, 194]; "European story uses triads" [180]; "Each repetition both builds the foundation and advances the story" [181]; the landscape is built by repetition, semirepetition, suggestion, foreshadowing, recollection, echo and reversal [189]; at a chapter's close, "Yet the tone is quiet, the language simple" despite drumbeat reversals [190, 191]; "The rhythmic structure of narrative is both journeylike and architectural." [193]. Kelso: rush-splitting works as "a suite of variations to indicate pause and emphasis" [53]. Her method was to go through one chapter "noting every major image, event, and feeling-tone" and their recurrences [187]; the narrative's gait is a walk, "One, two, left, right, on foot, all the way. And back." [81]; modern readers are wrongly taught to treat a story as "a road we're driving, well paved and graded" with no stops [183].

Rule: The dossier echoes its own nouns across blocks (the same wall, the same well, the same family name) with variation, never verbatim. A refrain that recurs identically is mechanical and fails. Reversal pairs (plenty and want, the open gate and the shut gate) are written as containing each other, not as flips. The seeded grammar order varies; the echoes do not.

### 14. Point of view: a detached author who is nonetheless intimate

Support: 12 sources, 22 rows [125, 234, 126, 127, 235, 244, 245, 274, 275, 276, 69, 151, 221, 222, 20, 29, 302, 303, 322, 329, 328, 108]. Against the workshop rule, "the narrator's voice is the most intimate voice of all" [125, 234]; the "narrative past tense involves and includes past, present, and future" [127, 126] and "But distance lends enchantment" [235]; limited third as now practised is "a kind of flashlight beam" [244]; "Distrust anybody who says fiction must use only limited third person." [245]. Her detached-author mode: people and places exactly described but "values and judgments can only be implied indirectly" [274]; "The author never enters a character's mind" [275]; it is "the least overtly, most covertly manipulative of the points of view" [276]. Walton: she passes from "the distance of the teller of fairy-tales to the very close" [69]; Eichner: Ged "is also treated with detachment and clear-sightedness" [151]; Slusser: the mood "strange and dreamlike", fluctuating between objective reality and Ged's thoughts [20]; Ai's naive first person learns as the reader learns [29]. Her model is Homer: "Homer truly doesn't take sides" [302] and "His impartiality is far from dispassionate" [303]. The Omelas narrator is "as distinct from an unreliable narrator" an uncertain one [322], who asks "How is one to tell about joy?" [328] and moves to "divest herself of authority and responsibility for defining Omelas" [329]. Journalism's suppression of sensibility "is deliberate" [108] and is the wrong kind of detachment.

Disagreement, marked: a Story Grid editor holds that the editorial omniscient voice "creates a huge emotional gap between the reader and protagonist" [221, 222], the reverse of Le Guin [125, 234] and Walton [69]; Eichner [151] sits between. A second tension: Le Guin defends the narrative past, and the dossier's contract is present tense; the evidence records the argument, it does not license a tense switch.

Rule: The archivist is a detached author: exact about people and places, never inside a head, values implied only by the choice of detail. Impartial but not dispassionate. Because the dossier is present tense by contract, the enchantment of distance is won by nouns and restraint, not by tense.

### 15. Place is described through economy, technology, and civic lacks

Support: 15 sources, 25 rows [147, 362, 363, 229, 230, 231, 232, 324, 325, 337, 215, 217, 374, 375, 376, 377, 344, 347, 136, 141, 192, 299, 301, 383, 314]. Each Earthsea island gets "a few defining details, usually in terms of the local economy" [147]; along the way we learn "its systems of trade and government" [362], and she "doles it out so skillfully" that the flow is never interrupted [363]. Her objection to lazy fantasy: preindustrial is no excuse for "no economics and no social justice" [229]; she sets off fireworks for "a genuinely imagined society and culture" [230]. Her definition: "Technology is the active human interface with the material world." [232], which means "how people get and keep and cook food", clothing, power, what they build with, their medicine [231]. Omelas is drawn by lacks: "They have no King, and do not keep slaves" [324]; "they have no stock exchange and no advertisements around the city" [325]. Bell: she works "from the big picture down to the minutiae of local life" [215, 337], with "her well-made ecologies and cultures tied to the unique geographies" of her maps [217]; Chabon: a planet plausible "from its flora to its weather to the dialects and ceremonies" [374], "conjured an entire planet into vivid existence" [375]; Vest: "each place's contradictions, tensions, mores, and rites" woven into portraits [376] of "real places rife with complex histories, cultures, and characters" [377]; Tomlinson: "a style that is spot-on for describing unfamiliar places and cultures" [344], the underbelly of a city "In just a few pages of dense, carefully-worked text" [347]; Dirda: the author reports "just as an anthropologist reports on an indigenous people" [136]; a great novel offers "a landscape of the imagination which we can inhabit" [141, 192]; she praises "unlabored imaginative authority and completeness of its setting" [299] and "stories that take you quietly into a place" [301]; the film failed for "no sense of islands in a lonely ocean" [383]. Eldridge's contrast: "Tolkien's world has virtually no ethnographic detail" while Le Guin's has no history [314].

Rule: Each settlement block earns its place by one economic fact (what is traded, what is grown, who is paid), one technology fact (how food is kept, what is built with, what is burned), and one civic lack (what the town has none of). The archivist descends from the whole to the minutiae in that order and never stops the flow to lecture.

### 16. Begin with the land; descend from far to near; keep the compass

Support: 6 sources, 13 rows [150, 353, 354, 350, 351, 352, 356, 355, 186, 185, 184, 140, 287]. "The story actually begins with the land" [150]; in The Dispossessed "No people appear in the first paragraph" [353] and "no names are mentioned until the seventh" page [354]; the Earthsea opening moves "from a grander, larger view toward a smaller, more intimate" one [350], "Sparrowhawk to Ged to Duny" [351], "structured by the oppositions between boy and man, village and world" [352]; the first paragraph lays out "things that do not look important but are" [356] and "the rough and improvised nature of a key setting" [355]. In Tolkien there is "no moment when we don't know, literally, where north is" [186]; "Mention of the compass directions" is frequent and often benign or malign [185]; the hill country recurs as "the scent of turf, the quality of the light" [184]. The NEA guide: "Consider the very first sentence of the novel" as the demonstration of plainness [140]. Her own praise of Tolkien's openings: "You know where you are, you know what the weather is" [287].

Rule: The dossier's opening block is the land and the weather, then the works, then the people, and only then a name. Compass directions are stated whenever the eye moves, and a direction may carry a settled connotation (the sea road, the fen side). The first sentence of the dossier is plain and direct and is the demonstration of the whole register.

### 17. Names carry conviction; they are pronounceable and few

Support: 12 sources, 15 rows [65, 66, 72, 201, 288, 283, 203, 253, 250, 60, 49, 50, 351, 354, 298]. Mahy: language is "at times, what the story is about" [65]; she asked how Le Guin came by "names which had conviction and inevitability" [66]; Walton could draw the map from memory because "Le Guin is so wonderful with names" [72, 201]; "Names come first with me." ### 18. Her own archival forms: the compendium, the report, the back of the book, and their cost

Support: 6 sources, 17 rows [26, 290, 292, 311, 312, 313, 315, 316, 317, 318, 307, 306, 308, 310, 320, 319, 321]. The Left Hand of Darkness is personal narration, diary extracts, myths and ethnological reports, a structure called "distinctly post-modern" [26]; The Matter of Seggri is "a compendium of reports on the society of a world" by various observers over many years, from the archives of the Historians of Hain [290]; linked stories "linked by place, characters, theme, and movement" form a story suite [292]. Always Coming Home is "a novel that masquerades as anthropology, a study of a people" [311], with third-person essays on "how they measure time, how they draw maps" [312]; "fiction without being storytelling" [313]; "not a single story but a collection of texts" [317] including "short plays, songs, a chapter of a novel, personal accounts" [318]; the back of the book gives more "in a traditional ethnographic form" [315]; Pandora is "a stand in for the author or the future anthropologist" [310] and stages "a dialogue between herself-as-narrator and a Kesh village archivist" [320].

Disagreement, marked, inside the reception: Bonnell [317 to 321] and Eldridge [314 to 316] take the archival form as its own satisfaction, best "read in very small pieces" [316]; Horton finds it "intellectually intriguing, but, really, often a struggle to read" [306], "like a writer deciding to include all her notes" [307], "more impressive than it is involving" [308].

Rule: The settlement dossier is exactly this form, a compendium of reports kept by an archivist, so it inherits the risk Horton names: an archive that reads like all the notes. The cure on the evidence is features 2, 3, 4 and 8 applied inside every report (sensory cue, relevance, leaping, cadence), and blocks short enough to be read in small pieces, with the Pandora move available: the archivist may, rarely, admit what the record does not know.

### 19. Register narrows with scale: mythic to mundane, grand to small

Support: 8 sources, 12 rows [332, 333, 342, 368, 369, 48, 33, 34, 31, 99, 179, 178]. The mythic of the first book "gives way to the realistic and mundane in this novel" [332]; Tehanu is "the final sharpening of scale across the first four" [333]; Left Hand is "very different from the Earthsea books in style and tone" [342]; Tehanu centres "not on the grand and powerful, but the small, the powerless" [368], on harvest and cooking [369]; "a sad, powerful, quiet book" [48]; "a darker, more realistic edge" [33]; yet "a restless book with very strange pacing" [34]; the travel coda leaves Tombs "solid and well rooted as ever" [31]; the second trilogy "sees exactly the same world with different eyes" [99]. Epic action moves in "balanced cadences in passages of epic action" [179] while noble speech keeps a stately gait without meter [178].

Rule: The same settlement is written at two scales without changing the archivist. A founding or war block may move in balanced cadences; a household block moves in the quiet register of harvest, cooking and the routines of death. The scale is chosen by the subject and held for the whole block.

### 20. Style is the book; coherence is plausibility

Support: 10 sources, 13 rows [0, 116, 10, 14, 64, 148, 228, 296, 38, 93, 58, 15, 65]. "The style, of course, is the book." [0, 116]; in fantasy the construct stands in a void with every seam exposed, so "And every word counts." Rule: The dossier's plausibility is its consistency with itself (the echoes of feature 13) and the exactness of its words, not the quantity of its facts. The generator treats the sentence, not the data table, as the product; a block that is correct in its numbers and wrong in its words has failed.

### 21. Telling is defended: description carries a story

Support: 4 sources, 10 rows [123, 233, 247, 242, 243, 279, 277, 278, 125, 234]; all four sources are Le Guin herself, flagged as single-author doctrine; the reader counter-voice is [365] and its defence [366]. Show-don't-tell leaves workshop writers who "think exposition is wicked": "They're afraid to describe the world they've invented." [123, 233]; "description without any apparent, overt action can carry a story" powerfully forward [247]; "stories are not shown, but told" [242] and "telling always involves moving on" [243] while showing can be static; "The story is not in the plot but in the telling." [279]; a fantasy world "must be created and explained in the story" [277], and crafty writers "grind it fine, and make it into bricks" rather than lumps [278]. A commenter: telling "lets the reader use their imagination more" [366].

Rule: The dossier tells. It does not stage scenes to smuggle facts. Exposition is ground fine and built into ordinary sentences rather than delivered in a lump, and a told block must still move: each sentence adds something the last did not.

### 22. Sound patterning: alliteration and assonance in short phrases

Support: 4 sources, 10 rows [37, 134, 361, 327, 326, 360, 213, 335, 214, 336]. Occasional alliteration and assonance "reminiscent of Old English verse" [37, 134], as in forest rising ridge behind ridge to the stone and snow of the heights; her "gift for alliteration brings us beautiful little phrases" [361]; Omelas opens "echoing the sonic features with a chiastic" flip [327], and its "long lines of the first paragraph mimic the festival parade" [326]; "her ear for rhythm turns a description of the midsummer Long" Dance into a dance [360]; in a Rocannon's World sentence one can "hear the parallel construction of the two rhyming sets" [213, 335] and "See the two progressions expanding its scale as it goes" [214, 336].

Rule: Alliteration is allowed in short phrases only (stone and snow, ridge behind ridge), never sustained. A long line may mimic what it describes (a procession, a dance) at most once per block. Parallel construction and expanding scale are the sanctioned ornaments; rhyme inside prose is not.

### 23. Language is relation: the mother tongue, and meaning without message

Support: 3 sources, 6 rows [89, 90, 91, 92, 293, 381]; four rows are one address (Bryn Mawr), flagged as near single-source. The father tongue's essential gesture is "not reasoning but distancing" [89]; the mother tongue "Expects an answer. It is conversation" [90], always on the verge of silence and "often on the verge of song" [91]; the native tongue of art is a "wedding and welding back together" of the two [92]. A story's meaning may lie "in the language itself, in the movement of the story" rather than in a tidy message [293]; Vint: she is sensitive to how "our ideologies and values are embedded in the language and metaphors" we use [381].

Rule: The dossier does not lecture. A block that reads as one-way speech from above is rewritten toward a voice that expects an answer. The dossier carries no message sentence; whatever it means is in its nouns and its movement.
