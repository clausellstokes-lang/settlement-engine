The Farseer Trilogy by Robin Hobb

2025 Bookshelf
Jun 22
Written By Taylor Jakubowski

Books in Trilogy:

Assassin’s Apprentice

Royal Assassin

Assassin’s Quest

SYNOPSIS

In a faraway land where members of the royal family are named for the vitues they embody, one young boy will become a walking enigma.

Born on the wrong side of the sheets, Fitz, son of Chivalry Farseer, is a royal bastard, cast out into the world, friendless and lonely. Only his magical link with animals - the old art known as the Wit - gives him solace and companionship. But the Wit, if used too often, is a perilous magic, and one abhorred by the nobility.

So wen Fitz is finally adopted into the royal household, he must give up his old ways and embrace a new life of weaponry, scribing, courtly manners; and how to kill a man secretly, as he trains to become a royal assassin.

~~~~~

Robin Hobb’s Farseer Trilogy surpassed my prior knowledge and experiences on the intricacies of world building in fantasy stories. Hobb pays extreme attention to detail and builds a brutal and immersive world through the eyes of our main character, Fitz. Fitz Farseer was born a bastard and instead of being accepted into the royal family he is abandoned by his mothers people and raised in the stables where he, eventually, is groomed into being an assassin for his royal family. The three books in this trilogy follow Fitz as he grows into his role as assassin and survives attempted coups, coups, and a kingdom in need of saving. Throughout this epic adventure we get layered insight into the depthless magic system that exists within Hobb’s fantasy world of the Realm of the Elderlings including Wit (the ability to bond/speak with animals) and Skill (an intricate form of telepathy). One widely accepted as the power passed down within the Royal family, and one seen as an abomination worthy of execution. Fitz, our dear hero, unfortunately has an inclination towards both which makes his already difficult life even more dangerous. I found the world to be incredibly immersive and it was one of the most well developed fantasy worlds I’ve read since Lord of the Rings. Hobb has a way of completely transporting you with setting, magic, politics, and religion in a way no one else has for me besides Tolkien. However, I personally found our main character somewhat insufferable so my journey through this trilogy felt at times like both an epic quest and a slogging task.

We start out with Fitz as a small toddler and spend a good portion of the first book growing into teenagehood/early adulthood with him. Most of the things that happen within that time are him learning his various skills and place within the castle. Developing his relationship with his pseudo-father, Burrich, and his complicated dynamic with the King and his sons (Fitz’s uncles). The second book delves more into the brutal attacks of the "Forged Ones” (people who have been unmade, zombie-like in their survival). It gets much more brutal and dark with the depiction of raping and pillaging of villages and the toxic and untrustworthy nature of people living in the Farseer home. I was shocked at the detailed brutality that Hobb went into given that the first book did not feel nearly so dar. It gave the sense that we grew alongside Fitz into more terrible and gruesome realities. With betrayal and death around every corner, this book is the beginning of the undoing of Fitz. Then in the third book, we have a meandering journey of a traumatized Fitz Farseer on a quest to find his lost King. Where nothing happens until the last couple hundred pages. I honestly felt that, although the first two books were slow, most of that slower pacing had meaning that built to something great. Whereas the third book didn’t hold a lot of interest or import to me given how many pages it was and then the ending felt extremely rushed.

Throughout it all, we follow Fitz as he develops a unique and lasting relationship with the King’s Fool. The Fool is by far one of the most interesting characters I’ve been introduced to in a fantasy world. The Fool is given ambiguous identity both cultural and gendered. A character that comes off as prophetic and insane ends up being the rock that Fitz needs in his life and these two characters perfectly balanced one another. If I continue with Hobb’s works, it will likely be due to needing to know what happens to these two in the future.

Personally, I really loved the political intrigue and the history of this world. I enjoyed the dynamic of Fitz and his fellow people including Burrich and The Fool as my two favourites. I think Hobb does an excellent job with action sequences and providing enough excitement for you to want to pick up the next book.

On the other hand, I found the pacing to be my biggest issue. It should not take only the last 100-150 pages for me to care to want to continue the series. I should feel like I’m involved in the plot alongside Fitz, not just listening to him complain about his situation for 300 pages before something is done about it. I get the need for extensive world-building, but I felt that the pacing could have been much better with the inclusion of more action or even more dialogue between characters. I found some of the side characters to be very one dimensional solely because of lack of dialogue and only hearing about their character from Fitz’s inner thoughts.

It was an interesting read. I’m still torn on whether I will continue with the wider world connected to Fitz’s story, however I’m glad I did read it and I’m glad I finished it to the end. If you’re a fan of high fantasy with slow and intricate world building with a penchant for pain and tragedy, this series would be right up your alley. Overall it was an average 3.75 star read for me.
