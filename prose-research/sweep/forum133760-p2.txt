 His plotting is brilliant, his dialogue is snappy, his characters are Dickensian, and his prose is also, too often, Dickensian. That is to say, while you can always tell he is a professional writer, and his prose does it's job, gets the story from A to B, and strings together all the plot points that must be included in that chapter, but there are many times when that is all it is doing (ie. his prose is straightforward and plain),  many times when the author seems to have dragged himself to the desk and pumped out another thousand words for a food reward, and there are other times when it seems to be building a fat mast to flagellate its author with (ie. inflated and/or surprisingly mundane and confused metaphors).
 It's not so much 'fat mast' (a clumsy metaphor for a clumsy situation that happens to a land-lubber on a ship - you can plausibly argue these are Samwell's word choices). I'm more bothered by things like
 "The queen of the rabbits could not be seen without her floppy ears."(DwD, Ch.50 Daenerys VIII) 
 Rabbits are not a species that flocks, they thrive in semi-arid grasslands rather than deserts, they are strongly associated with fertility, softness, and prey. Wild rabbits don't have floppy ears, and ears are not something that can be put on and removed at will (well, yes, they can be removed at will, and I suppose if it was a metaphor for hearing, you can listen or not at will). Rabbits have two hierarchies, male and female, the male more aggressive, the female more territorial, but being at the top of a hierarchy wins  a rabbit a 50% longer reproductive life...their social structure is all about the kits (although neither a breeding female in captivity, or the female at the top of the hierarchy is called a queen). And they are famously agile, able to change direction in a heartbeat.
 All in all, rabbits (especially tame ones, with floppy ears) seem a less than fitting metaphor for Daenerys, the Tokar, Meereenese society. But maybe that is the point. Rabbits thrive in places like Westeros, in Westeros they are bred by the smallfolk for meat and pelts. Maybe Daenerys finally makes it to Westeros, the Tallharts come to her side but she is killed by Arya, or brought North uneaten thanks to Bran's new warging/greenseeing skillz.
 If it turns out that the unmixed blood of the Rhyonar was in fact the floppy ears of a tame rabbit, that all Daenarys' hatred of oppression could not make her less the embodiment of it, it might not be an awkward metaphor, but brilliant foreshadowing.
 My point is, the Song is not sung. There are a lot of dissonant notes that may be a feature of the composition, or a fumble in the execution, and I won't know for sure which is which until the final chord is struck.
 I loved the TV series Rosanne when it first came out, but stopped watching in disgust half-way through the last series because it had become so pointlessly removed from reality. Years after, I saw a repeat of the last episode of the whole series. There was a point. That was really smart writing. It totally transformed my opinion of the 'filler' episodes of series nine, and the climax of series eight, and many other aspects of the whole series
 If, at the moment, there are many places where, to me, GRRM's prose seems uninspired, clichéd, repetitive, in stark contrast to his plotting and his characters, what do I know? This guy has been a professional writer since before I was born. He has taught writing, he has done research, and even with very plain and occasionally clunky prose, aSoIaF is addictive, every page brimming with compelling reasons to turn it, and with more and vastly different reasons appearing on a second and (here I can only speak for GoT) a third reading. It does what masterful prose is supposed to do. 
 So while I'd compare him more with Walter Scott or Stephen King than James Joyce or Ernest Hemingway at the moment, my opinion is provisional. It seems to me that the last two books are stylistically stronger than the first two, which makes me even more reluctant to dismiss the awkward metaphors as mistakes. Sometimes reading GRRM seems a bit like watching the paint on Michaelangelo's ceiling dry. He is no amateur, he knows this is his master work, and he seems to be just coming into his stride. And of all the authors I have read, the only one I can think of that handles indeterminacy better is Jane Austen (and he uses some of her tricks, too.) When I get impatient about the wait, I remember this. Fifteen to thirty years is not too long to wait for a master work. 
 ETA: On the offchance that a personal friend of GRRM's is reading this, it took Jane Austen less than a year to write her master work.

 Having read plenty of fantasy, I can't complain about his descriptions at all. They are quite concise compared to most (if you complain about the food descriptions, don't ever read Auel where you get pages on a hide, type of grasses, etc). Enough to give you an impression (including smell and noise). His skill is how he can alter the choice of words based on the POV. Each character truly has their own voice. But dialogue and character interaction is perhaps his greatest asset, and on rereads I find he deliberately uses certain words, adjectives and verbs and dialogue reveal a particular deliberation.
 For example, in Arya's arc the description of the secondary and tertiary characters alters with her level of attention. We know at least less than half about Hot Pie's facial expressions than we know of Gendry's. Shortly after meeting both he writes 'deep blue eyes' in relation to Gendry. Sure it's not elaborate and poetic proze, but just the addition of "deep" gives us enough subtle clues that Arya is highly focused on Gendry, way more than she is on Hot Pie. In another scene, she's making him hide with her for the Gold Cloaks. She's focusing on the Gold Cloaks, telling him to shut up, yet notices his ear smells of soap. Does it matter? No, it doesn't in a descriptive way. Since earlier she already noticed his hair being wet. We already know he's taken a bath. But in the middle of what is happening, Arya noticing he smells of soap (no matter how plain it's written), is a cue for us about Arya's high focus on him. 
 One can make an analogy for Arya with the story of the Ugly Duckling. I actually reread the Anderson story as it was written by Anderson, and discovered it's not just a loose analogy. He follows Anderson's story step by step - even by event. In Anderson's story at some point the Ugly Duckling arrives at a ruinous cottage of an old woman where a cat and a hen act as if they are the masters of the house. They think the world of themselves - the better half of the world. They believe they are smart, and that working hard is all you need to do. The cat can "purr", curl his back and "crackle" when rubbed the wrong way. The hen of course "cackles" and lays eggs. Harrenhal is not a cottage but a ruins. Goodwife Amabel pretty much acts out the hen and Weese the cat. And GRRM does use the verb "cackles" with Goodwife Amabel, and both "purred" and "crackled" with Weese who's the cat in that setting. Later Gendry takes part of the cat rol when he refuses to help Arya free the prisoners and escape, telling her he's fine working with master Lucan. Not without coincidence Gendry positions the piece of metal he's been working on into water and it "hisses like a cat".
 His proze is plain. It doesn't seem out of the ordinary or special, and yet it contains often deliberate word choices. In that way the proze's plainness is deceptive.
