That register, one calm voice compiling a record from sources it names and does not fully trust, is the register a settlement dossier uses, and section G below reconstructs it directly. The novels' engine (a rotating cast of tight third-person narrators, chapter-end cliffhangers, dialogue that outshines narration) does not transfer to an archivist; what transfers from the novels is the ground beneath the engine: the native-root lexicon, real old words in their offices, sensory nouns of food, cloth and blazon, sayings as the marks of a culture, names bent from real ones, a world that has plagues and pageantry at once, and consequences traced from every departure from the real. Each feature says which side of that line it falls on.

## A. Lexicon

### 1. Native-root diction: Anglo-Saxon words in, Latinate words out

Sources: 9 voices. Simon, two posts [0] [7] [77] [215] [237] [497] [505] [869] [870] [871] [874] [876] [945] [946] [952]; Mendelsohn in the New York Review [443]; Jennings in the Times [726]; the Saskatchewan History of English blog [320]; Rivera [68] [321] [659] [660] [923] [924]; Spathis at the primary [643] and via Winter Is Coming [69]; AutoCrit [323] [964] [968]; the TeachingBooks and LightSail catalogues [972] [973] [989]; Greenlight Coverage [148].

True wording: "a high frequency of words of Anglo-Saxon origin" [0]; "consistently chooses words of Anglo-Saxon origin" [7]; "a systematic exclusion from his vocabulary" [505]; "a cumulative feeling of woodiness, antiquity, pithiness" [497]; "The result is to make his prose feel archaic." [952]; "the ingeniously quasi-medieval diction and spellings of names" [443]; "a blunt and bawdy earthiness" [726]; "Martin hits a sweet spot between Modern and Middle English" [320]; "The ARI score for A Game of Thrones is sixth-grade" [68]; "comes in with a Flesch Reading Ease score of 87" [968]; "Lexile Level: 830L" [972]; "ATOS Reading Level: 5.5" [973]; "Game of Thrones to 11-year-olds" [643].

What the sources say. Simon's two posts make the strongest and most detailed case: the vocabulary systematically excludes almost all words from Latin, Greek and French, the feel of antiquity is cumulative and comes from that filter rather than from any pseudo-medieval marker [6] [869] [952] [876], and the lexical choice matters as much as the subject matter [876]. He reports noticing one really Latinate word in the fourth book [874], but his own post names five others in that book, so the figure is his impression and not a count that survives his page (the verifier flags this). Mendelsohn finds the diction and spellings ingeniously quasi-medieval [443]; Jennings hears a blunt, bawdy earthiness [726]. The Saskatchewan blog independently finds accurate late Middle English words used sparingly under modern syntax, with no thou or thee [320], and Rivera adds that each old term sits in a context that lets the reader decipher it without disruption [660]. The measured figures agree from the other side: a sixth-grade readability index and a lexical density near four [68] [321] [659] [923] [924], a Flesch reading ease of eighty-seven, which AutoCrit calls conversational English [323] [968], a summary score AutoCrit places in the bestseller band [964], a reading age of ten or eleven [69] [643], a Lexile of eight hundred and thirty and an ATOS level of five and a half [972] [973] [989]. Greenlight reads the archaic-and-modern blend as skilful [148]. Caution: the etymological census is one critic's; the other eight corroborate plainness, reading level and a period feel, not the census. Lanchester's long essay, the one general-interest essayist reached in full, contains no verdict on diction, sentence or dialogue at all; its praise is structural [439], so it counts here for nothing.

Reconstruction rule. The archivist writes in the old stock of the language. Hall, ward, hearth, kin, tithe, ford, mill, oath, fold, field, bread, harvest. Words that end in tion, ment, ity and ance are held back; population becomes folk or households, administration becomes the council or the reeve's office, infrastructure becomes roads, wells, walls and mills, regulation becomes the town's rule or custom. The sentence stays short enough for a child of ten to follow, however grave the matter. This is the base lexicon, unrestricted; feature 5 draws the line between it and the rationed old word.

### 2. Native affixes: un, be, the positional a, and the Old English suffixes

Sources: 1 voice. Simon, 2013 post [1] [2] [79] [219] [238] [506] [875] [947] [949]. SINGLE SOURCE.

True wording: "Almost none of these are found in Martin's work." [1] (of the Latin and Greek prefixes); "Martin uses almost exclusively English (i.e. Anglo-Saxon) prefixes" [506]; "atop, abed, ahorse" [238]; "convey a deep sense of their Anglo-Saxon provenance" [2].

What the source says. Prefixes are almost exclusively un and be, with the archaic positional a conspicuously used [1] [238] [875] [949]; the suffixes that recur are craft, dom, hood, ness, less, ling, ship and ward, each with an Old English pedigree [2] [506]; the Latin prefixes sub, super, inter and post are hardly present [79] [947], with re the admitted exception, and Latin and Greek forms are reserved for learned or religious contexts [219]. The verifier notes the page asserts rarity for Latinate affixes as a class, not for the four named suffixes in particular [238].

Reconstruction rule. Where the archivist needs a derived word, the suffix is native: kingship not monarchy, craft not industry, hardihood not resilience, wardship not custody, homeward not in the direction of. The positional a belongs to feature 5's third class: it is a dated form, allowed once in a quoted mouth and not in the archivist's own sentence.

### 3. Compounds and coinages: two short native roots make the world; an invented tongue is kept to a few words

Sources: 8 voices. Simon [3] [10] [78] [88] [220] [239] [245] [507] [508]; Mendelsohn [442]; Bednarska [54] [55]; Martin on faking languages via IGN [165], in Not a Blog [287], in the New Yorker [457] and as quoted by Honegger [769]; David J. Peterson on the book Dothraki [661] [662] [663] [664]; HBO's press release [665]; Jennings on the person-names [725]; Greenlight [148].

True wording: "coin scores of wonderful and evocative neologisms" [3]; "the greater the sense of moving into his otherworld" [220]; "derives not from a handful of obvious stock phrases" [508]; "'sellsword' for 'mercenary,' 'holdfast' for 'fort'" [442]; "most neologisms have the lexical character" [54]; "created its name on the basis of the phrase 'dire wolf'" [55]; "He invented entire languages, I just fake it." [165]; "playing with the syntax and sentence rhythms a bit" [287]; "I don't have a gift for languages" [769]; "The spellings in the books are canon." [662]; "In the names Pono and Bharbo" [663]; "Dothraki is now up to 3,163 words" [661].

What the sources say. Existing compounds of the warlike and feudal kind (battleground, bedchamber, breastplate, drawbridge, longship, warhammer) sit beside coinages cast in the same mould (sellsword, godswood, smallfolk, greenseer, dragonglass, skinchanger, weirwood, ironborn) [78] [239] [507]; Simon counts the fourth book's compounds at well over a hundred [239] and argues that the sense of an otherworld comes from their frequency on every page and not from any single stock medievalism such as prithee [220] [508]; the wholly new medieval-sounding words (maester, septon, warg) are few [10] [88] [245]. Mendelsohn calls the compound coinages imaginative linguistic evocations of the invented cultures [442]. Bednarska classifies the neologisms as lexical, made by compounding and affixation, mostly toponyms and proper names, and shows direwolf built from the paleontological phrase so a reader infers a very large wolf [54] [55]. Martin himself invents only the words he needs, about eight Dothraki words and seven of High Valyrian, and fakes the rest [165] [457]; a foreign tongue is conveyed by bending English syntax and rhythm [287]; he says plainly that he has no gift for languages [769]. Peterson, who built the screen Dothraki, treats the spellings printed in the books as canon, finds the letters p and b only in two book names, and reached more than three thousand words where Martin had a handful [661] [662] [663] [664] [665]. Jennings finds bliss in the person-names and place-names [725]. Disagreement of scope only: Simon locates the otherworld in the compounds, Bednarska in the proper names; both are verified and describe different word classes.

Reconstruction rule. The dossier's institutions are named by compounding two plain roots: moot-hall, tithe-barn, ward-reeve, fish-wharf, salt-road, kin-feud, oath-day, watch-fire. Each compound must decode itself from its parts in the way direwolf does. Invented single words are rationed to what the setting truly needs and are used in the same sense and spelling every time; a spelling once printed is canon. A foreign people's speech, where reported, is marked by word order and rhythm, never by an invented vocabulary of more than a few words.

### 4. Register keyed to rank and office: learned words for learned mouths

Sources: 5 voices. Simon [4] [80] [948]; Rivera [66] [321] [657]; Tosina Fernández [52]; Mendelsohn on the high and low register in one mouth [446]; the Gyldayn excerpt's courtly titles [363].

True wording: "technocratic and legalistic mindset of medieval religious inquisitors" [4]; "in the context of legal or religious or courtly matters" [80]; "A sort of dialect ruled by rank rather than region" [66]; "mock-medieval epic asking us to see beyond glitter to gore" [446]; "whilst; mayhaps; amongst" [363].

What the sources say. The rare Latinate words (fornication, adultery, intercession) are reserved for maesters, septons and courts, and stand out against the native backdrop [4] [80] [948]. Low-born speakers say m'lord where the high-born say my lord, a dialect of rank rather than region [66] [321] [657]. Proverbs sit chiefly with the upper class, against the usual folk association [52]. Mendelsohn reads Tyrion setting magnificent beside shit as a pointed deflation of romance [446]. The chronicle's own voice holds its register with consistent formal designations, His Grace, the queen, Lord Stark, and a scatter of whilst and mayhaps, with a fool's colloquial line let through [363].

Reconstruction rule. The archivist's own voice stays native. When the dossier quotes or paraphrases a charter, a court, a priest or a physician, a Latinate word may appear as the mark of that office, and only there. Rank shows in the form of address the dossier reports (the reeve is my lord to the ferryman and Aldous to his brother), never in adjectives about rank. A title once given is given the same way every time.

### 5. Real words bent, and old words with dates: the three classes of old word

Sources: 8 voices. Simon [89] [245] [499] [867]; Pulford at OUPblog [318]; Germani [257] [819]; the Saskatchewan blog [320]; Rivera [660]; Kirner-Ludwig on the screen transcripts [669] [670] [675]; the Gyldayn excerpt [363]; Mendelsohn on period nouns [444]. Partials cited for their quotation only: Martin at the Nottingham signing [162] [163] [164], whose quoted words are the con reporter's paraphrase; Pulford's dates [228]; the tumblr row on nuncle [311]; Germani on modern leaks [258].

True wording: "deformation of existing standard words into something rich and strange" [89]; "by sheer repetition, comes to seem the natural term" [499]; "the distorted English becomes your home setting" [867]; "Martin's archaic-sounding amendment of turncoat" [318]; "a few linguistic relics to give a historical veneer" [257]; "the ability to decipher the older meaning" [660]; "may not be particularly frequent token-wise, but are highly salient" [669]; "does not overdo the use of pseudo-archaisms" [670]; "destriers, palfreys, vair, samite give concrete reality; undramatizable" [444].

What the sources say. Ser for sir, flowered for a girl's first blood, turncloak for turncoat: ordinary words are slightly deformed and, by repetition over scores of chapters, become the reader's home setting [89] [245] [499] [867]. Pulford confirms that ser, southron and craven are attested fifteenth and fourteenth century forms (the verifier corrects the century for craven) and that turncloak is Martin's own remake [318]; his row on the dates is partial only because the claim called him a lexicographer and he works in marketing [228]. Germani lists the relics (must needs, soon or late, near as) that most fantasists settle for as veneer [257] [819], and elsewhere the modern leaks (pug-nosed, longshoreman, condone, yen, burp, snark) at which "Martin shows you the seams of his work" [258] (partial: two of the dates are not first-use dates). Mendelsohn's period nouns, destriers and palfreys, vair and samite, give the world its concrete reality and cannot be filmed [444]. The Saskatchewan blog and Rivera find the old words used sparingly and always decipherable from context [320] [660]. Kirner-Ludwig, measuring the screen transcripts rather than the novels, finds archaisms rare in token terms and highly salient, and judges that the show does not overdo them, while noting that Martin himself has no expert knowledge of the historical stages of English [669] [670] [675]. The chronicle voice carries whilst, amongst and mayhaps in narration [363]. The nuncle row reports the archaic-ing thickening from the fourth book "GRRM deliberately archaic-ing his language" [311] (partial: the other two words carry no book range). Martin's own reported compromise, "modern prose, but omitting all pop culture references" [162], and the editor's fear that "would follow shortly" a forsooth after mayhaps [163], survive only as a fan reporter's paraphrase and cannot be cited as his words.

Reconciliation (the chair's first self-contradiction). The previous section made reeve, tithe, hide and ward both base lexicon and rationed salt. The sources support three classes, and the rules now name them. Class one, native-root words still in daily use (hall, ward, tithe, ford, oath, harvest): unrestricted; this is feature 1. Class two, real dated words that are the names of things the settlement has (reeve, hide, hundred, wergild, glebe, assart, toll-bar, moot, tithe-barn): unrestricted as names, used every time the thing is meant and never as ornament, because a name for an institution is not a flavour word; this is Mendelsohn's destrier and samite [444] and Rivera's decipherable term [660], and it is these that carry the antiquity cumulatively, which is Simon's own mechanism [869] [952]. Class three, dated forms of manner and function (mayhaps, oft, elsewise, whilst, amongst, must needs, nuncle, the positional a, the dropped ly, ser): the salt. Class three is barred in the archivist's own sentence and admitted once, in a quoted mouth, an elder's saying or a charter's text, where the older form marks the speaker's age or the document's date. The old rule of one old word to a paragraph applies to class three alone.

Reconstruction rule. Bend a familiar word only where the bend is a rule of the world (ser for a knight) and then never vary it. Age the vocabulary by the speaker, not by the archivist: an old charter or an elder's line may carry the older form; the archivist's own sentence carries the modern one. No word the reader could date to a factory, a newspaper or a screen.

### 6. Register instability and cod medievalism: the standing criticism. CORRECTS MARTIN

Sources: 6 voices. Simon, 2014 post [5] [13] [81] [83] [84] [217] [241] [246] [495] [498] [862] [868]; Gelzer-Govatos [90] [92] [223] [324] [509] [510]; Pulford on butt and ass [318]; Doyle's parody [491]; Kirner-Ludwig [675]; Sady Doyle and the Overthinking It commenter (partial) [226]. Partials for quotation only: [226] [258] [311].

True wording: "prose larded with fake medievalisms" [5]; "veers from purely functional modern thriller prose" [241]; "would-be medievalisms which become annoying mannerisms" [246]; "removing the -ly suffix from adverbs" [83]; "as if in modern New York" [84]; "'Among' and 'while' become the archaic 'amongst' and 'whilst'" [498]; "faux-medieval flourishes go a bridge too far" [92]; "words and phrases he rides like a hobbyhorse" [223]; "Jape, aside from its pretentiousness, is a singularly displeasing word." [510]; "In reality they just sound ridiculous." [509]; "in days of yore, before the Striding Elves sailed West" [491]; "I think GRRM has a penchant for pseudo-archaic language" [226] (partial: one commenter, not several).

What the sources say. Simon's thesis is that the style is unstable: on one page fifteenth-century oft and elsewise and a Latinate lexicon suddenly stuffed in, and then things divvied up and someone asking what that is all about as if in modern New York [5] [81] [84] [217] [241] [495] [862]; the most persistent mannerism is the dropped ly (like to be angry, near finished), with oft, elsewise, amongst, whilst and much and more behind it [13] [83] [246] [498] [868]. Gelzer-Govatos names jape for joke and mummer's farce for every hostile council as pretension, words Martin presumably thinks sound archaic and exotic that sound ridiculous, and rates the prose at best serviceable [90] [92] [223] [324] [509] [510]. Pulford hears butt and ass jar against the fifteenth-century words [318]; Doyle's parody of the register opens in days of yore with unpronounceable names [491]; Kirner-Ludwig's judgment that the screen version does not overdo it sits against the novels' critics, and she attributes Martin's slips to a lack of philological training [670] [675]. Disagreement with feature 5 is explicit: the archaisms are salt to the Saskatchewan blog and Rivera [320] [660], mannerism to Simon and Gelzer-Govatos, and the modern leaks are a separate fault that no source defends.

Reconstruction rule. The two failure modes are named and both are barred in the archivist's own voice. No manufactured archaism: no oft, elsewise, mayhaps, jape, mummer's farce, must needs, dropped ly (class three of feature 5). No modern leak: no word or idiom the reader would date to the reader's century. The register is checked page by page for both faults, because the fault is the swing between them, not either alone. This corrects Martin: the sources show him doing both, deliberately by the reporter's account [162] [163] and by the tumblr reader's [311], and the rule takes the critics' side because a dossier has no character whose age the old form could mark except in quoted speech.

## B. Sentence, paragraph and figure

### 7. The default register is lucid, functional and quick; the verdict on it splits

[222]; "the other 60% of the text is forgettable" [882]; "his prose holds him back from being a truly great author" [514]; "Tolkien was never quite so ponderous" [531]; "But he writes pulp. There is no theme; there is no moral." [516]; "Martin is imprecise, not incompetent." [826]; "Flow between sentences is the litmus test" [827].

What the sources say. The praise and the blame agree on the description and differ on the verdict. The baseline is crisp and clear, with occasional flashes of limpid beauty, an uneven style that is often powerful [82] [216] [242] [504]; a reading group calls it high definition, crisp and fast [313]; the first trade review called it conventional in form but accomplished in prose [541] [689] [690]; a small-press reviewer says it never falls below the highest craft [550]; Pompella grants an uncanny prose style of detailed realism inside a hostile review [551]; Church rates it well above average for commercial prose and not the work of a seven-draft reviser [558]; Jennings hears vivid language [733]; García, his co-author, calls the poetic passages gorgeous and places Martin's gift in atmosphere and theme where Kay's is in the sentence [617] [618]; the first book's early reviewers rated it a competently told widescreen epic that aspires to page-turner status [33] [34]; the Citadel review found it grounded in history and reality [636]. Grossman calls the prose deft and a pleasure in itself, in a review now reached raw [45] [120] [518]. Germani grants an almost hypnotic readability that never makes you read a sentence twice [221] [249] [815] [878] and, in the same essay, that no sentence is beautiful and the sixty percent that is not dialogue is forgettable [222] [882]; his considered verdict is that Martin is imprecise rather than incompetent and passes the test that matters, flow between sentences [826] [827]. Against: Orr says the length hurts the prose [35] [713]; Brown finds the prose unexceptional and the idioms uniform and the book crying out for an editor [36] [233] [720] [721]; Radowski calls it shoddy, inconsistent and not poetic [465] [471]; Gelzer-Govatos says the prose is what holds him back [514]; Kirkus finds him more ponderous than Tolkien [531] [703]; Kain finds the fifth book unedited and half again too long [478] [479]; Cariaga calls it pulp [516]; a forum reader finds it in places uninspired, clichéd and repetitive against the plotting [850]. Wasson's concise [716] and Maenpaa's never concise [116] (partial) contradict in adjective and are resolved by noticing that Wasson describes sentences and Maenpaa the stories.

Reconstruction rule. The archivist's sentence is short, declarative and plain: the mill stands at the ford; the reeve holds the key; the harvest fails in the wet year. Beauty, where it comes, comes from the plainness (feature 13), not from ornament. The dossier is concise at the sentence and generous at the whole, which is the shape both camps describe. The measure of a passage is flow between sentences, not any one sentence's weight; the measure of the whole is that a reader would not call it unedited.

### 8. Adverbs are scarce and the modifiers are disciplined: the measured record

Reconstruction rule. The archivist uses almost no adverbs of manner and never a ly adverb inside a reported line. Where an adverb appears it carries a judgment the town itself holds (the levy is raised unwisely; the well is sunk too near the midden), which is the archivist reporting what the settlement thinks, not decorating a verb. Adjectives are rationed to those that carry a civic fact (a green banner, a wet year, a walled town); eye colour and other bodily decoration are not recorded. Three of a kind is the ceiling for any list. The generator's readability walker measures ly adverbs and all adverbs separately, because the sources show the two diverge.

### 9. Low-density sentences that work by volley; flow first, trim last

Sources: 5 voices. Germani [97] [248] [815] [824] [825] [827] [878] [879] [883] and (partial) [100] [259] [260]; Martin on cutting fat via Wikipedia [25], on line-by-line trimming via Hidden Gems [290], on constant revision in So Spake Martin [561] [562], on rewriting too much in the New Yorker [459], on the first chapter arriving whole [206] [293] and on cutting a loved chapter [329]; Huffman on sentence endings [884] [885]; AutoCrit on long sentences [966]; Becce's sentence lengths [653] [654]; the forum reader Harroway on chapter lengths [963].

True wording: "a full cartridge of it gets the point across" [97]; "no single sentence is that effective" [248]; "a steady flow of low-density sentences" [878]; "It's shotgun writing" [879]; "Martin's paragraphs are like Jenga towers" [824]; "built from the first sentences of 26 different paragraphs" [825]; "they come easily off the pen" [883]; "modular sentences that can snap together like LEGO" [259] (partial: one of several speculations); "Martin has a fair number of one-lines" [260] (partial); "he stresses rhythm" [100] (partial: the essayist's gloss). Against: "cutting out the fat and leaving the muscle" [25]; "trimming and tightening line by line and word by word" [290]; "I revise constantly as I go along, always honing and polishing" [561]; "change chapters three, nine, and twenty-one" [562]; "tinkering with some parts of A Dance with Dragons for ten years" [459]; "almost in the form you've read" [206]; "I wrote a Tyrion chapter I just loved" [329]. Measured: "Martin knows just where to drop his periods" [884]; "break up large descriptions into easily digestible sentences" [885]; "a comparatively small 851 sentences containing more than 30 words" [966]; "in the early books, you alternate between long and short chapters" [963].

What the sources say. Germani calls it shotgun writing: a steady flow of low-density sentences, none strong alone, effective by the cartridge [97] [248] [815] [878] [879]; paragraphs are Jenga towers from which several sentences can be pulled, and he assembles a readable passage of four hundred and twenty-seven words out of the first sentences of twenty-six paragraphs totalling nearly nineteen hundred [824] [825]; stock phrases persist because they come easily to a writer working for flow [883]. The modular-LEGO reading and the one-line-paragraph driver are his speculations, not findings [259] [260], and the flow-over-machining preference is his gloss rather than Martin's words [100]. Martin's own account runs the other way and forward in time: he revises constantly as he goes, a new idea in chapter fifty-two sending him back to three, nine and twenty-one [561] [562]; the final pass is the screenwriter's cut, which took almost eighty pages out of one book [25] [290]; he suspects he rewrites too much and tinkered with one chapter for ten years [459]; the first chapter came out in three days almost as read [206] [293]; a loved chapter was cut because it would change the whole book [329]. Huffman finds him knowing where to end a sentence inside a long description [884] [885]; AutoCrit counts few sentences over thirty words [966]; Becce measures dialogue at about eleven words a sentence for men and a little under for women [653] [654]; a forum reader notices the early books alternating long and short chapters where the later two run equally long [963]. Disagreement: the flow-first drafting and the trim-last habit are compatible in sequence, but Germani's evidence is that the trim leaves the density low, and Martin's is that the trim is real.

Reconstruction rule. The dossier's paragraph is built so that its first sentence could stand alone and the rest could be cut without loss of sense. Each sentence carries one fact. Momentum comes from the succession of plain facts, not from any single sentence's weight. The archivist trims: no sentence restates the one before, and no sentence runs past thirty words. Section lengths vary; a run of equal sections reads as filler.

### 10. Stock phrases, epithets and refrains: the Homeric machinery and its cost. CORRECTS MARTIN

Sources: 16 voices. Germani [95] [96] [98] [221] [247] [249] [251] [883]; Grossman [519]; Martin on intentional repetition [198] [282], on veering away from repeating himself [602], and in the 1983 speech [632] [633]; Overthinking It [225] and its commenter [227]; Groell's editing via Pajiba [563] [564] [567]; Whitehead on the novels [107] and on the world book [738] [739]; Gelzer-Govatos [324] [511]; Orr [35] [712]; Brown [36] [233] [720]; Radowski [466] [470]; Sheehan on the motto [538]; Publishers Weekly on recurring images [700]; Winter Is Coming on food phrases [137]; Rosenberg on the Reek punning [487]; Hurley on embedded reminders [71]; Simon undecided [9] [86]. Partials for quotation only: [91] [243] [296] [502].

True wording: "Martin as one of those minstrels" [95]; "to give their audience time to absorb" [96]; "almost a shorthand for description" [98]; "stock phrases are thermals" [247]; "we are cognitively lazy with the familiar" [249]; "those faces still as masks, and the faces dark with anger" [251]; "Like Wagner, he gives each of his characters leitmotifs" [519]; "no writer uses a phrase thirteen times by accident" [225]; "I have learned this technique from Stephen King" [198]; "but I do it intentionally" [282]; "The repeated phrase she tried to cut back on" [563]; "(which appears 14 times)" [564]; "on the road toward stagnation and self-parody" [632]; "themes recur, a distinctive voice develops" [633]; "I try to veer away from that" [602]. Against: "the phrase comes up at least ten times in every book" [511] (mummer's farce); "'Words are wind,' is oft-repeated, probably a little bit too much" [107]; "warm bread, fresh from the kitchens" [137]; "The general prose style is reasonable, although prone to repetition." [738]; "a phrase used and then re-used just a few paragraphs later" [739]; "constant punning on Reek irritates but shows the reinforcement" [487]; "These reminders are choreographed by creative dialogue with other characters" [71]; undecided: "signs of haste, or careful repetitions" [9].

What the sources say. Germani's thesis is that Martin is an American Homer: fixed epithets and stock phrases (black as night four times nearly unchanged, faces still as masks, men corded or thick with muscle) keep pace by triggering recognition rather than fresh sight, at the cost of memorable sentences, and they are the minstrel's breathing room and the writer's thermals [95] [96] [98] [221] [247] [249] [251] [883]. Grossman reads the recurring phrases as Wagnerian leitmotifs in each character's stream of thought [519]; Overthinking It counts words are wind thirteen times in one book across every faction and calls it motif [225]; Martin says the repetition of sayings is intentional and learned from Stephen King, despite reader annoyance [198] [282]; his editor tried to cut that phrase, which appears fourteen times in the fifth book, and Martin was stubborn [563] [564] [567]. Yet Martin's own theory of craft, stated in 1983 and again in 2022, is that a writer who repeats himself too often is on the road to self-parody and that he veers away from resonances between his histories and his novels [632] [633] [602]. Disagreement, and it is the sharpest in the file: Orr, Brown, Whitehead, Gelzer-Govatos and Radowski read the same recurrence as excess and haste [35] [36] [107] [233] [324] [466] [511] [712] [720]; Whitehead finds the world book prone to a phrase reused a few paragraphs on [738] [739]; Publishers Weekly notices recurring images rather than phrases in the fifth book [700]; Sheehan simply reports the characters repeating the motto [538]; Rosenberg finds the Reek punning irritating and yet an illustration of brainwashing by repetition [487]; Radowski reproduces the duel's refrain at length as flat, unbuilt violence [470]; a commenter grants the leitmotifs and still flags the tics [227]; Hurley praises the reminders planted in other mouths [71]; Simon cannot decide whether cool and still recur by haste or design [9] [86]. The fixed phrase is verified as a real device and a real fault.

Reconstruction rule. The dossier may own a small number of fixed formulae, each tied to one civic thing and repeated in the same words every time it recurs: the bell that calls the moot, the ford that floods at thaw, the saying the town keeps. These are the settlement's epithets. Everything else is said once. No adjective of appearance recurs as a tag; no phrase is reused for convenience; no phrase recurs within a few paragraphs. A formula the reader will meet more than twice must be a saying the town itself repeats, so its recurrence is the world's and not the writer's. This corrects Martin: he defends the repetition and kept it against his editor [198] [282] [567]; the rule sides with the editor and the critics, and with Martin's own stated law against self-parody [632].

### 11. One word repeated through a passage for cohesion

Sources: 2 voices. Rivera [67] [321] [658]; Simon [9] [86], with the purposive reading partial [243] [502].

True wording: "the insult is repeated five times" [67]; "signs of haste, or careful repetitions" [9]; "of 'cool' and 'still'" [243] (partial: the source refuses to settle the purpose).

What the sources say. Bastard is repeated five times across a few paragraphs to bind them and to press Jon's stigma, which Rivera names lexical cohesion [67] [321] [658]; cool and still recur through the Water Palace passage and Simon leaves it undecided between haste and a torpid atmosphere [9] [86]; the claim that the repetition builds stillness states one of his two alternatives as fact and is partial [243] [502].

Reconstruction rule. The archivist may repeat one plain word through a short passage to hold it together, and the word is a noun the passage is about (the wall, the debt, the flood), never an adjective of mood, because the only verified case of the noun (bastard) is read as design and the only case of the adjective (cool, still) is read as possible haste.

### 12. Simile is the dominant figure; the figures are bodily and sometimes misfire

Sources: 5 voices. Ehiosun at Book Analysis [144]; Weed [300] [301]; Germani [102] [103] [105] [261] [263] [275] [833] [834] and (partial) [267]; Attewell [306]; Huffman [887].

True wording: "simile was the most predominant figurative language used" [144]; "Fear filled his gut like a meal he could not digest." [300]; "like an animal screaming in pain" [301] (the sound of swords, with no ring of metal); "cruel iron rams lapping at the water" [275]; "I found ~250 examples of it" [834]; "148,130 by my count" [833]; "an 'iron rose, thorns gleaming' is typical" [306]; "I examine twenty-five metaphors and similes" [887]. [105]; "time slept when swords woke" [267] (partial: personify is the claimant's label).

What the sources say. Simile predominates, with personification of natural forces [144]. The best figures make emotion bodily (fear as an undigested meal) [300] and defamiliarise a stock sound (swords screaming like an animal) [301]. Germani counts about two hundred and fifty zeugmatic sentences in roughly one hundred and forty-eight thousand, most about the body and about sky or weather [263] [833] [834], praises the homonym in rams lapping at the water [275], and lists the misfires: brigands melting like snow (snow melts slowly), glowering through a beard, a low quiet voice, anger both cold and hot [102] [103] [105] [261]. Reconstruction rule. The archivist's figures are few and drawn from the town's own stock: the granary, the ford, the forge, the herd. A simile must be true to the physics of its vehicle. Paired near-synonyms are barred (low, quiet). One figure to a section, and it should be a plain noun's likeness, not a personification of an abstraction.

Reconciliation (the chair's distance contradiction). ### 17. Dialogue is terse and charged; it outshines narration and does exposition's work

True wording: "terse, charged dialogue" [12]; "a real dramatic shock" [87]; "it's no surprise that dialogue would shine brightest" [250]; "These tags, when strategically placed, can serve as extra punctuation" [264]; "Martin's dialogue does a great job of revealing social dynamics." [277]; "I think working in Hollywood sharpened my dialog." [172]; "It's all structure, structure and dialogue." [173]; "much shorter back and forth kind of, kind of dialogue" [171]; "Being there improved my sense of structure and dialogue" [138]; "There's no Dornish accent." [600]; "explain past events to people who don't need them explained" [582] (Druon's fault, not Martin's); "Romanian translation made awkward dialogues less painful" [851].

What the sources say. Confrontations are carried by terse, charged exchanges whose implications land as shock [12] [87] [218] [501]; the contrast with narration is the claimant's framing, not Simon's (partial) [244]. Germani's Reddit-grounded finding is that readers remember the dialogue and forget the narration [99] [250]; tags and action beats are placed as extra punctuation and the ideal exchange, in his opinion, is untagged [264]; dialogue exposes hierarchy [277]. Martin credits television with shorter back-and-forth and sharper lines [138] [171] [172] [173], and admits that a Dornish accent exists only as an instruction to the reader and the audiobook actor [600]. Reconstruction rule. A dossier has no scenes, but it has reported speech: the town's sayings, a charter's clause, an elder's remembered line. No line tells a listener what the listener already knows. A regional voice is marked by word order and a single word of address, never by spelling.
