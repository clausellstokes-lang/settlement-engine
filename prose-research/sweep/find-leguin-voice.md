# Le Guin prose style — the author's OWN WORDS angle — raw sweep notes (2026-09-06)

Angle: interviews, forewords, essays, blog posts, talks, workshop posts — what Le Guin herself said about prose.
Method: WebSearch budget for the session was exhausted after the first 12 queries (200/200 session cap), so discovery
continued via Bing-through-curl; pages that 403'd WebFetch (Paris Review, LARB, Guardian, Locus, AWP, Guernica, SF Site,
NPR, Interview, Electric Lit, Millions) were fetched with curl + browser UA or via web.archive.org `id_` snapshots and read
as stripped text in `voice-raw/`. Every source below marked READ was read in full or in its full relevant section.

## A. Core primary essay — "From Elfland to Poughkeepsie" (1973) — READ IN FULL
Source: digginganddeepening.com PDF (full text; local copy leguin1973.txt / voice-raw/elfland-pdf.txt). The ursulakleguin.com page
for the essay is a publication note only (no text).
Concrete prose features she states:
- Style is the diagnostic of real fantasy: the Poughkeepsie test — change four words in a Deryni Rising dialogue and it becomes a Washington
  political novel; "You can't clip Pegasus' wings that easily—not if he has wings." Eddison/Morris/Tolkien passages cannot be so converted
  "unless you changed half the words in every sentence."
- "Speech expresses character"; the man who says "I could have told you that at Cardosa" is an "I told you so" man; "Nobody who says, 'I told
  you so' has ever been, or will ever be, a hero." In fantasy "Every word spoken is meaningful."
- Tolkien's dialogue: "an English extraordinary for its simple timelessness"; "It has sobriety, wit, and force. It is the language of men of character."
- Imitation is healthy (baroque composers) but "you can't imitate what somebody does until you've learned how he does it."
- Dunsany: KJV + Irish speech + "delicate ear for speech rhythms"; imitation Dunsany = made-up names, vague gorgeous cities, "a great many
  sentences beginning with 'And.'"
- Archaism is the beginner's trap: "what is wanted in fantasy is a distancing from the ordinary"; thee/thou misuse, subjunctive was→were,
  She-To-Whom trap, fancy words (Eldritch, Tenebrous, smaragds, mayhap, perchance) and "the infallible touchstone of the seventh-rate: Ichor."
- "The archaic manner is indeed a perfect distancer, but you have to do it perfectly. It's a high wire: one slip spoils all." Eddison's
  Elizabethan prose "totally artificial, but it is never faked"; good prose = "exact, clear, powerful" visually precise, and "musically—that is,
  in the sound of the words, the movement of the syntax, and the rhythm of the sentences—it is subtle and very strong."
- Register-alternation fault (Leiber, Zelazny): colloquial American for jokes, "old formal usages" for earnest moments → "I am jerked back and
  forth between Elfland and Poughkeepsie; the characters lose coherence." Consistent tone (Cabell, Vance) is the virtue; Vance's style
  "contains no archaisms at all."
- "After all, archaisms are not essential. You don't have to know how to use the subjunctive in order to be a wizard."
- The epic model: "Most epics are in straightforward language"; Song of Roland 4000 lines, one simile, no metaphors; Mabinogion and sagas
  "as plainspoken as they could well be"; "Clarity and simplicity are permanent virtues in a narrative. Nothing highfalutin is needed.
  A plain language is the noblest of all. It is also the most difficult."
- Tolkien's plain style: "Its outstanding virtue is its flexibility, its variety. It ranges easily from the commonplace to the stately";
  "Tolkien's vocabulary is not striking; he has no ichor; everything is direct, concrete, and simple."
- Fake plainness = journalism: "It is not really simple, but flat. It is not really clear, but inexact"; sensory cues "vague and generalized;
  the rocks, the wind, the trees are not there"; journalism suppresses the author's sensibility for objectivity, "written fast, and read faster";
  "A language intended to express the immediate and the trivial is applied to the remote and the elemental."
- "The style, of course, is the book. If you remove the cake, all you have left is a recipe." "Style is how you as a writer see and speak."
- Why style matters most in fantasy: "there is nothing but the writer's vision of the world... a construct built in a void, with every joint
  and seam and nail exposed"; "The only voice that speaks there is the creator's voice. And every word counts."

## B. Paris Review, Art of Fiction No. 221 (John Wray, Fall 2013) — READ IN FULL via 2016 archive snapshot (live page now paywalled after ~40%)
- Anthropological hinting: "I try to hint at the complexity of the society I'm creating, instead of just referring to an empire."
- Models: "Charles Dickens. Jane Austen. And then... Virginia Woolf. Shoot for the top, always."
- Genre flatness: "genre writers deliberately cultivated an attempt to be styleless, to write a very flat, journalistic prose" — she suspects
  male temperament admiring "the ostentatiously clear, flat style of someone like Hemingway as quintessentially masculine."
- "To me the style is the book, to a large extent. Take Borges. When he experiments with ideas, he is experimenting with form, too."
- Earthsea's register and its later loosening: "In the sixties and seventies, the language of serious fantasy was still based largely on the
  styles of writers of earlier generations—Tolkien, of course, but also Dunsany, Eddison, MacDonald, clear back to Malory. As I began to
  depart from the heroic or adventure tradition of fantasy, I found a less formal vocabulary and a cadence better suited to what I had to say."
- Economy with age: "you don't need fancy stuff any more. You've learned how to just say it... I didn't need all that stuff... Cut!"
- Forward rhythm: "I want the story to have a rhythm that keeps moving forward... Even if the rhythm is very complicated and subtle, that's
  what's going to carry the reader."
- Not minimalism: "I'm not talking minimalism, which is a highly self-conscious mannerist style I can't write... I'm perfectly ready to describe
  a lot and be flowery and emotive, but you can do that briefly and it works better." Model = late Beethoven; old painters "so plain and simple."
- Voice-first composition: Lavinia began "With a voice. With a voice in the ear."
- Form leads: "the form leads you to what you want to say"; genre as form.
- Her agent Virginia Kidd "never meddled with my prose."

## C. AWP Writer's Chronicle, "A Conversation on Craft with Ursula K. Le Guin" (David Naimon, Mar/Apr 2017) — READ IN FULL (archive.org)
(The same conversation is the fiction section of *Conversations on Writing*, Tin House 2018.)
- "I hear what I write... if you are hearing what you write, then you can listen for the right cadence, which will help the sentence run
  clear... you can't find your own voice if you aren't listening for it... And so we get prose that goes clunk, clunk, clunk."
- Woolf: "Style, she says, is rhythm—'the wave in the mind'—the wave, the rhythm are there before the words, and bring the words to fit it."
- Tolkien: "Short rhythms repeated form long rhythms; there's a cyclical repetition in his work... We are caught in this rhythm and are happy there."
- Grammar as tools: writers without grammar are "thrown into a carpenter's shop without ever having learned the names of the tools";
  diagramming = "drawing the skeleton of a horse"; sentences have "A different gait" but a piece follows "a certain underlying, integrating rhythm."
- Grammar bullies vs moral use of language; singular "they" was right until the 18th century; Orwell's essay "went really deep into me.
  Often I'm simply rephrasing Orwell."
- Present tense = "flashlight focus"; past tense "extends into the misty reaches of the subjunctive, the conditional, the future" (from her
  Bone Clocks review); "To assume that the present tense is literally 'now' and the past tense literally remote in time is extremely naïve."
- POV: authorial ("omniscient") POV "is the most flexible and useful of all the points of view. It's the freest"; Tolstoy/Woolf slide between
  minds "so gracefully"; "inconsistent point of view" is the commonest workshop fault; first person and limited third "the easiest ones, the least interesting."
- Conflict is not story: "To see life as a battle is a narrow, Social-Darwinist view, and a very masculine one."
- Patrick O'Brian "for his long sentences, or for his descriptions."
- "Chastity" exercise (invented at 14): a page with no adjectives or adverbs; "You end up with a chaste, plain piece of prose. And because you
  have to put all that energy into the verbs and the nouns instead, it is stronger and richer." "A Terrible Thing to Do": cut a piece in half.
- Steering the Craft excerpt appended: "The sound of the language is where it all begins. The test of a sentence is, Does it sound right?";
  "Dull, choppy, droning, jerky, feeble: these common criticisms of narrative are all faults in the sound of it"; "The chief duty of a narrative
  sentence is to lead to the next sentence."

## D. Lit Hub, "A Writing Lesson from Ursula K. Le Guin" (Steering the Craft ch. 1 excerpt) — READ
- Mind's ear; read aloud "loudly"; exemplars Kipling (exuberant vocabulary), Twain (dialect cadence), Hurston ("hypnotic, fatal, forward drive"
  via repetition), Molly Gloss ("perfect placement and timing of words"); names carry sound-weight (Uriah Heep, Yoknapatawpha);
  techniques: onomatopoeia, alliteration, repetition, rhythm, invented words, dialect, varied sentence length; excluded: rhyme and regular meter.

## E. Lit Hub, "Dictators Are Always Afraid of Poets" (Conversations on Writing, poetry section excerpt) — READ
- "when you're talking about the sound in the rhythm of prose, it is so different from poetry, because it's in a way much coarser. It's a
  very long beat." Swinburne taught her "there is a deeper meaning of story that comes from the beat and the music of the words."
  Woolf: "rhythm is what gives her the book"; "one of these experiential things that we don't really have a vocabulary for."

## F. Book View Cafe workshop posts on ursulakleguin.com (2009–2016) — READ (each page)
- Session 1 (Aug 2015): "Vary the pattern — play around with the order of words, the connections of sentences. Read them aloud…";
  "Stories are not shown, but told"; showing can be static, "telling always involves moving on"; "Load every rift with ore"; a novel is
  "a living architecture"; POV shifting = "psychological de-centering."
- Session 1 Continued: "Any book that tells you to 'write short sentences' probably doesn't contain any sentences worth reading";
  "variety and appropriateness"; race-car novel vs camel-caravan novel; sensory/emotional passages are substance, not padding; manuals' rules are "parrot-squawk."
- Session 1 Part 3: "The ideal is flexibility, a balance of different sentence lengths"; warns against "macho staccato"; O'Brian's long clear
  sentences; passive voice used deliberately ("The cowardly writer says..."); no unpronounceable names, no "sweet swoony names ending in -a";
  a poet's ear for "sounds, rhythms, cadences" carries into prose; imagine the reader as "highly sympathetic, but not an intellectual."
- Session 1 Part 5: read dialogue "OUT LOUD"; too many novels are "disembodied voices yaddering on in a void"; action/description a false
  dichotomy (Hardy); "I don't never not agree subject number and predicate... There are no other Rules"; her process "visceral and intuitional."
- Session 2 (Feb 2016): "You learn an art by doing it – and in the case of literature, doing it means reading it as well as writing it";
  "packing your sentences with specifics, not with generalities"; world-detail "embodied" in concrete language, not listed.
- Session 2 Round 1: prose rhythms work "on a very much larger, longer scale" than verse and lack vocabulary; her image: "monitoring traffic
  on I-5, looking out for problems in the flow"; reading aloud finds "a word or a phrase that tangles up the pace"; conflict-as-story is
  "reductionism"; "Describe your characters... Often one physical detail will do it."
- Navigation Q1: "The way to make something good is to make it well."
- On Rules of Writing / Riffing on Rechy (2009): absolutized "show don't tell" makes writers avoid describing invented worlds and over-rely on
  dialogue and limited-third present; narrator's voice "the most intimate voice of all"; past tense holds temporal depth.
- Online critiques (2015): "If it's somebody else's idea of how to write, not mine, I ignore it."

## G. Blog posts on ursulakleguin.com — READ
- #95 "Are they going to say this is fantasy?" (2015): "The imaginary must be imagined, accurately and with scrupulous consistency";
  "A toneless, inexact language is incapable of creating landscape, meaningful relationship, or credible event."
- #52 "Some Recent Fantasies" (2012): praises "fine, vivid English"; "unlabored imaginative authority and completeness of its setting";
  "a haunting quality, a sense of depth, of unspoken further implication" = "real magic."
- #25 "Petty Expectations" (2011): "Everything in a good story or novel is essential"; beauty "bone-deep"; variety of pace over speed.
- #17 (2011): swearwords as noise — "become a noise that's supposed to intensify the emotion"; she analyses the phonetics of her own
  favourite ("the shh beginning, and the explosive t! ending").
- #51 "The Narrative Gift as a Moral Conundrum" (2012): story = "a coherent, onward movement, taking the reader from Here to There";
  a page-turner can succeed "in the most conventional, banal prose, if the writer has the gift."
- #0 (2010): the word "blog" "sounds like a sodden tree trunk in a bog."
- "Ursula on Ursula" FAQ: pronunciation "affects the sound and rhythm of a sentence – and since names are magic in Earthsea"; vowels
  ah/eh/ee/oh/oo, "all the E's are pronounced, including final e"; "Write. Revise. If possible, publish."
- "Some Assumptions About Fantasy" (2004 talk, on site): fantasy is "an alternate world, outside our history"; rejects pseudo-medieval
  recycling; "I don't write about battles or wars at all"; imagination "the instrument of ethics."
- "A Few Words to a Young Writer": "A writer is a person who cares what words mean, what they say, how they say it."
- "The Panda Story": the comma anecdote; punctuation is meaning.

## H. Interviews — READ
- Guardian, Hari Kunzru, "Wizardry is artistry" (20 Nov 2014): "If you're going to create a world out of whole cloth, that is to say, out
  of words, then you better get the words right." (Kunzru's own description: "clear, clean prose that is never tainted by inkhorn medievalism
  or technological jargon.")
- Guardian, Maya Jaggi, "The magician" (17 Dec 2005): "From JRR Tolkien, 'I learned the trick of hinting at a whole background with a few
  names, so you'd feel situated in a real world, not a fantasy bubble.'" Symbolic language of fantasy "a deep grammar of understanding";
  "to clinch a point is to close it"; leaving the reader free "makes the work inexhaustible"; "You have to shoot as high as you can shoot."
- Guardian online Q&A "Chronicles of Earthsea" (9 Feb 2004): Rowling "stylistically ordinary, imaginatively derivative"; true names — "an
  artist whose medium is words, is likely to find the idea of magic as naming, words as power, a quite natural one"; consolation defended
  with Tolkien behind her; "I sit and listen"; "What the book says, the book says best in its own words"; Ged pronounced "Jed" made her scream;
  Earthsea = "one of the conventional pre-Industrial Revolution worlds of fantasy," Archipelagans "generically farmer/merchant."
- SF Site, Nick Gevers (Nov/Dec 2001, by letter): "Nothing I do is exactly deliberate. But I do work very hard and consciously at my craft.
  At the sound, the flow, the exactness, the connections, the implications of my words." Multiple narrative voices "can lead, paradoxically,
  to brevity"; "short, allusive, suggestive. Have had enough of novels where one voice yatters on and on"; "I have never written a plot-driven
  novel"; second trilogy "sees exactly the same world with different eyes."
- Locus, "A Return to Earthsea" (Sept 2001): "Fiction needs a certain roughness"; "I've read overpolished fiction, and it's kind of a bore";
  polishing "every single little word just right" is the fun part but must stop; "My books are character-driven. I don't have plots; I have
  situations, I have stories."
- Locus, "The Age of Saturn" (Oct 2008): Vergil's battles; "You use what is known to be known" (Delany) then invent.
- Guernica, Alexander Chee, "Breaking into the Spell" (Feb 2008): (Kirkus quote) "Vergil is truly untranslatable; his poetry is the music of
  his language"; the Western Shore books' literacy theme is "the drumbeat they walk to"; passion, patience, obsession.
- Public Books, John Plotz, "The Story's Where I Go" (2015): "the leaving-out is half the art"; invented worlds must describe "more than a
  realist does"; Tolkien: "You know where you are, you know what the weather is, from what direction the wind's blowing. He tells you what he
  can about it—but the rest of it's up to you"; "Names come first with me"; Earthsea map + island names before writing; present tense =
  flashlight vs past tense = sunlight; derivative "orcs against elves" fantasy is "childish."
- The Millions, Paul Morton (Jan 2013): "So much of fantasy and science fiction is just the art of suggestion. You don't really tell people
  that much, but they think you have because they imagine it"; "you do as little as possible... my writing has tended to be shorter and more
  allusive"; Lathe of Heaven "They talk too much. They explain things too much"; "I love the sound of language. I play with word sounds in my
  head"; Hemingway's Spanishfied English "affected and foolish"; shifgrethor needed only "a phoneme pool"; oral childhood culture.
- Interview magazine, Choire Sicha (2015): "Writing an English sentence takes a good deal of craft and skill. Writing a good English sentence
  takes a lot more of it"; hedging qualifiers ("sort of") are "just a noise"; "I would like to say it plain"; "Writing is a slow art"; readiness
  "listening for the incoming story."
- Electric Literature, with Michael Cunningham (2016): "I only follow rules that take me where I want to go"; "the imagination can only take
  apart reality and recombine it"; genre systematized becomes formula.
- LARB, David Streitfeld, "Writing Nameless Things" (Nov 2017): "you can mash a lot of orcs and unicorns and intergalactic wars together
  without actually imagining anything"; "I am into content. Presentation is something that just has to be there"; "I like to hide it in fiction."
- Lightspeed / Geek's Guide (2012): singular they; "you have to know them before you can break them" (grammar rules); "I don't write tracts."
- School Library Journal, Francisca Goldsmith (June 2004, PDF read): wrote Wizard "in five or six weeks"; Dunsany at 11–12 "whoosh!"; poetry
  wrestled, some stories "come as if they've been dictated"; family reading aloud until the kids were 11–12; "The words that I work in are the
  words of the story... If my story seems to begin preaching, I make it stop."
- NEA Big Read Reader's Guide interview (Dan Stone, 2008; local PDF text): "What a wizard does is like what a writer does. He or she is making
  things out of words and making things happen with words"; "Ged and I learned how to face his enemy as I wrote the book."
- Writer's Digest / Salon, Faith L. Justice (2001): "Authors are writing artists... words are mine"; conflict is only one kind of behavior
  ("relating, finding, losing, bearing..."); Woolf "incredibly fruitful"; "When I'm writing, nothing affects me, I'm just trying to do the story."
- Lit Hub, Euan Monaghan (2016): "Like Joan of Arc, I'm hearing voices!"; taking dictation; avoids datable contemporary markers.
- Den of Geek (2015): naming magic from Frazer; "I'm a writer who makes things out of words"; reading "gets into their bones."
- Fantasymundo (2008, Spanish): "my reason works through imagination"; nothing on prose.
- Room Magazine (2009): male viewpoint kept her "at one remove"; poetry group brought her "up to the mark technically."
- Vice (2008), Author Hour (2009), Swarthmore/White (1994), Salon: little or nothing on prose mechanics (White: "We can't restructure our
  society without restructuring the English language"; war-metaphor avoidance).

## I. Talks / essays quoted at second hand (own words, verified through the reader's summary)
- "Rhythmic Pattern in The Lord of the Rings" (Meditations on Middle-earth 2001; The Wave in the Mind 2004) — via Kate Nepveu's summary and
  Vermont Softworks: two-beat "Stress, release. Inbreath, outbreath. A heartbeat"; "we walk from the Shire to the Mountain of Doom... One,
  two, left, right, on foot"; polarities darkness/daylight, confusion/clarity, trap/freedom; reversals "not simple binary flips"; final
  chapter's reversals "like drumbeats"; "repetition, semirepetition, suggestion, foreshadowing, recollection, echo, and reversal";
  "Even when the sentences are long, their flow is perfectly clear, and follows the breath; punctuation comes just where you need to pause";
  "Tolkien must have heard what he wrote."
- Bryn Mawr commencement 1986 (Serendip full text): father tongue = "distancing—making a gap, a space, between the subject or self and the
  object or other," "Spoken from above. It goes one way"; mother tongue "expects an answer," "binding, not... distancing but... uniting,"
  "always on the verge of silence and often on the verge of song."
- George Saunders Story Club (reader's PS quoting The Wave in the Mind): "Every book, even every short piece, has its own rhythm, and if
  you hang onto that rhythm while writing it, you can't go wrong"; "The rhythms are so subtle and have to be irregular."
- Stan Carey blog: repeats Elfland quotes + "Dreams Must Explain Themselves": her tools were the Shorter Oxford, Fowler's and Follett's.
- Nicole Bianchi / Michelle Richmond (Medium) / Asia Lenae — Steering the Craft quotes: "Adjectives and adverbs are rich and good and
  nourishing…They cause obesity in prose only when used lazily or overused"; "they ran quickly = they raced"; "suddenly" and "somehow"
  meaningless through overuse; "Prose consisting entirely of short, syntactically simple sentences is monotonous, choppy, irritating... the
  thump-thump beat gives it a false simplicity that soon just sounds stupid"; the "Expository Lump."
