 AI and Short Fiction #1 – Some recommendations for readers 

 By Neil Clarke 

 On 07/14/2026 

 In generative ai 

I’ve been working on a series of posts that have to do with issues related to “AI” and short fiction (particularly in light of some events of the last month) that I plan to post as I finish them. I’m a slow writer, so this could take some time. First up, one aimed at the general public. This is the easy one. 

So something you read feels like it was written with “AI”… What should you do to be helpful? 

 YOU DON’T HAVE TO DO ANYTHING. But if you want to let them know, contact them directly by email, letter, DMs, or any other method that isn’t public (perhaps not letter tied to a rock and thrown through their window). Contact information is often on their website. Tell them why you think it was generated. 

 DON’T POST ABOUT IT ON SOCIAL MEDIA. Social media is the opposite of helpful. Consider for a second that you might actually be wrong and could be doing reputational damage to an innocent person. Instead of being able to focus on identifying the truth of what happened, the publisher will also have to deal with the pressure of managing an upset and impatient online community. The added attention can get in the way of a fair investigation, place some parties on the defensive prematurely, or even cause harm. 

 BE PATIENT. A proper investigation will take time to gather and analyze materials, talk to all parties involved, come to a conclusion, and take action (if necessary). Some might even want to seek legal counsel before acting or making a public statement. Also keep in mind that the parties involved may be living in disparate time zones, dealing with illnesses, or using different languages. This will slow communication. 

 ACCEPT THAT YOU MIGHT NOT BE TOLD THE FINDINGS. If the work is pulled (assuming it can be), it doesn’t necessarily mean that the accused has admitted wrongdoing or been judged to have used “AI.” It’s entirely possible for these situations to end with the accused withdrawing their work to avoid the sideshow that often erupts around these events or just out of the sheer embarrassment of being accused. Sometimes people act to preserve their own mental health rather than be part of a spectacle. If the publisher doesn’t want to disclose their conclusions, that’s entirely fair. The legal standard for proof and the standard for what is good enough for the editor to take action can very different. There could be potential legal consequences for calling someone out (something you could be at risk of too, if you go public) or the publisher may simply be following their own confidentiality policies. 

 ACCEPT THAT YOU MIGHT BE WRONG. It happens. Don’t try to force that you’re right, even if you think they came to the wrong conclusion. You’ve done your part. Disagree and move on or move away. Life’s too short. 

 And in some rare cases… 

 YOU MAY DISCOVER THEY DO NOT CARE. There are a handful of publishers that are ok with generated slop. Horrifying, I know. At the bare minimum, they should be transparent about what they are doing and own it, but that’s not always the case. I won’t advise you on what to do next.
