## Features

### I. Who speaks

**1. The narrator is a historian of that world, speaking as an inhabitant, at a distance, from what became known.**
Sources (10): Rettino #22 #23 #24 #28 #186 #187 #216 #217; Töyrylä #54 #55 #56 #196 #199 #200 #473; Pugar #193 #194; Hatch #90 #201 #202; Harrison #136; Davison #256 #262; Wolfe #310; Capossere #320; Olley #523; Kay #287 #290 #358 #192.
Wording: "reads like an entry in a chronicle" (#22); "voice at times resembles that of a historian" (#186); "The narrator presents himself as one Kitan historian among many." (#187); "reporting what the world seems like to an average person" (#56); "the narrator describes things like an inhabitant of that world would" (#199); "reading a historical text with geographical references" (#193); "at the mercy of those who chronicled them" (#358).
Rule: the archivist is a native clerk of the settlement's own world, never a visitor from ours. An entry records what became known ("word went forth"; "according to most reports later", #55), names places as a local would without saying where they are (#193), and adopts the ward's common knowledge ("as everyone knows", #56 #200) as its own frame.
Disagreement: Davison reads the same register as "a dry slice of history" with "a certain feeling of detachment" (#262 #260) and Rettino calls the historian's voice "a breach" in immersion (#24). The detachment is the cost of the register; the dossier accepts it because a dossier is a record, not a scene.

**2. Report-style summary: short sentences, concrete description, minimal commentary, approximate counts.**
Sources (5): Töyrylä #53 #54 #55 #196 #198 #473; Randall #5 #171 #172; Davison #257; McBean #240; Wolfe #309.
Wording: "Sentences are short, description is concrete." (#5 #171); "The tone, moreover, is matter-of-fact." (#172); "matter-of-fact description with only minimal commentary" (#54); "merely reporting what happened, without any commentary" (#55 #196); "The approximate number of the dead" (#473); "the on-the-ground tale is a model of clarity and focus" (#309).
Rule: violence, death and loss enter the dossier as summary. A casualty count is approximate and written as words ("some two score", after the novel's "some thirty or forty", #55). The ceiling of comment is one plain evaluative word; Töyrylä finds the narrator's whole ideological trace in the word "unfortunately" (#54). Stepping out of a person's view is what permits the matter-of-fact record of that person's execution (#53 #198): the dossier records the hanging of the innkeeper from the square, not from inside him.
Disagreement: Allbery finds that "if something is important, Kay tells you, explicitly and sometimes repetitively" (#248), Michal names "long tangents describing the vast import of some historical moment" (#231), and a forum reader counts "portentous thoughts for five and a half pages" (#273). Both modes exist in the corpus; the archivist takes the plain one and leaves the portentous aside to the chronicle line, rationed (feature 30).

**3. Veiled omniscience: the teller knows everything, draws and lifts veils, and comments apart from any character's mind; distance zooms.**
Sources (8): Walton #137 #139 #140; Allbery #246 #247 #249 #250; Falden #252 #253; Rettino #25 #26 #188; Töyrylä #48 #198; McBean #245; Kay #277 #359; Elitist Book Reviews #702.
Wording: "distanced, elegaic style that I want to call veiled omniscient" (#137, Walton's spelling); "there’s always a pulling back" (#139); "the master of a specific type of omniscient tight third person" (#246); "The narrative commentary functions like a soundtrack in a movie" (#249); "Kay changes narrative distance incredibly well, zooming in and out" (#253); "a story being told to you by someone" (#277).
Rule: an entry may stand back from one doorstep to the whole ward and return in a single block, but the change of distance is carried by a change of noun scale (from the cooper's yard to the river quarter), never by an adverb. Exposition is slipped in as what a resident would notice (#25 #26 #188): the dossier tells what is unusual by naming what a local finds unusual.

**4. Many windows: every office and rank gets a focalized moment, the antagonist and the innkeeper included, and no window is thrown away.**
Sources (13): Randall #0 #1 #3 #175 #176 #177 #178; Kleander #12 #203; Töyrylä #47 #50 #53 #477; Malosse #81; Fischer Guy #115; Wolfe #306; Kay via Urie #117 and via Wilt #337 and #558; Walton #140 #142; Allbery #250; Falden #252; Hatch #91.
Wording: "employs twenty-six different agents of focalization" (#1 #175); "shifts focalization twenty-one times" (#0 #176); "more than 150 separate focalizations" (#203); "an undercook for the Blue faction making soup" (#142); "he never takes up a character just to throw them away" (#140); "Using the antagonist as a focal character makes him" less distant (#50); "a world full of other stories" (#477).
Rule: a dossier has no focalizer, so the rule lands on standpoint: each civic block is written from the standing of the people who use it (the stable from the driver, the stand from the crowd, the kitchen from the undercook), and the tyrant's hall and the tavern both get a window. A window once opened is not abandoned: a name given in one block is met again in another.
Disagreement: Dusmann finds that "in-depth examination of so many characters slows the tale down" (#164); Walton hates the Dunnett trick of describing "but doesn’t say who is who" (#138); Davison finds "Characters often sounded the same" (#258). The dossier answers the first with a block ceiling, the second by naming on first sight, the third with feature 15.

**5. One telling among many: the record is a retrospective construction, can be rewritten, and competing versions are kept side by side.**
Sources (8): Cobb #184; Olley #523; Hatch #89 #90 #202; Rettino #34 #187 #217; Harrison #136; Töyrylä #51; Malosse #210 #85; Kay #349 #278 #426 #428 #429 #441 #358; Allbery #250.
Wording: "the past cannot be changed, but history" can (#184); "The nature of history is retrospective construction" (#523); "writers of history often seek the dramatic over the truth" (#90 #202); "the earliest version of a legend isn’t always the most important" (#349); "we remember the same moments differently" (#426); "some of the events are narrated twice or even thrice" (#51); "Only a patient historian with access to records" would find the link (#136).
Rule: when two accounts of one event survive, the dossier gives both and adjudicates neither ("the guild's book records the fire in the malt-house; the ward's memory holds it in the chandlery"). Kay's proofreader flagged every deliberately varied repetition as an error, and Kay kept "the change! The difference." (#429); the archivist's second version differs in a detail, never in the claim.

### II. What is said and what is withheld

**6. Withholding and inference: hints that read only in retrospect, identities and outcomes deferred, the unsaid vital, the reader trusted.**
Sources (14): Randall #3 #4 #177 #178; Kleander #12 #17 #18; Rettino #32; Töyrylä #52 #60; Hood #153; Falden #255; Capossere #323; Pembroke College reporting Kay #301 #302; Olley #520; Moscrop #333; Cawsey #93; Walton #138; Kay #339 #298 #284 #285 #408 #447.
Wording: "the shifts delay the unfolding of the story" (#3 #178); "the hints only make sense in retrospect" (#17); "readers are not directly told who the characters are" (#60); "obstructed by a veil of sunlight" (#153); "the vital importance of the unsaid and the unexplained" (#301); "he largely leaves them to be supplied by the imagination" (#520); "even for the mysteries it refuses to explain" (#333); "I trust my readers" (#339).
Rule: an entry may name a consequence and withhold its cause; a later block supplies it, or nothing does. What every inhabitant knows is not written. Once an outcome is inevitable it is not narrated (#520). The explanation of anything uncanny is never given (#93 #333).
Disagreement: Walton, of the withheld-identity trick, "does anybody like this?" (#138); Kay wants readers who know nothing of the history to never "feel they’re behind the curve" (#236). Resolution: withhold the answer, never the question; a name is given on first sight, a motive may wait.

**7. Prolepsis and the flash-forward: outcomes told early, small "would remember" asides, glimpses of future lives, delivered with detachment.**
Sources (10): Hood #152; Milicia #600 #601; Speculiction #485 #486; Capossere #316 #495; Wolfe #310; McBean #245; Allbery #247; Walton #137; Rettino #27; Kay #402 #517 via chokipokilo.
Wording: "Kay relies heavily on prolepsis" (#152); "elaborate foreshadowing with a sort of detached amusement" (#600); "This, in turn, might have occasioned a significant change" (#601); "brief glimpses into their future lives" (#310); "a simple, quick in and out flash forward" (#495); "there are numerous throwaway lines to the effect" (#485).
Rule: the chronicle line may carry one proleptic clause per entry ("a night the mill-hands speak of for a generation"); the dossier's present tense forbids prolepsis outright; the Herald never looks forward. Kay refuses to say what happens to anyone after the close (#402): the flash-forward is a glimpse, not a fate.
Disagreement: Speculiction is relieved that the throwaway lines "all but dry up" once the story reaches the city (#486); a forum reader found the commentary and foreshadowing heavy-handed (#271, partial, quotation only). Ration accordingly.

**8. Omission as record: most tales are untold, many events are not known, an erased name is recorded as erased.**
Sources (6): Capossere #318 #319; Rettino #30 #217; Kay #352 #353; Cobb #184; Töyrylä #473; Hromic via Wikipedia #165.
Wording: "Some tales are told, most are not" (#318); "by revealing that many events in history are not known" (#217); "not only killed, but made to never have been" (#352); "glimpsing a few shining threads in a larger tapestry" (#165); "living through the drama and passion of his own life" (#30, the courier whose letter is lost).
Rule: the dossier states what the record does not hold ("the founder's name is not kept"; "the charter's second leaf is lost"), and a lost document is entered as lost, with the one thing known about its carrier.

**9. No lecture, no allegory, no map: interactions not ideologies, meaning left to the reader.**
Sources (6): Kay #299 #384 #422 #439 #119 #378 #629 #125 #298 #385 #284 #379 #97; Töyrylä #54; Cawsey #97; Kleander #21; Trudel reporting Kay #629; Fischer Guy #115 (Kay declining the reading offered).
Wording: "I’m offering a story not giving a lecture" (#299 #384); "I don't write any 'one to one' allegories" (#422); "I wanted to explore interactions not ideologies" (#378); "where a character maps onto a real person" is refused (#439 #119); "letting readers decide what they find in or take away" (#298 #385); "each reader makes the book they read" (#284 #379); "denying readers" a guiding moral voice (#54).
Rule: the dossier draws no parallel to the player's world; a faith is entered by its rite and its feast, not its doctrine; no moral is stated and no lesson is offered. This is mostly Kay's stated intention; Töyrylä (#54) and Kleander (#21, readers left "torn between different views") are the two observers who confirm the effect on the page.

### III. Scale and subject

**10. Great events at the personal level: the margins of history, the not-powerful, the household bill.**
Sources (12): Kay #94 #110 #118 #123 #191 #205 #206 #295 #296 #371 #390 #396 #421 #442 #361; Cobb #46; Hatch #91; Capossere #157 #498; Fram #334 #335; Mather #263; Walton #142; Alexander via Wikipedia #160; Dirda via Wikipedia #162; Töyrylä #477; Wilt reporting Kay #338 #341.
Wording: "The death of the emperor far off means far less" than the hired hand's broken leg (#94); "in the lives of not-powerful people" (#110 #191); "the people at the margins of history" (#123 #205); "A master of telling small stories in a big world" (#334); "scaling down epic events so that history becomes humanized" (#157); "the tavern poets, the bartenders, the guardsmen" (#498); "wanted security, wanted a roof, wanted a harvest" (#341); "see their homes fade from the map" (#335).
Rule: every civic event lands on a named household or trade: the levy is the cooper's second son, the tariff is the fishwife's price, the war is the road the carter cannot use. The dossier prefers the broken leg to the distant death.

**11. Peripheral figures carry the setting; a few lines of dignity at a death; two paragraphs are enough for a life.**
Sources (11): Kay #396 #363; Capossere #322 #325; Rettino #30; Wolfe #310; Alexander #160; Dirda #162; Töyrylä #53 #477; Walton #140; Wilt reporting Kay #338; Dusmann #164 (dissent).
Wording: "through the peripheral figures I can achieve my strongest effects" (#396); "grow in importance because I like their voice" (#363); "a few lines of gentle dignity" (#322); "are given just two or three paragraphs" (#325); "depth and tenderness and intelligence" (#160); "appealing minor characters" (#162); a whole chapter "from the viewpoint of a grizzled medieval courier" (#338).
Rule: Herald pools and dossier blocks give the gate-warden and the under-cook two or three sentences of their own life; a death entry closes with one line of consideration of the life ended (#322), never with the cause of death alone.
Disagreement: Dusmann, "so many characters slows the tale down" (#164). A per-block ceiling, not fewer people, is the answer.

**12. Consequence is faced: triumphs provisional, victories costly, the attractive killed, endings weighted with sorrow.**
Sources (9): Gunn #77 #79 #182; Rettino #31; McBean #244; Fram #335; Cobb #40 #41; Tarantino #552; Kay #623; Kleander #18; newtothegarden #549.
Wording: "facing-up to the consequences of actions glossed over" (#77); "his willingness to kill off major characters" (#79); "a happy ending, though one weighted with sorrow" (#31); "Triumphs are provisional. Victories are costly." (#244); "This memory is both a wound and a rod of support" (#41); "compassion that is deeper because of the terribleness" (#549).
Rule: no civic victory without its bill in the same block: the aqueduct and the drowned crew, the charter and the hanged rioters, the harvest and the tithe.

**13. The elegiac register: the melancholy of farewells, things fading into history, the long view of time passing.**
Sources (9): Tarantino #170 #552 #553; Rettino #35 #36; Dirda via Bright Weavings #569; Walton #137; Capossere #320; Kay #623 #291 #340; McBean #244 #245; Olley #528.
Wording: "suffused with an elegiac tone and the gentle melancholy of farewells" (#552); "Everything quietly, ineluctably fades into history" (#569); "an aching sense of time’s passage" (#320); "not even the dead can grieve forever" (#35); "shaping a long view, a sense for the reader of time" (#291); "They are meditative rather than bombastic." (#553).
Rule: the chronicle line is allowed one valedictory clause per entry; the dossier carries elegy only as fact (the well is dry, the guild is dissolved, the bell is not rung), never as a sigh.

**14. Cross-section of a society: leaders and common people in one frame, many classes.**
Sources (4): Kay #361 #206; Hatch #91; Walton #142; Capossere #498.
Wording: "cross-sections of a culture or society" (#361); "focused on both the affairs of leaders" and the common man (#91); "the chronicles of the dukes and the popes" loved alongside the unwritten lives (#206).
Rule: each dossier holds the reeve and the rag-picker in adjacent blocks, and the Herald's pools draw from both.

### IV. The world on the page

**15. Register modulation: plain for death and fact, elevated for the mythic set piece, and the elevation follows who perceives.**
Sources (10): Randall #5 #6 #173 #174; Campbell #214; Whitehead #312 #313; Harrison #131 #134; Kay #289 #610 #103 #623 #615; Bennett via RPGnet #275; Ruffolo #328; Webb #66 #185; Wolfe #306 #307.
Wording: "an obvious attempt at rhetorical “high style.”" (#6 #173); "perceived by minds capable of comprehending magnificence" (#174); "his measured prose, sometimes minimalist, sometimes ornate" (#312); "when to deploy words like bludgeons and when like scalpels" (#313); "harder, terser, less lyric than the books before" (#610); "the elevated register is characteristic of Kay’s work" (#134); "further mythic gravitas to this section" (#214).
Rule: the dossier holds one register, the plain one; the Herald may lift for a founding or a festival; the chronicle line may lift for a death of state. When it lifts, the lift is a matter of who perceives (the priest's procession is high, the sweeper's the same procession is plain), so a raised register is always attached to a named standpoint.

**16. Diction: formal, precise, lyrical without being flowery; concrete nouns that bite; research digested to noun choice, never dumped.**
Sources (12): Walton #144; McBean #240; Fantasy-Faction #708; Walschots #659; Gunn #78 #180; de Lint via Wikipedia #161; Wilt reporting Kay #341; Kleander quoting Kay #16 #204 and Kay #357; Kay #395 #365; Palmatier #151; Das #572; Halasz #627.
Wording: "The details are all real enough to bite" (#144: tesserae, mud, fish sauces, the arrow tool); "does not bury the reader in invented terminology" (#240); "His writing is lyrical without being flowery." (#708); "The language in River of Stars is formal and poetic" (#659); "the quiet, steady accumulation of detail and complexity" (#16 #204 #357); "great gobbets of raw fact are tucked into the book" is the failure (#365); "not a word of this novel feels gratuitous" (#572); "the punctilious choice of word and image" (#627).
Rule: concrete civic nouns (tithe-barn, salt-pan, bailey, chandlery); one biting detail per block; research shows only as the exactness of a noun, never as an explanation of it.
Disagreement: "superlative-rich style risks beauty fatigue" (#129, partial, quotation only); writing "that sometimes gets carried away with itself" (#159); "many other readers find it to be annoying" (#556); "he just goes on rather more than he needs to" (#272). The dossier keeps the nouns and drops the superlatives.

**17. Metaphor discipline: the world mediated through one trade's craft; fantasy functional; the tangled metaphor cut.**
Sources (6): Walton #143; Kay #687 #620 #350; Oden #268; Tarantino #553; Barbour #666; Palmatier #224.
Wording: "mediates the world through the chariot races" and mosaics (#143); "the sentence simply read better after deleting the word ‘taste’" (#687); "you don’t blend colours – you contrast them" (#620); "I tried to imagine myself with a stiletto not a bludgeon" (#350); "Kay’s fantasy is functional" (#268).
Rule: each settlement gets one mediating craft (the tannery, the loom, the salt-pan) through which its politics and faith are described; a metaphor that needs a second clause to work is deleted; adjacent blocks contrast rather than blend.

**18. Names carry history: transparent renaming, punning names, a single cosmetic signal that the world is not ours; invented species names resented.**
Sources (11): Kay #353 #354 #109 #416 #458 #105 #355; Webb #64; Malosse #84; Cobb #43; Wolfe #308; Palmatier #223 #491; Nepveu #484; Gunn #78; lepusdomesticus #502 #503 #504; Falden #254.
Wording: "Names link to history" (#353); "a deliberate pun on ruin" (#64); "under the new names – often transparent" (#84); "The two moons say, We’re not here" (#109 #416 #458); "the flavor of Italy is there in the word structure" (#223); "instead of nightingales and partridges" (#502); "And frankly, some of his names are poor" (#254); "these are mere cosmetic changes" (#491).
Rule: a place or office name in the dossier carries a history that another block can open (a ward named for a drowned reeve); one cosmetic signal per world is enough and it is never explained; a nightingale is never renamed.
Disagreement: Palmatier locates the craft in altered everyday ritual, not in two moons (#491 #225); Kay and Gunn treat the two moons as a deliberate signal (#109 #78). Both hold: signal once, then build the difference in ritual.

**19. No dates and no figures: readers never learn the year; the minutiae of dates and places withheld; counts approximate.**
Sources (3, two of them Kay): Kay #288 #375 (near-history essay); Kay #292 #386 (author questions); Töyrylä #55 #473. Flag: thin, and mostly intention; the one observed instance is Töyrylä's Senzio courtyard.
Wording: "Readers will never learn the year of the Treaty of Utrecht" (#288 #375); "the minutiae of dates and places" are not the point (#292 #386); "The approximate number of the dead" (#473).
Rule: no digits anywhere in the dossier; counts as words and approximations ("some forty", "a score", "half the ward"); years as reigns, floods or festivals ("in the year of the second flood"), never as numerals.

**20. Telescoping and the palimpsest: centuries collapsed into a lifetime, single days heavy with what stood before, a series of situations with gaps.**
Sources (9): Kleander #15; Kay #163 #286 #291 #356 #364 #394 #340 #238; Ordway #73; Ringel #167 #168; Walton #141; Capossere #497; Salon Futura #710; Bethune via Ringel #168.
Wording: "I telescoped the Reconquista in Lions" (#286); "I reserve the right to change, or telescope events" (#163 #356); "a palimpsest of time and space" (#167); "often collapsed into a single era" (#168); "still carry the hard imprint of what has gone before" (#394); "not a seamless narrative but more a series of situations" (#497); "a series of vignettes" (#710); "more use of the pluperfect tense in these than anything else" (#141).
Rule: the chronicle line is a series of situations with gaps between them, and it is allowed to compress; the dossier records the palimpsest as standing fact (the older wall beneath the new, the field that was a fort, the shrine that was a well).

**21. The world as its inhabitants believe it: period conceits and superstitions as fact, no modern smugness, no modern emoting.**
Sources (6): Kay #456 #457 #671 #460; Malosse #82; Cawsey #93; Töyrylä #56 #199 #200; Wilt reporting Kay #341; Oden #266 #267.
Wording: "make the world be as my characters believe it to be" (#456); "it removes that smugness from the reader" (#457); "the knowledge and the conceits of the time" (#82); "feels like a betrayal (even a large one) of the characters" (#671, of making a controlled society emote as we do); "it’s also, for me, staggeringly alien" (#460); "you believe in the politics, the cultural tensions, the way power" flows (#267).
Rule: the archivist reports the ward's belief as the ward holds it ("the well is known to be lucky"; "the east gate is not crossed at dusk") with no wink; superstition is a civic fact; feeling is recorded in the forms the culture permits, not in ours.

**22. Magic small, unexplained, internalised: no talismans, a throwaway line as the first signal, a trajectory away from magic.**
Sources (12): Webb #61; Walton #146 #149 #481; Ringel #166 #545; Wolfe and Kirkus via Wikipedia #156; Wilt #342; Barbour #666; Harrison #130; Cawsey reporting Kay #93; Oden #268; Allard #542; Nepveu #484.
Wording: "the text contains no talismanic objects" (#61); "like the silver threads in shot silk" (#146); "There are no elaborate, concrete magic systems here" (#342); "magic but little mystery" (#130); "Confined to small things, the coinage of hearth and heart" (#545); "leaves the exact details of the workings of magic unexplained" (#93); "a throw-away line in the prologue is the first signal" (#484).
Rule: mostly DM-page doctrine, but one rule binds the dossier: the uncanny appears as a single throwaway civic line (the sandal-maker who saw a ghost) and is never explained or systematised.

### V. The sentence

**23. Sentence shape: the line of nineteenth-century romance, layered, sometimes convoluted, sometimes archaic; musical, rarely flashy; rhythm and precision.**
Sources (12): Webb #66; Hoyle #589 #590; Walschots #659 #660; McBean #241 #242; Hobbs via Wikipedia #155; Oden #269; Fischer Guy #114 #461; Halasz #627; Snyder #563; Patton #213; Marcus #616; Kay #615.
Wording: "the sentence structure, the ambience, the style of nineteenth century romance" (#66); "sentences are often convoluted, meanings often obscured" (#589); "flavoured with an archaism that prompts a second glance" (#590); "the sentences laid out like layers of diaphanous fabric" (#660); "The beauty of the prose comes from rhythm and precision" (#241); "Kay’s sentences are musical, but they are rarely flashy" (#242); "the almost opulence of your language" (#616); "A reverence for language runs through everything he writes." (#461).
Disagreement, explicit: McBean (rhythm and precision, rarely flashy) against Hoyle (convoluted, obscured) against Hobbs ("bothersome sentence structures" like Tolkien's, #155) against Marcus (opulence). The corpus contains all four readings of the same sentences.
Rule: a dossier sentence is periodic but not tangled: one main clause and at most one subordinate; the archaism lives in the noun (the moot, the tithe, the bailey), never in the verb or the word order.

**24. The plain register's tools: short sentences, sentence fragments, the polysyndetic chain, the refrain, rapid speech under stress.**
Sources (8): Randall #5 #171; Töyrylä #49; Michal #228; Kay #558 #683 #303 #305; Capossere #324; Wolfe #311; Whitehead #312.
Wording: "the long list of verbs" ("battered and levelled and burned", #49); "rife with sentence fragments and strong images" (#228); "No sentence fragments. One narrator only." as rules broken (#558); "Lives change, turn, pivot on small things, accidents of timing" repeated (#311); "You could die so easily" repeated (#324); "She is lying beside her love dreaming of her love." (#303 #305); "certain characters speaking rapidly at times, under stress" with no semicolons (#683).
Rule: a refrain may recur across dossier blocks (the same clause about the river under Water and under Trade), and the polysyndetic chain is the archivist's one permitted figure, reserved for loss. Fragments belong to the Herald's pools, not to the dossier.

**25. Tense and person: past by default, present tense for chosen standpoints, the pluperfect for what stood before, first person rare and distinct.**
Sources (7): Harrison #135; Stern #656 #657 #658; Wilt reporting Kay #336; Walton #141; Wolfe #307; Elitist Book Reviews #702; Capossere #320.
Wording: the women's sections "are in present tense" (#135); "Most sections are written in the past tense, except for those" following Shan (#657); "his choice of tense, depending on which character" (#656); "his narration shifts to present tense for the scene" (#658); the rule "Don’t jump from the past tense to the present tense" is one Kay does not keep (#336); "the occasional shift into first-person" as a soloist (#307).
Rule: the dossier is present tense throughout, because it is a standing record; the chronicle line is past, with the pluperfect for what stood before; first person appears nowhere except inside a quoted deposition, and then it is set apart.

**26. Punctuation as authorial craft: own rules, italics cut in proof, the colon before a list, punctuation by speaker and rhythm.**
Sources (2, an author cluster): Kay #680 #681 #683 #684 #686 #691 #679 #381; his production editor Sandra Tooze #676 #678. Flag: effectively single-voice; no outside critic attests it.
Wording: "he forges his own rules when it comes to punctuation" (#676); "I’ll use punctuation varaibly depending on context, speaker, rhythms." (#680, sic); "italics look LOUDER in galleys than they do on my computer" (#684); "a colon better sets off the ‘list’ that follows" (#686); "LAST LIGHT is punctuated differently than the MOSAIC was." (#681).
Rule: no italics in the dossier; a colon introduces a list; the semicolon is rationed. The house rule against the em dash is ours, not Kay's: his own pages use dashes freely (#84 #620 #710), so the rule is a settlement-house convention layered over the model, and should be recorded as such.

**27. Dialogue: restraint, implication, subtext, emotion in what is not said; formal; quick under stress.**
Sources (5): McBean #243 #450; Kay #683 #671; Davison #258 #259; taoofjord via SFFWorld #515; Hood #154.
Wording: "Emotion is often conveyed by what is not said." (#450); "People speak with restraint, implication, and subtext" (#243); "Characters often sounded the same" (#258); "little more than a summary of history between speech marks" (#259); "The book is almost written like a play." (#515).
Disagreement, explicit: McBean's restraint against Davison's sameness and history-in-speech-marks, and taoofjord's "almost all long conversation".
Rule: in the Herald's pools quoted speech never explains history; a saying is under ten words; a speaker's feeling is shown by what the speaker leaves out; two speakers are told apart by what each refuses to say.

**28. Emotion by subtlety: momentum from a look or a stance, power in quiet moments, a single line that moves because the reader knows why.**
Sources (8): Hood #154; Olley #526; Kay #293 #350 #629; McBean #450; Mather #264; Kleander #21; Davison #257; Randall #7.
Wording: "The novel’s momentum is driven by subtleties" (#154); "ability to find power in these quiet moments" (#526); "readers can be moved by a single line" (#293); "hammered on the head with the author’s didactic or pedantic point" is what Kay hates (#629); "much more subtle and quietly spoken" (#264).
Disagreement, explicit: Randall reads the Darien plot as "a rhetoric of the sentimental" (#7), Oden reports critics calling Kay "too sentimental, too romantic" and half concedes (#270), Michal finds readers "told how to feel" (#230, partial, quotation only), while Davison finds the prose "cold and clinical, even lacking in emotion" (#257). The register is read as both too warm and too cold.
Rule: the dossier is never told how to feel; the Herald pool may hold one line meant to move, and it is earned by a fact stated in the preceding sentence.

### VI. Structure, openings, closings

**29. Structure: an architect not a gardener; episodes laid next to each other like stones; strands plaited; chronology rearranged; cause and effect from adjacency.**
Sources (7): Kay #279 #620 #696 #127; Webb #62 #63; Patton #212; Milicia #604; Hatch #89; Capossere #326 #497; Walton #141.
Wording: "the shape of a novel matters a lot to me" (#279); "Stories, he reminds us, are told, but novels are shaped." (#696); "narratival episodes were laid next to each other" in lapidary fashion (#212); "creates a sense of cause and effect in history" rather than a timeline (#89); "an intricately rearranged chronology" (#604); "meticulously crafted in structure, pace, language, dialogue" (#326).
Rule: dossier blocks are laid side by side without transitions; neighbouring blocks contrast in scale and rank (the palace and the soup kitchen); cause and effect emerge from adjacency, and the word "because" is nearly absent.

**30. Narratorial aphorism and the portentous aside: the generalising sentence on pattern, coincidence or mortality, used and resented.**
Sources (9): Rettino #34 #29; Harrison #136; Hatch #90 #202; Capossere #318 #324; Wolfe #311; Allbery #248 #249; Michal #231; Uqbarian via RPGnet #273; Milicia #600.
Wording: "questions the integrity of historical narratives" (#34, the narrator on pattern in flux); "Only a patient historian with access to records" (#136); "Some tales are told, most are not" (#318); "Repeatedly characters (or the narrator) reflect on how" easily one could die (#324); "The exclamation point is telling" (#29); "if something is important, Kay tells you, explicitly and sometimes" repetitively (#248).
Disagreement, explicit: Capossere and Wolfe count the aphorisms as craft; Allbery, Michal (#231) and Uqbarian (#273) count them as heavy-handed.
Rule: the aphorism is barred from the dossier and the Herald; the chronicle line may carry at most one per entry, and it is one sentence long.

**31. Opening sentences: a chronicle entry that misleads truthfully, the aphorism on historians, a placid start seeded with later guns.**
Sources (9): Rettino #22 #23; Töyrylä #56 #58; Kay #351 #303; Hatch #90 #202; Capossere #321; Falden #255; Milicia #605; Nepveu #484; Palmatier #225.
Wording: "In the autumn season of wine, word went forth" (the novel's line, via #22); "the narrator does not claim that Sandre is dead" (#58); "start a book about subterfuge and deception with an outright lie" (#351); "The memory of home can be too far away" (#321); "things and people that seem innocuous in that placid beginning" (#255); "the long Prologue is a bit plodding" (#605).
Rule: a dossier opens with what the settlement says of itself ("word went forth that the old duke is dead"), which may be untrue but is never the archivist's lie; the first line is a record, not a scene, and it plants a name that a later block returns to.

**32. Closing sentences: everything fading into history; an unnamed figure; an object left standing; verse or a tag quotation; nothing said of what comes after; a short sentence repeated.**
Sources (9): Dirda via Bright Weavings #569; Rettino #35 #36; Olley #528; Taylor #87; Webb #65; Kay #692 #402; Moscrop #331; Ordway #75; Michal #229 (partial, quotation only).
Wording: "Everything quietly, ineluctably fades into history" (#569); "not even the dead can grieve forever" (#35, two unnamed men burying the dead); "Three full glasses of wine, intentionally left standing together" (#87); "whose presence is a prophecy in itself" (#65); "heightens the pathos of the moment" (#528, the verse lament); "saying exactly what happens to characters after a novel closes" is refused (#402); "I did that. I did that." (#331, via #332 partial).
Rule: a dossier block closes on an object left standing (three glasses, a bell not rung, a door kept shut), never on a summary; the chronicle line closes on a name not given; the Herald may close a pool entry on a three-word sentence.

**33. Teaching the reader to read: trust, no talking down, the grammar of the book taught early and never re-explained.**
Sources (2 voices: Kay across five venues #339 #124 #437 #408 #447 #357 #236 #96, and Kleander reporting Kay #16 #204). Flag: single-voice, an intention multiply attested, not an observed effect.
Wording: "I trust my readers" (#339); "every serious writer is teaching a reader how to read them" (#124 #437); "flatly refuses to talk down to her readers" (#408, of Dunnett, as model); "don’t ever feel they’re behind the curve or missing something" (#236); "a drive towards complexity" (#96).
Rule: the first three blocks of a dossier teach its grammar (what a block holds, how a name is given, how a count is written); nothing later is explained twice.

### VII. Reception and counter-reception (thresholds, not models)

**34. What readers hold against the register: sameness of archetypes, sentimentality, telling, portentous length, convolution, coldness, too much prose, a split room.**
Sources (14): Elitist Book Reviews #703 #704; Oden #270; Davison #257 #258 #262; Loutfi #556; Hobbs via Wikipedia #155; Kirkus via Wikipedia #159; Randall #7; Walton #147; Uqbarian #272 #273; Michal #231; Allbery #248; Dusmann via Wikipedia #164; Quill to Live book club #510; Hoyle #589.
Wording: "his books tend towards a certain… sameness" (#703); "the poet, the warrior, the artist, the lover, the priest" (#704); "mannered and distanced and held at arm’s length" (#147); "he just goes on rather more than he needs to" (#272); "half of the group had scores of nine or above" and half below five (#510); "I found it on the whole to be telling rather than" showing (#327, partial, quotation only).
Rule: these become walker thresholds on the generated corpus: an archetype pool wider than five roles; no aside longer than one sentence; a per-block word ceiling; no adjective for a person where an act will serve (feature 36); an emotion never named where an omission will carry it.

### VIII. Process and lineage (DM page only)

**35. Process: no outline, setting then theme then people, a scene layered ten or fifteen times, lightly edited, no rules, anecdote not prescription, a starter bibliography in every book.**
Sources (Kay across eleven venues #127 #280 #281 #625 #232 #237 #380 #282 #374 #424 #711 #283 #297 #679, with Whitehead #314 and Wolfe #696 confirming the bibliography and the shaping). Flag: single-voice by nature.
Wording: "I don’t outline the books." (#127); "sometimes ten or fifteen times for a scene" (#625); "There are no rules" (#232 #380); "this is anecdotal, not prescriptive" (#424); "be skeptical when writers present intuitive processes as thought-out planning" (#282 #374); "And I always include starter bibliographies" (#283); "a bad draft was not a death sentence to a work" (#711).
Rule (DM page): the dossier's sources block is a starter bibliography of in-world documents (the guild's book, the assize roll, the sexton's list), mirroring #283 and #314; the generation notes never claim a template Kay denies having (#237).

**36. Lineage: Dunnett's character through action, poet first, Yeats and Seferis reread, the T'ang poets, Tolkien's lyric reach, and "not an echo of anyone".**
Sources (8): Kay #104 #128 #107 #235 #615 #408; Fischer Guy #265; Webb #69; Snyder #563; Oden #269; Walton #138; Gunn quoting Kuzca #80.
Wording: "revelation of character through action" (#128); "reveal and showcase character within and through action scenes" (#104); "My first published work was poetry" (#107); "the poetry of Yeats or George Seferis" (#235); "Certainly not a linguistic attempt to echo anyone else." (#615); "the inspirational influence of the T’ang poets" (#563).
Rule: an office-holder in the dossier is revealed by a recorded act (the reeve who opened the granary in the second bad year), never by an adjective.

**37. The quarter turn: history steeped and twisted, the seam between invention and record invisible, the tale detached from a narrow context, real lives not co-opted.**
Sources (10): Kay #409 #116 #126 #285 #99 #345 #97 #113 #415 #459 #106 #373 #343 #346 #101 #411 #105; Flood reporting a critic #410; Cobb #42 #43; Rettino #38; Johnston #208; Malosse #83; Walton #145; Olley #525; Ordway #219 #220; Palmatier #225.
Wording: "history with a quarter-turn to the fantastic" (#410, a critic's phrase Kay loves); "we can’t get it exactly right" (#116); "we are all guessing" (#343); "It detaches the tale from a narrow context" (#345 #99); "Its false Byzantium is more real" (#83); "a historical fantasy is open" (#145); "you’ve botched your storytelling" if the seam shows (#113 #415 #459); "variations on historical themes, not create-from-scratch worlds" (#106).
Rule (world doctrine, binding the dossier through the SETTING-AGNOSTIC law): the dossier never names a real-world analogue, and invention and record are indistinguishable in texture; Cobb's dissent (#42, a reader can miss the recasting entirely, so the revision "uncomfortably" resembles the tyrant's spell) is the risk to log, not to cure.
