## Features

### I. Who speaks

**1. The narrator is a historian of that world, speaking as an inhabitant, at a distance, from what became known.**
Sources (13): Rettino #22 #23 #24 #28 #29 #186 #187 #216 #217; Töyrylä #54 #56 #196 #199 #200; Pugar #193 #194; Hatch #90 #201 #202; Harrison #136; Davison #256 #260 #262 #1082; Wolfe #310; Capossere #320; Olley #523; Cobb #768; Ruffolo #329; Morgan #761; Kay #287 #290 #358 #192.
Wording: "reads like an entry in a chronicle" (#22); "voice at times resembles that of a historian" (#186); "one Kitan historian among many" (#187); "reporting what the world seems like to an average person" (#56); "describes things like an inhabitant of that world would" (#199); "reading a historical text with geographical references" (#193); "a true account, giving evidence of the past" (#194); "the spectacle of the world that he records" (#768); "at the mercy of!) those who chronicled them" (#358); "the recorders, chroniclers of a time" (#290).
Rule: the archivist is a native clerk of the settlement's own world, never a visitor from ours. An entry records what became known and what is held ("word went forth"; "according to most reports later", Töyrylä's phrase at #197, partial, quotation only), names places as a local would without saying where they are (#193), and may adopt the ward's common knowledge as its own frame. Resolution (a): the frame "as everyone knows" (#56, #200) is permitted for a fact the READER lacks and the ward holds ("as everyone knows, the east gate is not crossed at dusk"); feature 6's withholding rule governs explanation, not fact, so the frame may introduce a belief but never the cause behind it. Morgan's #761 adds the archivist's one licensed doubt: a chronicler so partial that "his testimony must be suspect" may be entered as such.
Disagreement: Davison reads the same register as "a dry slice of history" with "a certain feeling of detachment" (#262, #260) and as "quite academic in its structure and descriptions" (#1082); Rettino calls the historian's voice "a breach" in immersion (#24). The detachment is the cost of the register; the dossier accepts it because a dossier is a record, not a scene.

**2. Report mode: short sentences, concrete description, minimal commentary, calm kept through violence, counts approximate and spelled out.**
Sources (8): Randall #5 #171 #172; Töyrylä #53 #54 #196 #198 (#55 #473 partial, quotation only); Das #571 #573 #572; Wolfe #309; McBean #240 #1084; Cilli #810; the Rector's Council passage of Children of Earth and Sky quoted by Capossere #641; Manguel and Guadalupi #634.
Wording: "Sentences are short, description is concrete." (#5, #171); "The tone, moreover, is matter-of-fact." (#172); "matter-of-fact description with only minimal commentary" (#54); "merely reporting what happened, without any commentary" (#196); "enables the matter-of-fact description of his execution" (#53, #198); "River of Stars is never showy" (#571); "retains a sense of contemplative calm" even in battle (#573); "not a word of this novel feels gratuitous" (#572); "the on-the-ground tale is a model of clarity and focus" (#309); "names and dates are tucked neatly in a row" is what Under Heaven refuses (#810); "The approximate number of the dead" (#473, partial, quotation only).
Rule: violence, death and loss enter the dossier in report mode, meaning the event compressed to its outcome and its bill. Resolution (c): "summary" is retired; feature 2's mode is called report, and feature 32's forbidden closing move is called the summing sentence. A count is written in words and, where a source exists, hedged ("some two score by the sexton's list"); the Rector's Council passage is the model, a spelled-out figure in the present tense with its correction in the next sentence (#641; the wording is the novel's and runs over twelve words, so it is cited to Children of Earth and Sky, the Rector's Council passage, without page on the source page). The ceiling of comment is one plain evaluative word; Töyrylä finds the narrator's whole ideological trace in the word "unfortunately" (#54). Stepping out of a person's view is what permits the plain record of that person's execution (#53, #198): the dossier records the hanging of the innkeeper from the square, not from inside him.
Disagreement: Allbery finds that "if something is important, Kay tells you, explicitly" (#248), Michal names "long tangents describing the vast import" of a moment (#231, #1080), a forum reader counts "portentous thoughts for five and a half pages" (#273), and a Goodreads reader finds a book "so melodramatic and garrulous" (#1026). Both modes exist in the corpus; the archivist takes the plain one and leaves the portentous aside to the chronicle line, rationed (feature 30).

**3. Veiled omniscience: the teller knows everything, draws and lifts veils, comments apart from any mind, and looks back with hindsight rather than forward with suspense.**
Sources (11): Walton #137 #139 #140; Allbery #246 #247 #249 #250; Falden #252 #253; Rettino #25 #26 #188; Töyrylä #48 #57; McBean #245; Olley #522; McShane #1060; Kay #277 #359; Elitist Book Reviews #702; Randall #10 (partial, quotation only).
Wording: "distanced, elegaic style that I want to call veiled omniscient" (#137, Walton's spelling); "there's always a pulling back" (#139); "the master of a specific type of omniscient tight third person" (#246); "The narrative commentary functions like a soundtrack in a movie" (#249); "zooming in and out" (#253); "to look back with the tragic clarity of hindsight" (#522); "a story being told to you by someone" (#277); "leaps from one to another, sometimes within" a scene (#1060).
Rule: an entry may stand back from one doorstep to the whole ward and return in a single block, but the change of distance is carried by a change of noun scale (from the cooper's yard to the river quarter), never by an adverb. Exposition is slipped in as what a resident would notice (#25, #26, #188): the dossier tells what is unusual by naming what a local finds unusual. Olley's clause (#522) fixes the direction of the gaze: the dossier looks back, never ahead.

**4. Many windows: every office and rank gets a standing, the antagonist and the innkeeper included, and no window is thrown away.**
Sources (18): Randall #0 #1 #3 #4 #175 #176 #177 #178; Kleander #12 #203; Töyrylä #47 #50 #52 #477; Malosse #81; Fischer Guy #115; Wolfe #306; Kay #117 #337; Walton #140 #142; Allbery #250; Falden #252; Hatch #91; Das #574; joshua-bush #1020; Mogsy #1055 (partial, quotation only); Dusmann #164; Davison #258; Cobb #765; Jake Bishop #1089.
Wording: "employs twenty-six different agents of focalization" (#1, #175); "shifts focalization twenty-one times" (#0, #176); "more than 150 separate focalizations" (#203); "an undercook for the Blue faction making soup" (#142); "he never takes up a character just to throw them away" (#140); "Using the antagonist as a focal character makes him" less distant (#50); "appear for a mere scene before fading back" (#574); "a world full of other stories" (#477); "the eyes of these more humble characters" (#1020). Randall's counts and Kleander's are critics' hand counts, not measurements of the program's.
Rule: a dossier has no focalizer, so the rule lands on standpoint: each civic block is written from the standing of the people who use it (the stable from the driver, the stand from the crowd, the kitchen from the undercook), and the tyrant's hall and the tavern both get a window. A window once opened is not abandoned: a name given in one block is met again in another.
Disagreement: Dusmann finds that "so many characters slows the tale down" (#164); Walton hates the Dunnett trick of describing "but doesn't say who is who" (#138); Davison finds "Characters often sounded the same" (#258); Cobb concedes the characters "are not sufficiently differentiated from book to book" (#765); a Goodreads reader finds late voices "sound slightly too similar" to the narrator (#1089). The dossier answers the first with a block ceiling, the second by naming on first sight, the third with feature 27.

**5. One telling among many: the record is a retrospective construction, can be rewritten, and competing versions are kept side by side.**
Sources (12): Cobb #184; Olley #523; Hatch #89 #90 #201 #202; Rettino #34 #187 #217; Harrison #136; Töyrylä #51; Malosse #210 #85; Allbery #250; Siriane #1001; Morgan #761; Mogsy #1055 (partial, quotation only); Kay #349 #278 #426 #428 #429 #441 #358 #108 #189 #190 #412 #413 #907.
Wording: "the past cannot be changed, but history" can (#184); "The nature of history is retrospective construction" (#523); "writers of history often seek the dramatic over the truth" (#90, #202); "the earliest version of a legend isn't always the" most important (#349); "we remember the same" moments differently (#426); "some of the events are narrated twice or even thrice" (#51); "the conversation isn't the same" (#190); "un intéressant effet kaléidoscopique" (#1001); "Only a patient historian with access to records" would find the link (#136).
Rule: when two accounts of one event survive, the dossier gives both and adjudicates neither ("the guild's book holds the fire in the malt-house"; "the ward's memory holds it in the chandlery"). Kay's proofreader flagged every deliberately varied repetition as an error, and Kay kept "the change! The difference." (#429); the archivist's second version differs in a detail, never in the claim.

### II. What is said and what is withheld

**6. Withholding and inference: hints that read only in retrospect, identities and outcomes deferred, the unsaid vital, the reader trusted.**
Sources (16): Randall #3 #4 #177 #178; Kleander #12 #17 #18; Rettino #32; Töyrylä #52 #60; Hood #153; Falden #255; Capossere #317 #323; Pembroke College reporting Kay #301 #302; Olley #520; Moscrop #333; Cawsey #93; Walton #138; joshua-bush #1018 #1019; Morgan #1045 #1046; Liptak transcribing Kay #1064 #1065; Kay #339 #298 #284 #285 #408 #447 #626.
Wording: "the shifts delay the unfolding of the story" (#3, #178); "the hints only make sense in retrospect" (#17); "readers are not directly told who the characters are" (#60); "obstructed by a veil of sunlight" (#153); "the vital importance of the unsaid and the unexplained" (#301); "he largely leaves them to be supplied by the imagination" (#520); "even for the mysteries it refuses to explain" (#333); "never bludgeons the reader with it, instead planting seeds" (#1018); "he shows us only what is most interesting" (#1019); "We must not let in daylight upon magic" (#1064, Bagehot's line as Kay's text); "a dialogue with the reader, and not just a monologue" (#1045); "I trust my readers" (#339).
Rule: an entry may name a consequence and withhold its cause; a later block supplies it, or nothing does. Resolution (a), restated from feature 1: what is withheld is EXPLANATION (cause, mechanism, motive), never a fact the reader lacks; "as everyone knows" may introduce the fact, and nothing introduces the why. Once an outcome is inevitable it is not narrated (#520). The explanation of anything uncanny is never given (#93, #333, #1046).
Disagreement: Walton, of the withheld-identity trick, "does anybody like this?" (#138); Kay wants readers who know nothing of the history to never "feel they're behind the curve" (#236). Resolution: withhold the answer, never the question; a name is given on first sight, a motive may wait.

**7. Prolepsis and the flash-forward: outcomes told early, small "would remember" asides, glimpses of future lives, delivered with detachment.**
Sources (12): Hood #152; Milicia #600 #601; Speculiction #485 #486; Capossere #316 #495; Wolfe #310; McBean #245; Allbery #247; Walton #137; Rettino #27; Kay #402; chokipokilo #517 (partial, quotation only, moved out of the Kay column); Uqbarian #271 (partial, quotation only).
Wording: "Kay relies heavily on prolepsis" (#152); "elaborate foreshadowing with a sort of detached amusement" (#600); "This, in turn, might have occasioned a significant change" (#601, the novel's own conditional, Sailing to Sarantium as quoted by Milicia); "brief glimpses into their future lives" (#310); "a simple, quick in and out flash forward" (#495); "there are numerous throwaway lines to the effect" (#485); "that's a device GGK uses in all his works" (#517, a forum reader, partial).
Rule: the chronicle line may carry one proleptic clause per entry ("a night the mill-hands speak of for a generation"); the dossier's present tense forbids prolepsis outright; the Herald never looks forward. Kay refuses to say what happens to anyone after the close (#402): the flash-forward is a glimpse, not a fate.
Disagreement: Speculiction is relieved that the throwaway lines "all but dry up" once the story reaches the city (#486); a forum reader found the commentary and foreshadowing heavy-handed (#271, partial, quotation only). Ration accordingly.

**8. Omission as record: the gazetteer says what is not known, who gave no account, what is disputed and what is only reported; most tales are untold; an erased name is recorded as erased.**
Sources (9): Manguel and Guadalupi, Dictionary of Imaginary Places, entry on Fionavar #638 #637 #639 #635 (#636 partial, quotation only); Capossere #318 #319; Rettino #30 #217; Kay #352 #353 #626; Cobb #184; Töyrylä #477; Hromic #165; Harrison #133 (partial, quotation only); joshua-bush #1019.
Wording, rebuilt on the rescued gazetteer first: "neither has offered an account of what they saw there" (#638, of the only two visitors to a place); "it is a matter of some dispute which race came first" (#637, left unsettled); "There have been reports of a lessening of this sentient forest's" antipathy, pending confirmation (#639); "Access to the Godwood and the tree is strictly controlled" (#635); "Fionavar's prehistory is hazy." (#636, partial: the entry then narrates that prehistory, so the quotation is a model sentence, not a model of restraint); "Some tales are told, most are not" (#318, the narrator of All the Seas of the World); "by revealing that many events in history are not known" (#217); "not only killed, but made to never have been" (#352); "glimpsing a few shining threads in a larger tapestry" (#165); "There are other things, just offstage, that the reader knows" will happen (#626).
Rule: the dossier states what the record does not hold, in the gazetteer's four forms: the unaccounted ("the two who entered the crypt have given no account"), the disputed ("which family held the mill first is disputed"), the reported ("reports say the ford is passable; carters use the bridge") and the controlled ("access to the reliquary is by the sexton's leave"). A lost document is entered as lost, with the one thing known about its carrier (#30). The gazetteer's failure at #636 is the rule's boundary: having said the prehistory is hazy, the archivist does not then tell it.

**9. No lecture, no allegory, no map: interactions not ideologies, meaning left to the reader.**
Sources (7, mostly intention): Kay #299 #384 #422 #439 #119 #378 #629 #125 #298 #385 #284 #379 #97 #720; Töyrylä #54; Kleander #21; Fischer Guy #115 (Kay declining the reading offered); Labrousse-Marchau #740; Kirkus #788; Oden #266.
Wording: "I'm offering a story not giving a lecture" (#299, #384); "I don't write any 'one to one' allegories" (#422); "I wanted to explore interactions not ideologies" (#378); "where things map directly" is refused (#439, #119); "letting readers decide what they find in or take away" (#298, #385); "each reader makes the book they read" (#284, #379); "denying readers" a guiding moral voice (#54); "The shadows are inextricably linked to the light" (#740); "versions that rhyme with the real thing" (#266).
Rule: the dossier draws no parallel to the player's world; a faith is entered by its rite and its feast, not its doctrine; no moral is stated and no lesson is offered. This is mostly Kay's stated intention (marked: intention). Töyrylä (#54), Kleander (#21, readers left "torn between different views"), Labrousse-Marchau (#740, no good-and-evil dichotomy) and Kirkus (#788, "less interested in re-creating history than in playing" the changes) are the observers who confirm the effect on the page.

### III. Scale and subject

### IV. The world on the page

**15. Register modulation: the writing changes in every book; plain for death and fact, elevated for the mythic set piece; the elevation follows who perceives; verse is a change of register.**

### V. The sentence

**23. Sentence shape: the line of nineteenth-century romance, layered, sometimes convoluted, sometimes archaic; calm kept in battle; musical, rarely flashy; rhythm and precision; one contemporary line on the first page.**

**24. The plain register's tools: short sentences, sentence fragments, the polysyndetic chain, the refrain, rapid speech under stress.**
Sources (9): Randall #5 #171; Töyrylä #49; Michal #228 #1078; Kay #558 #303 #305 (#683 partial, quotation only); Capossere #324; All the Seas of the World as quoted by Wolfe #311; Whitehead #312; grembu #1011; Hamad #1026.

Rule, with resolutions (e) and (f): the polysyndetic chain is the archivist's one permitted sentence-level figure, reserved for loss (#49). A refrain may recur across dossier blocks (the same clause about the river under Water and under Trade), and the property that governs whether a recurring sentence is a refrain or an aphorism is GENERALISATION, not repetition: a recurring clause about a concrete thing in the settlement is a refrain and permitted (the Drabble's "her love ... her love", #305, is the model); a recurring sentence about lives, history or mortality in general (#311, #324) is an aphorism whatever its repetition, barred from the dossier and the Herald and rationed to the chronicle line by feature 30. Fragments belong to the Herald's pools, not to the dossier. A reader's complaint that characters "kept repeating the same thing" in synonyms (#1011) sets the ceiling: a refrain recurs verbatim or not at all.

**25. Tense and person: the standing record is present tense, dated to the morning of writing, in the passive; the chronicle is past with the pluperfect for what stood before; first person rare and set apart.**

Rule: the dossier is present tense throughout, because it is a standing record, and its two model exhibits are now on the page: Kay's own Rector's Council passage (primary text: present tense, a spelled-out count, civic nouns, an institutional inventory in the passive) and the gazetteer's standing conditions (a reference work's register applied to Kay's world, not Kay's prose). Resolution (b): the chronicle line is past, with the pluperfect for what stood before, and Tigana's "word went forth" opening belongs to the chronicle line (feature 31); the dossier's reported-speech exception is that a past-tense report may sit inside a present-tense frame verb ("the guild's book records that word went forth"; "it is held that the old duke died in exile"). The tense-by-standpoint rule (present for chosen viewpoints) is now partial on all four of its rows and is not a dossier rule.

**27. Dialogue: formal without being stiff, shaped by the speaker's social world; restraint and subtext; sayings short and about the transitory; history never in speech marks.**

**28. Emotion by subtlety: momentum from a look or a stance, power in quiet moments, a single line that moves because the reader knows why; the counter-charge of telling.**

Rule: the dossier is never told how to feel and never names a feeling; the Herald pool may hold one line meant to move, and it is earned by a fact stated in the preceding sentence; an office-holder is shown by an act (#776).

### VI. Structure, openings, closings

**29. Structure: an architect not a gardener; episodes laid next to each other like stones; strands plaited; chronology rearranged; a scene opens on a view of the place; cause and effect from adjacency.**
Sources (12): Kay #279 #620 #696 #127 #892; Webb #62 #63; Patton #212; Milicia #604; Hatch #89; Capossere #326 #497; Walton #141; Barbour #824; Randall #8; Molly Ison #1035; Salon Futura #710; Erfael #1070 (#514 partial, quotation only).
