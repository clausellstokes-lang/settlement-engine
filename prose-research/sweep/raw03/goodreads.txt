 I have been reading through The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings again, in the process trying to scrutinize Tolkien's language to tease out what it is about his prose that makes it so muscular, forceful and compulsively readable. I have come up with a partial answer, which may surprise Tolkien fans who think they know Tolkien's writing well: Tolkien's prose consists almost entirely of words of one syllable. Furthermore, he uses almost entirely native English words. Most of Tolkien's sentences of normal length contain no more than two or three words of two syllables (the possible exception being the word precious ). Words of three or more syllables - this applies particularly to words of foreign origin (words descended from Latin or Latinized Greek) - are even rarer; so that when they appear in the text, they stand out and are special, even if the reader perceives this only subliminally. Finally, he uses English words, including words he himself coined, in place of more familiar foreign words, e.g., "unlooked-for" instead of "unexpectedly." Interesting to note that Chaucer did the opposite, e.g., instead of using the perfectly good Middle English word "unhap," he introduced the French word "difficulty" into the language. And fortunately for us, Tolkien wrote in a language that is ideal for his brand of story. English is a declarative language. In its ability to tell you what happens next , it is unsurpassed. The English author John Masefield wrote a novel entitled "Odtaa." It turned out that that was an acronym for "one damn thing after another." There are those who would dispute my observations by contending that modern English has evolved into a language where the basic syntactic elements consist of words of one syllable, so that this can be said of all writers in English. (The previous sentence contains 25 1-syllable words, 9 2-syllable words, 4 3-syllable words, and 1 4-syllable word.) This may be true; but if so, few do it so well or so effectively as Tolkien.

 It's worth bearing in mind that Tolkien was a professional philologist specialising in Old English. He had a natural affinity for languages and the sound of words, taught himself both Icelandic and Finnish and created two Elvish languages in some depth, along with smatterings of dwarf and Adûnaic. He literally wrote his great mythology to provide a history for the languages he loved to create. If you ever read the History of Middle-earth series you will see the almost endless reworking of names and elvish vocabulary that he pursued over decades of work. As regards the obvious difference between the tone of The Hobbit and LotR the former was created for his children and in many ways it was a very happy accident that it ever made its way to a publisher. Whilst LotR was requested as a follow up to The Hobbit JRRT's real passion at the time was to get The Silmarillion published. Indeed he offered it as the follow up but it wasn't deemed suitable (which was very fortunate or we would never have got LotR). Bits of The Silmarillion had already crept into The Hobbit (e.g. Gondolin and Elrond) and it became inevitable that as he finally became inspired to start on LotR that so much more of The Silmarillion would underpin that story and give the tremendous feeling of historical authenticity that very few other fantasy books have even got close to. It was probably a fortunate coincidence that JRRT was a very slow writer and that WW2 slowed progress even more. By the time LotR was ready to be published in 1952, 15 years had passed since The Hobbit first appeared. It was then another 2 years before LotR actually began to be published, unfortunately having to be artificially split into 3 volumes due to production costs and paper shortages. This delay was largely down to JRRT still wanting The Silmarillion to be published at the same time and his negotiations with a different publisher before returning to Allen and Unwin. As a result the children who had read and loved The Hobbit were more than ready and mature enough to appreciate LotR. (E.g. if you were 8 when you read The Hobbit you'd be at least 26 when The Return of the King finally appeared.) Personally, I was more than ready to read LotR when I was 12 or 13. Humphrey Carpenter's authorised biography of JRRT has much more information on the above.
