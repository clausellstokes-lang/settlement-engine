 Article excerpt 
 The opening epigraph to guy Gavriel Kay's world fantasy award-winning novel Ysabel comes from the Robert Graves poem "To Juan at the Winter Solstice" and begins "There is one story and one story only/That will prove worth your telling." It sounds like a brief proclamation or anthem of what we might expect as we launch into Kay's luminous tale of the youthful Ned and his family, who find themselves drawn into a tangle of myth, history, and legend in contemporary Provence, but at the end of the novel, Kay offers another quotation, this time from the English critic John Berger: "Never again will a single story be told / as if it were the only one." To some extent, not only Ysabel but Kay's entire career as a fantasy writer inhabits the space between these two quotations, but in the end, I think John Berger wins. As privileged as story may be in his worldview, it's never an absolute, and since the beginning of his career exactly a quarter century ago, with the publication of The Summer Tree, Kay has been fascinated with the multiplicity of story, with the negotiations between new stories and old ones, between story as represented in history and story as transformed by fantasy. That first trilogy, collectively titled The Fionavar Tapestry, set out to celebrate and to some degree restore the tradition of an earlier writer with similar concerns, J. R. R. Tolkien. A decade earlier, Kay had traveled to Oxford to assist Christopher Tolkien in the editing of The Silmarillion, and it is clear he has learned many lessons from the master. …
