 Sailing to Sarantium by Guy Gavriel Kay

 By way of introductory remarks, let me note I must dwell on this book’s ending at length.

 So be warned. Spoilers abound in this review of Guy Gavriel Kay’s Sailing to Sarantium , the first book of his Sarantine Mosaic duology. They’re not super heavy but they’re probably more than many readers want.

 If that is you, then know this is a typically elegantly written riff on history with a few fantastical notes, and such as appealing to those who like such things particularly if they like Byzantium, and please look away now.

 For everybody else though, there’ll be a couple of big cover images, and then we’ll get to work.

 Is that a big enough spoiler break? I think it is.

 Sailing to Sarantium is mostly the tale of Crispin, an ordinary enough mosaicist from Rhodias (Rome) until a big surprise upends his life. That surprise is a summons from the Emperor Valerius (Justinian) at Sarantium (Byzantium) to work on a mosaic there, a summons that’s not even meant for him. It goes to his mentor, but his mentor is old and crochety and sends Crispin in his place.

 The journey is long, perilous, and full of magic and profanity. Sarantium is also perilous and full of profanity. If some of the insults Kay uses here are sample of the surviving documents of the period, then my suspicion that this is my favourite period I never read up on looks very correct. Crispin responds to all this with courage, wisdom, and – you guessed it – profanity. As a result, he wins the usual prizes of a Kay book; the friendship of his fellow wounded outcasts, the admiration and patronage of the great and cunning, and a host of petty enemies. The book is rich with detail of the period, with a particular love given to the chariot races and political intrigues of Sarantium, and is filled with dramatically satisfying scenes.

 In short, it has everything a book should have bar an ending. 

 Yes, the book ends, but it does not have an ending. If you told me that Kay submitted the whole duology to publishers as one book and they made him split it, I would scream “I knew it” like a fishwife learning her husband had been carrying on with some hussy. There is a sense of resolution to the end, but there is not the sense of some great difficulty having been vanquished. Rather, it feels like Crispin has scored a goal right before half-time against a really good team.

 Naturally, I was unimpressed by this.

 Some day, I will read the second book and tell you what I make of the whole series. I suspect that, given Kay’s penchant for creating huge dramatic weight, I will find that I love the characters and story.

 Until that day, this will be a somewhat marred book.
