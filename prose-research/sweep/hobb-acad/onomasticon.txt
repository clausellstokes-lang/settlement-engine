ROBIN HOBB’S «THE WORLD OF THE ELDERING» 
ONOMASTICON: THE STRUCTURAL AND FUNCTIONAL 
SIGNIFICANCE OF CHARACTONYMS 
 
Abstract. The article is devoted to a comprehensive functional and 
semantic analysis of charactonyms within the literary universe created by the 
prominent American fantasy writer Robi n Hobb in her renowned series The 
World of the Elderlings. In literary texts, proper names lose their purely 
identifying real-world function and transform into multi -layered linguistic tools 
intentionally designed by authors to encode deep narrative meanin gs, establish 
socio-cultural contexts, outline psychological profiles, and steer readers' aesthetic 
perception. This study addresses a notable gap in contemporary literary 
onomastics, as Hobb’s intricate onomastic space has remained largely unexplored 
despite its profound semantic and structural complexity. 
The structural and functional analysis conducted in the article reveals that 
the onymic space of the Six Duchies is meticulously constructed upon the 
fundamental principle of national-cultural contrast. This artistic device serves as 
a primary marker of cultural differentiation: evocative, transparent, and 
semantically dense English -derived names function exclusively within the 
dominant culture of the Six Duchies, closely aligning with the diegetic concept of 
the magical potency embedded in a name. Conversely, representatives of 
neighboring nations, such as the Mountain Kingdom or Kalsid, are assigned 
names characterized by phonetic foreignness and an absolute lack of transparent 
semantics for the reader ( e.g., Eyod, Rurisk, Kettricken), which strongly 
reinforces their cultural "otherness" and alien status within the narrative fabric. 
Researcher L. Belei proposed a rigorous classification of the anthroponyms, 
dividing them into distinct functional -semantic subgroups: names of national or

regional significance, socially significant charactonyms, and informational -
evaluative names. The study demonstrates that socially significant anthroponyms 
represent the most dominant and structurally diverse group, which perfectly 
mirrors the early medieval setting of the fantasy universe where a person's social 
mask, status, and destiny are inextricably linked. Key examples such as Chivalry, 
Regal, Fitz, and The Fool are analyzed to illustrate how names indicate royal 
legitimacy, political ambitions, or severe societal stigma. Furthermore, 
informational-evaluative charactonyms, including Shrewd, Desire, and Patience, 
are investigated as tools of direct psychological characterization that explicitly 
encapsulate the core inner nature, behavioral patterns, and moral capacities of the 
characters. The paper concludes that Hobb's charactonyms transcend basic 
nomenclature, serving as a vital mechanism of character development and world-
building. Prospective areas for further scholarly scientific inquiry are identified, 
including expanding the linguistic analysis to other cycles of the universe and 
examining the translated variants of these proper names. 
Keywords: literary onomastics, charactonym, anthroponym, Robin Hobb, 
The World of the Elderlings, fantasy literature, functional-semantic analysis.

Statement of the problem.  Fiction is a unique space in which proper 
names take on a special functional and seman tic significance. Unlike real-world 
proper names, which primarily serve an identifying function, charactonyms – 
proper names in a literary work – are linguistic units deliberately chosen by the 
author to shape a character’s image, set the tone of the work and influence the 
reader’s perception. 
This issue is particularly relevant in the context of fantasy literature, where 
the author constructs not only characters and a plot, but also a complete fictional 
universe with its own culture, social hierarchy and onomastic space. The novels 
of the American writer Robin Hobb, which make up the literary universe of ‘The 
Elderlings’, are a striking example of such construction: every name in this 
universe carries semantic weight, reflecting the cultural, social and psychological 
dimensions of the society depicted. 
The study of charactonyms in Robin Hobb’s novels has not only a purely 
linguistic but also a literary significance, as it allows us to uncover the 
mechanisms of the author’s artistic thinking an d to trace how linguistic means 
serve as a tool for character development and the construction of the works’ 
imagery. 
Analysis of recent studies and publications.  Literary onomastics has 
emerged as a distinct discipline at the intersection of linguistics a nd literary 
studies. The domestic tradition of literary-onomastic studies is represented by the 
works of Yu.  O. Karpenko, who introduced the term ‘ poetonym’ into academic 
discourse and defined it as a proper name in a literary text characterised by a 
particular connotative potential [1]. 
L. Beley, founder of the Uzhhorod School of Literary Onomastics, defines 
this as the proper names of characters that appear in literary and artistic texts [2]. 
M. M. Torchynskyi proposed a comprehensive classification of th e onymic field 
based on the following criteria: the nature of the named objects, the characteristics 
of proper name formation, the stylistic features of proper names, and the scope 
and form of their usage [3]. 
Foreign researchers refer to proper names in l iterary texts as ‘names in 
literature’, which coincides with the name of the discipline that studies these 
names. The terminological g roup of the International Council of Onomastic 
Sciences (ICOS) refers to them as charactonym [4], indicating that the emph asis 
is placed on anthroponyms rather than on all other categories of onyms in a literary 
work [5, p. 94]. 
At the same time, the onomastics of Robin Hobb’s novels remain largely 
unexplored in both domestic and international linguistics. Isolated references  to 
this material can be found in fan communities and online forums dedicated to 
discussing ‘The Elderlings’, yet no scholarly studies have been identified that

systematically analyse Hobb’s charactonyms from the perspective of literary 
onomastics. It is precisely this gap that the present article seeks to fill. 
Aim of the article.  The aim of this article is to conduct a functional -
semantic analysis of charactonyms in Robin Hobb’s novels from the ‘World of 
the Elderlings’ series, to identify typological groups of charactonyms, to establish 
their main functions, and to determine their role in character development and the 
construction of the works’ imagery. 
To achieve this aim, the following tasks are to be addressed: 1) to define 
the concept of a charactonym and clarify its characteristics in relation to the 
material of the analysed novels; 2) to describe the principles of constructing the 
onymic space of the ‘World of the Elderlings’; 3) to classify the identified 
charactonyms into functional subgroups and carry out their semantic analysis. 
Presentation of the main research material.  A charactonym is a proper 
name (most often an anthroponym or toponym) in a literary work that creates and 
portrays a character, shapes the perception of that character, and influences the 
reader’s attitude towards them. Unlike real proper names, a charactonym is always 
the result of the author’s choice and therefore conveys certain information about 
the bearer: their social status, ethnic origin, character traits or role in the plot. 
‘The Elderlings’ is a literary universe invented by the American writer 
Robin Hobb and described in several trilogies. This world is at the developmental 
stage of the early Middle Ages. All proper names of characters and geographical 
features belonging to the culture of the Six Duchies carry semantic meaning; that 
is, they are charactonyms in the full sense of the word. 
A fundamentally important feature of the onymic space in the novels is its 
construction based on the principle of national -cultural contrast. Evocative, 
semantically charged names are used exclusively within the culture of the Six 
Duchies, which is linked to the concept of the magical power of a name, a central 
theme in Hobb’s fictional world. In contrast, representatives of other peoples 
(mountain folk, outlanders, etc.) bear names with an unusual sound, which ca rry 
no semantic weight for the reader and emphasise the characters’ cultural 
‘otherness’. 
Researcher L. Belei [2] proposed a rigorous classification of the 
anthroponyms, dividing them into distinct functional-semantic subgroups: names 
of national or region al significance, socially significant charactonyms, and 
informational-evaluative names. Charactonyms of national (or regional) 
significance primarily serve to create the national flavour the author seeks in a 
work. In Hobb’s novels, this subgroup is repres ented by the names of characters 
who do not belong to the culture of the Six Duchies. Their distinctive feature lies 
in the absence of semantic meaning coupled with phonetic ‘foreignness’, which 
in itself serves as a marker of foreign origin.

An example is  the name Eyod – the reigning king of the Mountain 
Kingdom: 
«Our Sacrifice, er, ah, you say, King Eyod will welcome you. And he will 
show to you all his daughter, to be your Sacrifice, hem, ah, Queen. And his son, 
who will rule for her here». She stumbled through this explanation, with many a 
pause, and many encouraging nods from me [6]. 
The name Eyod has no literal meaning, yet it stands out from the names of 
characters in the culture of the Six Duchies due to its phonetic unusualness. At the 
same time, the epithet shared by all members of the ruling dynasty is significant: 
Sacrifice – The Sacrificial One – which points to the symbolic role of the ruler in 
the culture of the mountain people. 
The names of Prince Rurisk and Princess Kettricken are constructed  in a 
similar way. The unusual nature of the name Kettricken is even emphasised in the 
characters’ dialogue – King Shrewd cannot remember how it sounds, which is a 
telling detail: 
«Almost I forgot to tell you why I had called you here. It is, of course, th e 
mountain princess. Ketkin, I think her name was...» «Kettricken. I at least 
remember that much. A skinny little child, the last time I saw her. So, she is the 
one you have selected?» [6]. 
The case of a character named Burrich – a stablehand at the royal court, 
originally from Kalsid – is somewhat unusual. His name is also perceived as 
phonetically ‘foreign’, which emphasises his foreign origins. At the same time, 
researchers allow for partial semantic interpretation: the name may contain an 
allusion to th e English word ‘burr’ (a thorn or bur), which correlates with the 
character’s complex nature. This is the only instance in the novels where a 
‘foreign’ name may have a hidden connotation. 
Standard English names are almost entirely absent from the novels, and 
when they do appear, they belong mainly to members of the working class (Gill, 
Hod). The exception is the name Molly – the protagonist’s beloved. Dictionaries 
record ‘molly’ as a colloquial/slang term for a girl or young woman, which gives 
grounds to regard this name as a charactonym with a tender, affectionate 
connotation, aptly characterising this woman’s role in the protagonist’s life. 
Chronologically significant charactonyms are intended to situate a work 
within a specific time period. Howev er, no examples with this function were 
found in the analysed novels. This is explained by the very nature of the work: the 
novels are based on entirely fictional events and characters in a fictional universe, 
so any reference to a real historical period w ould be incomprehensible to the 
reader and not perceived as significant. 
Socially significant anthroponyms reflect the characters’ social status, their 
origins and their role in society. It is this subgroup that is most prominently 
featured in Hobb’s novels and most diverse in its manifestations.

One of the key examples is the name Chivalry – the eldest son of King 
Shrewd, heir to the throne of the Six Duchies: 
And didn't Verity, after all, make a much better king -in-waiting than had 
Chivalry? So rigorously noble was Chivalry that he made all others feel slatternly 
and stingy in his presence; he'd never allowed himself a moment's respite from 
what was right, and while he was too chivalrous to sneer at those who did, one 
always had the feeling that his perfec t behavior was a silent reproach to those 
with less self-discipline [6]. 
The word ‘chivalry’ is defined as ‘the combination of qualities expected of 
an ideal knight, namely courage, honour, courtesy, justice, and a readiness to help 
the weak’ (chivalry, no bility, bravery). The name Chivalry is not merely a 
description of a person, but also a social marker: it points to the ideal qualities of 
a knight-ruler and, at the same time, to the burden borne by a person who must 
live up to their own name. 
A telling example in the context of social connotations is the name Regal – 
the king’s younger son: 
«When you talk about rogues who stay in bed all morning and do nothing 
except fuss about hair and garments, you mean how Regal is» [6]. 
The word ‘regal’ means ‘of, resembling, or fit for a monarch, especially in 
being magnificent or dignified’. Regal is the third son and has little chance of 
inheriting the throne; however, his mother deliberately gave him a name that 
emphasises royal dignity, seeking to legitimise her son’s claim to the throne. Thus, 
this name encodes not only the character’s traits (arrogance, haughtiness) but also 
a social intention linked to the struggle for power. 
A special place in the social onomastics of the novels is occupied by the 
name Fitz – the illegitimate son of Prince Chivalry, who is the main character: 
Regal looked perplexed. «Him? It's the Fitz. Chivalry's bastard. Sneaking 
and thieving as always» [6]. 
The Urban Dictionary [7] defines ‘Fitz’ as ‘means “son of”... but can also 
refer to an  illegitimate child, a bastard, someone born out of wedlock’. 
Historically, the prefix ‘Fitz’ was used by the British monarchy to denote 
illegitimate offspring who were granted official recognition. In Hobb’s work, this 
name functions as a social marker of  dual nature: it simultaneously signifies 
recognition and disgrace, status and stigma. The choice of this charactonym for 
the protagonist is of fundamental importance to the entire plot and thematic 
structure of the novels. 
Another significant social charactonym is The Fool – King Shrewd’s court 
fool. This character’s real name, origins and even gender remain a mystery 
throughout the entire cycle. His sole name in the eyes of the reader and most of 
the characters is his social function or role: The Fool. This is a charactonym where 
the social mask becomes the character’s sole visible identity.

The names of the ancient kings are also socially motivated: Taker and 
Ruler, reflecting the nature of each monarch’s reign. The author even specifically 
points out the nature of such naming: 
A more ancient tradition attributes such names to accident, at least 
initially... But for the purposes of royalty, it is better to have the common folk 
believe that a boy given a noble name must grow to have a noble nature [6]. 
Descriptive-evaluative charactonyms serve to provide an emotional and 
expressive assessment of the characters. Hobb’s novels feature exclusively 
informative-evaluative charactonyms, whilst no expressive -evaluative ones 
(where the evaluation is an end in itself ) have been identified. This is consistent 
with the overall stylistic approach of the novels: the author strives for 
psychological depth rather than an external evaluative effect. 
The most telling informational-evaluative charactonym is the name of King 
Shrewd. The characters repeatedly point out the correspondence between the 
name and the character: 
«He is the King!» Molly protested. «And named Shrewd to be shrewd». 
But our father the King is not a hasty man, as well we know. Shrewd is as 
Shrewd does, as the common folk say [6]. 
The word ‘shrewd’ means ‘having or showing sharp powers of judgement; 
astute’ (practical, intelligent, cunning). Shrewd is indeed an extremely pragmatic, 
and at times ruthless, ruler who views even his own grandson primarily as a pawn 
in the political game. 
Lady Desire – the king’s second wife – bears a name that encapsulates her 
defining trait: ‘a strong feeling of wanting to have something or  wishing for 
something to happen’ (desire, passion, ambition). As queen, she was never 
satisfied with what she had achieved and always strove for more – power, 
recognition, and privileges for her son. 
Patience – the wife of Prince Chivalry – is one of the novel’s most profound 
characters. The word ‘patience’ means ‘the capacity to accept or tolerate delay, 
problems, or suffering without becoming annoyed or anxious’ (forbearance, 
tolerance). A woman who was unable to bear a child and had to endure the public 
revelation of her husband’s bastard son, she eventually accepted Fitz and raised 
him as her own son. It is precisely her patience and capacity for forgiveness that 
are her defining traits, accurately reflected in her name. 
Conclusions. The character names in Robin Hobb’s novels form an integral 
part of the fictional world of ‘The Elderlings’. They serve not only a nominative 
function, but also play a role in character development, social identification and 
cultural differentiation. The onymic space of the novels is constructed on the 
principle of national -cultural contrast: evocative, semantically rich names 
function exclusively within the culture of the Six Duchies, whilst characters from

 380 
other nations bear phonetically ‘foreign’ names without transparent semantics. 
The most prevalent are socially significant charactonyms (Chivalry, Regal, Fitz, 
The Fool, Taker, Ruler), which reflect the characters’ social status, origin and role 
within the social hierarchy. This corresponds to the medieval setting of the novels, 
where name and status are inextricably linked. Informational and evaluative 
charactonyms (Shrewd, Desire, Patience, Chivalry) serve as psychological 
characterisations, revealing the inner essence of the character through the 
semantics of the name.  
In most cases, the correspondence between name and character is not a 
coincidence but a conscious authorial device, emphasised by direct references in 
the text. Chronologically significant and expressive-evaluative charactonyms are 
absent from the novels, which is a natural consequence of the nature of a fantasy 
text with a completely fictional universe and a psychologically realistic approach 
to characterisation. 
Areas for further research include extending the analysis to other cycles of 
“The World of the  Elderlings”, a comparative analysis of charactonyms in the 
original and in translations, and an examination of the toponymic level of the 
onomasticon in Hobb’s novels.
