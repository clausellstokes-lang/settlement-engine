## Part A. The record register (features 1 to 5)

These five features come from the record-register angle (29 kept rows), joined by the rows from other angles that read the same forms: the compendium of reports, the handbook entry, the field note, the back of the book, and the tense of the anthropologist's report. This is the register nearest the dossier, and the register map names it first.

### 1. The report's apparatus: it dates itself, names its institutions by their own terms, codes and cross-refers

Support: 4 sources, 10 quotations, 10 rows [485, 487, 742, 765, 767, 778, 781, 783, 784, 788]; all ten are Le Guin's own fiction read at second hand (a fan transcription of 'The Matter of Seggri', the estate's excerpts page for The Birthday of the World, a blog quoting the Rocannon's World handbook in full, a BSFA review quoting The Left Hand of Darkness chapter seven). Each form is a single work, so each limb is single-source. The Seggri frame dates its own assertion "As of the date of this report" [485] and never against a calendar year; it names a procedure as a capitalised term it never glosses, "They made Resehavanar's Choice" [487]. The Rocannon's World handbook closes two species entries on a two-word code, "Status C-Prime" and "Status E-Query" [778]; gives the year to the second, "Revolution: 800 Earthdays 8 hrs. 11 min. 42 sec." [788]; sends the reader to numbered maps inside a parenthesis, "(see maps 3114-a, b, c, 3115-a, b.)" [783]; cross-refers forward rather than repeating, "(see description of intelligent species below)" [784]; and addresses its reader with a bare imperative, "Note horizontal social cleavage into 2 pseudo-races" [781], a row the verifier routed to the DM page. The Left Hand field notes put an exact decimal and a range in one sentence, "the 8.4 month gestation period and the 6 to 8 month" [742]. 'Mountain Ways' opens, before its first narrative paragraph, with "Note for readers unfamiliar with the planet O" [765] and sets out the four marriages as a labelled list, each pairing given its own term, "the 'Morning marriage'" [767].

Rule: A block carries the apparatus of a report in words. It dates itself against the record (as of this entry, in the year the mill burned) and not against a calendar figure. It names a settlement institution by the settlement's own capitalised term (the Reeve's Court, the Ash Tithe, the Widow's Portion) and does not gloss it in the block that first uses it. It cross-refers to another block rather than repeating that block. The record's precision is a register marker, but the contract bans digits, so the archivist writes a span and a single figure in the same sentence in words, exactly as the field note does (a gestation of eight months and a fortnight, and a range of six to eight). Status codes, numbered maps and the bare imperative to the reader (note this) are DM-page voice and never enter a block.

### 3. Many voices, one plain frame: the register of an embedded voice is its own, the frame's is not

Dialect and accent are named (she speaks with the fen vowels) and never spelled. A note in the archivist's own voice is dry.

### 4. How the record is made: the story is told first, the chart is drawn, the facts are got right

Support: 7 sources, 9 quotations, 9 rows [225, 291, 304, 432, 738, 759, 763, 851, 861]; eight are Le Guin's own words, from her forewords, two interviews and a workshop answer; one is Atwood. "research into nonexistent history is to tell the story" and find out what happened [738] (Foreword to Tales from Earthsea, quoted by Troy Press); for the kinship rules of the planet O, "I drew charts, with male and female symbols, and lines" [763]; the Historians of Hain are to reports "as squirrels are to nuts" [759]. Asked whether she builds the world before writing the story, "Yes. I do." [851]; in a historical novel as in science fiction, "There are some facts, and you want to get 'em right" [861]. Yet the record grows from the telling: places and events she named were, when written, merely words, "'empty' nouns" [225, fullSentence: 'Plausibility in Fantasy', the paragraph on the later exploits of Ged]; "I had put clues into the book, without knowing it" [304, fullSentence: Karabatak interview, answer six]; and of her whole invented universe, "its history consists largely of gaps" [291, fullSentence: Foreword to The Birthday of the World, second paragraph]. Atwood on that foreword: "Le Guin describes process rather than theory" [432].

Rule: The generator's order is Le Guin's own. The chart (the seed's tables of kin, trade and tenure) exists before any block is written, and its facts are exact; the record is nonetheless written by telling what happened and finding out. A name set down in one block as an empty noun is a clue a later block may honour, and need not. The dossier states its facts exactly and declares the gaps in its history as facts of the archive, not as faults.

### 5. Tense: the ethnographic present is a distancing device; narrative keeps the past

Support: 4 sources, 10 quotations, 10 rows [70, 126, 127, 235, 244, 869, 870, 872, 874, 875]; nine are Le Guin's own words from two essays and one workshop answer, one is Walton. In 'Some Thoughts on Narrative' (1980, in Dancing at the Edge of the World, p. 38) she observes that anthropological reports about people long dead, "whose societies no longer exist, are written in the present tense" [869], and that "The present tense takes the story out of time." [870]; narrative, by contrast, "does not normally use the present tense except for special effect" or affectation [872], because "narrative is language used to connect events in time" [874] and "Narrative is a stratagem of mortality." [875] (p. 39). In 'On Rules of Writing' (2003) she faults writers who "abandon narrative past for the inflexible present tense" [126, the verifier's compression] since the "narrative past tense involves and includes past, present, and future" [127], and answers the fear of distance with "But distance lends enchantment" [235, fullSentence: the fourth paragraph]; limited third person as now practised is, like the present tense, "a kind of flashlight beam" [244, fullSentence: answer to Patricia, Navigating the Ocean of Story, Session 1]. Walton on A Wizard of Earthsea: the story is "told entirely within the world, but after the story" [70].

Reconciliation (the chair's tense contradiction). The dossier's present-tense contract is not the workshop present Le Guin attacks, a flashlight on a single actor; it is the anthropologist's present she describes as the tense of a report on a society, which takes the report out of time and is itself a distancing device. So the archivist's blocks are present tense by her own account of the form, and that present is a form of the distance she prizes. The chronicle line is narrative, language that connects events in time, and it is written in the narrative past, told within the world after the event, as Walton reads Earthsea. The earlier draft's feature 5 wrote "told within the world, after the story" as a rule for the whole dossier and thereby contradicted its own contract; the rule now belongs to the chronicle line only. One caution: [869] and [870] are her observation and her gloss; [872] opens with "This might be why", a hedged causal claim; the observation is exact and the causal story is hers.

Rule: The archivist writes in the ethnographic present. The present is the tense of a report on a people, and it holds the settlement out of time; it is a distance, not an intimacy, and the archivist does not use it to make a scene feel immediate. The chronicle line, which connects events in time, is written in the narrative past and told from inside the world after the event. A block never switches tense to make a moment vivid.

Rule: The archivist writes short plain declaratives in familiar words, in a grave and even tone. Plainness is the chosen register, not a fallback from a richer one, and it is the hard register: a flat block is a failed block, not a plain one. The dossier accepts that spareness can read as cold and answers with an exact object and a heard cadence in every block, never with ornament, and never forgets that a block's plainness does not by itself make it worth reading.

### 7. Plain is not flat: the journalism trap

Support: 2 sources, 10 quotations, 10 rows [2, 3, 106, 107, 108, 294, 295, 799, 800, 801]; both sources are Le Guin herself (Elfland p. 8; blog 95, 2015), so this is single-author doctrine, corroborated from outside only by "precise" at [139] and [371]. The failed style "is not really simple, but flat" [2]; "It is journalistic prose." [107, 799]; in journalism "the suppression of the author's personality and sensibility is deliberate" [108, 800]; "This technique is right, for a newspaper." [801], wrong for a novel, dead wrong for a fantasy; its sensory cues are vague, so "the rocks, the wind, the trees are not there" [106] and "the scenery is cardboard, or plastic" [3]. Blog 95: "A toneless, inexact language is incapable of creating landscape" [295, fullSentence: paragraph three], and "A fantastic setting requires vivid and specific description" [294, fullSentence: the same paragraph].

Reconciliation with feature 25 (the chair's "telling" contradiction). The earlier draft's rule, "any sentence that merely reports is rewritten", pulled against feature 25's "the dossier tells". The fault Le Guin names is not that journalism reports; it is that it is toneless, inexact, and suppresses sensibility. So the walker's target is the toneless sentence, not the reporting sentence. A told sentence with an exact noun and a heard cadence passes; a report with a vague noun and no cadence fails. The old wording is withdrawn.

Rule: The record register is the dossier's own danger. An archivist who suppresses all sensibility writes journalism, and journalism cannot make a landscape. Every block keeps at least one exact sensory cue (the wet thatch, the cracked ice on the cup) and one exact verb; a sentence that is toneless or inexact is rewritten until the thing it names is felt. Reporting itself is not the fault.

### 10. The glimpse of a longer history, and the record that has none

Support: 10 sources, 14 quotations, 14 rows [70, 73, 223, 224, 225, 287, 291, 304, 314, 319, 321, 323, 372, 735]; seven rows are her own words, and four of the fourteen are her doctrine from Tolkien or a critic's contrast with him. Her doctrine on Tolkien: references to places, people and events outside the immediate story give "a conviction of the reality of the immediate scene" [223, fullSentence: 'Plausibility in Fantasy', third paragraph of the answer] because the scene is shown as "part of a much greater landscape, a long history" [224, fullSentence: the same sentence]; "You know where you are, you know what the weather is" [287, fullSentence: Public Books, section one]. Her own practice, in her words: she named events and places that were then "'empty' nouns" [225, fullSentence] and "put clues into the book, without knowing it" [304, fullSentence]; her Hainish "history consists largely of gaps" [291, fullSentence]. Walton: Earthsea has a history and other countries beyond the page, yet is "a world with a clear line drawn around it" [73]. Readers of Always Coming Home: the present day is "remembered only in stray fragments of folklore" [319]; she asks readers to "bear with some unfamiliar terms" until they come clear [321, fullSentence: Bonnell quoting her opening]; the Omelas narrator "confesses to lacking detailed knowledge of the laws" [323, fullSentence: Tearle's summary]; Earthsea's laws are "more hinted on than explained" [372, fullSentence].
