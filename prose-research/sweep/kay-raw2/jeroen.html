Guy Gavriel Kay – Tigana (1990). Metaphor versus Realism.

Posted on April 11, 2025 by Jeroen Admiraal

8/10

A long, slow, yet impeccably realised and dramatic revenge story. Tigana is a transitionary work when looking at Guy Gavriel Kay’s bibliography as a whole. Here, he maintains his classical fantasy roots with magic and wizards, yet also moves towards the historical fantasy settings that he would become so well known for. For me, this novel is ultimately a success, but I had some problems with it.

Here is where I think this novel falls flat, and it takes a little explaining… Kay took the kind of story that Lord Dunsany would write: a beautiful, dreamlike fairytale story about a wizard taking revenge on a nation by removing all memory of it, and the few survivors who remember it grieve over its loss with the kind of pathos of longing for a lost place of idyllic perfection, an idealized Eden that cannot be reclaimed. And Lord Dunsany would populate this with some archetypes and write this in, say, 17 pages, and it would be the most beautiful, melancholic, heart-rending thing that you’ve ever read. In fact, I wouldn’t be surprised if Dunsany has actually written such a story. And one would take such a story at face value for the kind of fairytale that it is; that portrays an exaggerated mood, a pathos, and holds no more detail than can be communicated in 17 pages because it is complete in what it achieves and can be appreciated in that way.

Now blow up that story to nearly 700 pages by adding copious amounts of world-building for immersion, lots of deep dives into character motivations which ultimately lead to a lot of repetition.

The first problem is that the more world-building Kay adds, the more questions about details bubble up about this Dunsanian fairytale premise. The simple fairytale idea of a kingdom lost to memory is suddenly complicated by the mechanics of it all. Because, the type of world-building employed in a 700 page fantasy novel is a totally different beast than one would find in a short story, and insists on details. The act of removing a kingdom entails an invasion and displacement of a people and possibly genocide, and suddenly the grief over the loss of the idea of the kingdom, which Kay still focuses his story on, seems less pressing than the crimes against humanity that were part of it. The loss of the idea of Tigana through magical means works as a romantic fantasy metaphor for the more grim reality of wiping a country from the map, but Kay put both of these things in his novel, the metaphor and the reality, and the two clash in their expression.

A downstream problem of this is that it makes the characters weird. The characters are still a form of archetypes that have the loss of the idea of Tigana as their main motivation to do anything, as if that is so much more important than their murdered family members. And since their motivation is rather abstract in nature, Kay has to keep repeating it in the text, and I didn’t readily feel the emotion behind that. This problem is compounded by Kay’s immense wordiness. He easily waffles on for page after page on every little governor’s or farm wife’s deliberations. He also hasn’t quite found the right balance between giving backstory and letting the plot move forward. The end result is a lot of pages about a lot of details and a lot of repetitive description, and not much of it feels meaningful. Parts of this novel feel quite slow.

However… if one chooses to buy into the idea of the dreamy romanticism of a lost country of beauty, then this novel becomes beautifully melancholic in turn. After all, this is a tale of vengeance, full of injustices and dramatic emotions. In short, compelling stuff. And when the characters feel hatred over their personal losses, and the magical removal of Tigana feels like a personal insult, then the story comes alive. Then the abstract becomes personal. One of the main characters, Devin, rediscovers that he had been part of Tigana, and that in itself is only an abstract motivation, but the fight to reclaim his heritage is set in motion and driven by the princely figure Alessan, whom Devin looks up to, so his journey becomes one of personal growth. Alessan, meanwhile, edges dangerously close to obsession.

In some ways, Kay critically regards the monomania of the surviving Tiganans, and this way he lifts the characters above that of archetypes. A thematic complexity enriches their stories too, as Kay explores the roles of memory, love, identity, the power of music, the finer details of nationality, generational guilt, and so on. He set up all the moral ambiguities as pieces on a chessboard, just waiting for the final moves in the last part of the novel. In short, this is a complex and bittersweet novel. And even when parts of the story feel slow, Kay is such an eloquent writer that scenes and dialogues can be savoured just for their writing.
