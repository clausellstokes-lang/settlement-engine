

 Guy Gavriel Kay: Margins of History - Locus

 May 12, 2025September 12, 2025
 locusmag

 0 Comments

GUY GAVRIEL KAY was born November 7, 1954 in Weyburn, Saskatchewan, Canada, and grew up in Winnipeg. He was influenced by Greek myths, fairy tales, and the works of J.R.R. Tolkien, E.R. Eddison, and Fritz Leiber, among others. He attended the University of Manitoba, earning a BA in philosophy in 1975. During Kay’s time at the university, J.R.R. Tolkien’s son Christopher Tolkien asked him to help complete his father’s unfinished The Silmarillion. They worked on the book at Oxford from 1974-75, and it was published in 1977.

Kay studied law at the University of Toronto, graduating in 1978. He was admitted to the bar, though he never practiced law, choosing to focus on writing instead. From 1982-89 he was associate producer and principal writer for the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation radio series The Scales of Justice, which dramatized real legal cases.

While working for the CBC, Kay began publishing novels set in invented worlds, starting with the Fionavar Tapestry: The Summer Tree (1984); The Wandering Fire (1986), winner of the Casper Award (now known as the Aurora Award); and The Darkest Road (1986). His next books included fewer overt fantasy elements and drew more on actual history. Sailing to Sarantium (1998) and Lord of Emperors (2000) form the Sarantine Mosaic, set in an alternate version of ancient Byzantium; both were World Fantasy Award finalists. Aurora Award winner and World Fantasy Award finalist Tigana (1990) is a standalone set in a world inspired by Renaissance Italy. A Song for Arbonne (1992) is inspired by medieval Provence, The Lions of Al-Rassan (1995) by medieval Spain, and The Last Light of the Sun (2004) is set during an analogue of the Viking invasions. World Fantasy Award winner Ysabel (2007) is related to the Fionavar Tapestry, but stands alone. World Fantasy Award finalist Under Heaven (2010) and River of Stars (2013) are set in a fantasy version of China. Children of Earth and Sky (2016), A Brightness Long Ago (2019), and All the Seas of the World (2022) are set in a milieu again inspired by Renaissance Europe. Some of Kay’s poetry has been collected in Beyond This Dark House (2003). He was named to the Order of Canada in 2014, the country’s highest civilian honor.

His latest novel, Written on the Dark, is set in a world reminiscent of medieval France.

Kay married Laura Beth Cohen in 1984, and they have two sons. They live in Toronto.

Excerpt from the interview:

My new book Written on the Dark is out at the end of May. You want to laugh? The Globe and Mail ran a feature on the Big Books of the Spring recently, and it’s on that list, and a lot of friends online and people I know were saying, ‘It’s great that you’re on the list, but why is it at the bottom of the list?’ Because it’s out on May 27th! The list is chronological! It’s the mood; people are looking for a reason to be indignant or outraged or irked. It’s a mood that penetrates everywhere. I got a rueful smile out of it.

“Mark Twain did not say, ‘History doesn’t repeat, but it rhymes.’ Someone else did, a psychoanalyst whose name I forget, which tells you how obscure it is. But it’s always attributed to Twain – anything is always Twain or Wilde or Churchill. They said everything. But ‘History doesn’t repeat, but it rhymes’ resonates for me a lot. I don’t ever want to write a book where things map directly – where a character maps onto a real person, or where an event maps precisely onto a real event. I’m actually wondering if I have almost backed into intense contemporary relevance with this novel inspired by events in the Hundred Years’ War, that had no such formal purpose in my mind.

“Every book I write, I want to be relevant for the reader today, in the sense that they find aspects of history that relate in various ways to aspects of the present. I always want to do that. But I wonder if readers in May, June, and July will respond to the characters feeling nervous and anxious about ordinary, everyday actions. I wonder if the intensity of what people are feeling right now will end up infusing their read, perhaps more than it would have two years ago.

“I’ve written directly before about art and power, the dynamic of artists rely­ing on patrons and the powerful to live. In this case, it’s Marina di Seressa. But that is also a very contemporary thing, because I know too many young writers who have to write in a certain way because the market tells them what it will take from them, and I know too many – sadly – older writers who cannot get published anymore because the market has decided that what they write doesn’t work for it anymore. If you call power, in the modern case, the marketplace rather than a Borgia or Medici prince, I’ve written about that issue directly here.

“Marina, in this book, is a classic case of someone who says, ‘I have a fam­ily to support. My husband died, I’ve got two young children and an elderly mother, and no other source of income – I need to write what significant pa­trons might want from me. I’m lucky enough to appeal to significant patrons, I know what they want, and my life requires that I try to deliver that.’ Thierry Villar, by contrast, lives poor, and is comfortable being poor – if ideally not in debt to dangerous people – and has the freedom today of someone who writes villanelles and sonnets; they’re not looking to make a living from that writing. I was interested, with the two of them, in the idea of art needing to deliver what the market, in some incarnation, wants from the artist. There are a couple of other poets in the novel. It’s fun writing a bad writer, too. I did a bad troubadour early in A Song for Arbonne, and it’s ridiculously enjoyable to hack around with a hack’s work.

“I don’t actually enjoy writing. It’s not fun for me. I have to quell murder­ous impulses towards writers who say, ‘I love writing!’ I’m always too aware of the space between what I want to achieve and what I’m capable of doing as a fallible human being. Writing, for me, is working as hard as I can to reduce the gap between what I would like to produce and what emerges. But once in a while, it’s fun, and often, these are funny scenes. I labor under the sad delu­sion that I’m funny. I like funny people, and I like being with amusing friends. I include limericks and puns in this category, by the way, and writing funny scenes, characters, bits, is the fun part of the writ­ing process. Sports scenes, too; I’ve written various sporting activities, like the chariot races in the two Sarantium books. I’ve always been a sports fan, and played a lot of sports when I was younger – probably for longer than I should have, according to my body.

“If one character skewers another with a line of banter, I can enjoy writing that, essentially as much as I enjoy doing it or having it done to me by a friend. That kind of writing, the Round Table – the Algonquin, not the Arthurian! – is pleasurable because often you’re told a character is witty or bril­liant but you never see it demonstrated. Someone is said to be the funniest person in their college group, but nothing they say is memorable. That’s tricky writing – to convey the idea that a character is genuinely smart. Dorothy Dunnett did this better than anyone in her Lymond books, and one of the ways she did it is by having him, or sometimes other people, say things the reader doesn’t understand – he’ll make a reference, a joke, make a sly remark, and you don’t get it. You might get it later – somebody else may explain it, or a clue may be dropped, but in the moment, what you’re coming away with is, ‘These are really smart people.’

“I almost feel I’m overidentified with the issue with writing about the innermost thoughts of real people. I’ve done speeches on this. I say, ‘I don’t want to pretend I knew Henry VIII’s favorite position in bed.’ That’s flippant, but it makes the point for a certain number of people. When I fictionalize, my Thierry Villar is not François Villon. He is inspired by François Villon, and when I do it that way, the reader and I are sharing the recognition that we don’t know what the real person’s actual thoughts, loves, hates, feelings were. I’ll use their writing and extrapolate but beyond that, we don’t know. Embedded in my method is an exculpation from the anxiety of formal historical accuracy – because we are not in France, because this is not Henry V and the arrogant French cavalry at Agincourt. The reader and I share an understanding, and I’ve always depended on the understanding of readers, to misquote Tennessee Williams.

“I sometimes have trouble reading historical work when I feel like the writer is forcing their contem­porary wish that something be so onto figures from 500 or 1,500 years ago – when they are superim­posing the present on the past, rather than finding things in the past that the present is interested in looking at. That’s a distinction for me that matters. I’ve talked often about looking for the unwritten histories, the people at the margins of history, and this has been part of my fiction forever. The unwrit­ten lives of women, and of the poor. Though I do also love the chronicles of the dukes and the popes. They are not exclusive ways of looking.

“I think every serious writer is teaching a reader how to read them. That fits into a classic paradigm: If you think someone’s poured you a cup of coffee, but they’ve poured you tea, your first sip is, ‘Oh!’ Even if you like tea. Because you weren’t prepared for it, it’s startling. Because there’s so much distrac­tion, so much competition for our attention, and so much stress in the world, reading slots more and more into, ‘I want this to make me comfortable. I bought this book because a friend said it would fit what makes me comfortable, and… it’s not quite that.’ We retreat towards what we already know we like.

“Interviewers, reviewers, and readers have sug­gested that there has been a diminishing level of the fantastic in my work from book to book to book through time, and it’s just not true. For me, it’s about, what does this story demand, require – re­quest, if it’s more polite – of me as the writer in terms of various elements that an author has available to work with? One of them will be the supernatural. Another might be the erotic. Another might be intrigue, or suspense, and another might be lyric language. What does this book need from me to be as good as I can make it? Some books that I’ve writ­ten (and still write) require a greater component of the fantastic, but with others, it would be pandering if I tucked in some flamboyant illustration of the fantastic, if it didn’t actually need to be in that book. I would be doing it because there’s a market that’s happier when they have it (along the lines of what we were saying about Marina in this new novel). And by gosh, if there’s a way to tuck that something in without twisting yourself in knots, why not do it? Well, not twisting yourself in knots isn’t a sufficient motivation for me to put something into a book. It’s got to be more: ‘The book needs this.’

–Guy Gavriel Kay
