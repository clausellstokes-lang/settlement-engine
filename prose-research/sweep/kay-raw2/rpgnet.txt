I love his works on the whole, personally. The bulk are fantasy by way of thinly veiled historical fiction, and just about any can work as a starting point depending on how you like your fantasy-history balance and what particular times and places hold your interest. Personally, my one book recommendation is The Lions of Al-Rassan, set in post Ummayad but pre Reconquista Spain, as the Taifa Emirates are beginning to be incorporated into larger more state like structures. Sure, none of those proper nouns make an appearance, but it's not subtle. Also not named but not subtle is the religious backgrounds of our three viewpoint characters, one of whom is clearly Jewish, one of whom is clearly Muslim, and one of whom is clearly Christian - and all of whom are friends with each other and who want to get along...but two of whom have ties and loyalties to somewhat more hardline people in an era where the hardliners are on the rise and things are kind of going to shit.

I read "The Summer Tree" as a teenager, and I had an extremely visceral negative reaction to it. I did finish the trilogy, but I was so disgusted with the author I actually downloaded PDF rips from a sketchy pirate site (and probably infected my PC with loads of trojans) rather than give him any more money. While I think an adult me would deal with the heavy themes in the story far better than the volatile little **** I was back then could, for me the damage is already done. It was the wrong book at precisely the wrong time, and I don't think I've finished a traditional fantasy novel ever since.

Don't let that deter you from trying, though. There is a tragic grandeur to the way those books are written, at the very least, an element that many Tolkien-influenced authors grasp at and fail to realize in their own work, and I wouldn't still carry the memory of my reading them all those years ago if it hadn't been well-executed.

He's one of my favourite authors. Like buy on the first day authors.

It's worth dividing his career into roughly three phases, off the top of my head. Fionavar Tapestry is him doing Tolkien or talking to Tolkien. He helped compile The Silmarillion, so there's a sense, to me, of sneaking Toronto middle class people into Tolkien's very English deep memory. It's all fine, but worth maybe one or two reads and then best to move on.

Which moves to phase two. Tigana and Song for Arbonne. These books are very much set in the Fionavar multiverse, very much follow themes and callbacks to Fionavar: particularly Tigana is a highish magic setting with epic stuff going on that could easily be set in the next bit of the map over from Fionavar. However both are transitional and you see him starting to think about history, and historical fiction. Tigana is a love letter to Italy; Arbonne is sort of the Albigensian crusade (except, spoilers, with a different historical outcome). 

And then onto the bulk of his work: Sailing to Sarantium and everything after. Sailing to Sarantium is magic realist historical fiction, set in a fantasy world that is itself a mosaic-like assemblage of real world history (see what I did there? nevermind ). It's very good. It's sort of a soapy story about power, art-making and sex but also very much about a particular historical moment, the random chaos of life and invites us to think about what we leave behind. Eventually all these big passions, small passions, petty feuds are stripped of context by time and distance, rendered into images on a wall and ultimately forgiven.

It's also a tad self indulgent. And you see all the patterns that will repeat basically forever in all his books since (save one). Most of his books following take place in the Sailing to Sarantium "universe," exploring different moments and facets (The Italian wars - A Brightness Long Ago, Alfred vs the Danes - Last Light of the Sun). Probably the standout of these is, as mentioned upthread, The Lions of Al-Rassan, about El Cid. Has some of the best prose and it moves interestingly between a sort of adventure story and a people living through history story. But again, it sort strengthens the patterns and themes he revisits over and over again. 

Which leads us to the most recent book, which is the third in a sort of unofficial out of order series, all centred on the Italian wars, the fall of Byzantium/Sarantium and various personalities of that era. I don't think the series was particularly planned. It's also probably the weakest of the lot. It's fun, its nicely written, its diverting but it feels very much the same as a lot of his previous works. It's also about swashbuckling Jewish pirates (sort of); but there's a lot of historical baggage that is quite painfully clear. Anyway, I found it quite disappointing. It was also extra indulgent with its callbacks and gracenotes and shoutouts. Hardly an essential book at all. Definitely not one to start with. 

So yeah, read the Sarantium Mosaic (Sailing to Sarantium and Lord of Emperors), the Lions of Al Rassan and Children of Earth and Sky. If you don't like those, you won't like the rest, either.

They're a transition between different phases of his writing.

He started as a ghostwriter helping Christopher Tolkien turn his father's notes into the Silmarillion, then went and wrote a fairly traditional fantasy trilogy.

Then he started moving away from that standard, writing fantasy novels that are heavily flavored by different historical periods than was common in fantasy. These are the transitional books.

Then he moved into what most people consider the essence of his writing--books set in in fantasy allegories of specific historical settings. They're almost historical fiction with the serial numbers filed off and a light touch of fantasy added.

It's worth understanding that The Fionavar Tapestry was written in 1984-1988. It is earlier material than a lot of novels that it might be considered derivative of to someone picking up the books in the last twenty years. The 70s and 80s saw a few pieces of fiction that transported protagonists from modern America to a fantasy world; Kay's version was among the best treatments of that theme. His use of Celtic mythology within a more familiar 'nations fighting a Dark Lord' plot was stunning; unlike lesser fantasy novels, he spent time developing the character of each of his protagonists. I've re-read the trilogy probably 5-6 times and it's been an influential body of work to how I've evaluated other fantasy works since. I wouldn't consider it 'fairly traditional', though it does have multiple themes that have been pursued in other fantasy.

I bounced off of Sailing to Sarantium, and haven't read any of his later works. I've preferred the earlier stuff. Tigana, Lions of Al-Rassan, Song for Arbonne were all extraordinary.

The sense of self-indulgence is what stopped me from really loving Kay's work. (And the melodrama, though I think that's related.) He is a top-notch prose stylist, but he just goes on rather more than he needs to. (Not unlike Tad Williams.) In particular, his narrator commentary and foreshadowing often felt really heavy-handed to me. Like, Al-Rassan has a scene of key characters just looking at each other and thinking portentous thoughts for five and a half pages.

And maybe his later works smooth this out a bit, but the earlier ones have some rather clunky insertions where Kay evidently found an idea he really wanted to write about.

 Spoiler: Show

The example of this that most stands out for me is the Night Walkers in Tigana, where he'd pretty clearly been reading Carlo Ginzburg's Night Battles and thought "These Benandanti guys seem really interesting, I'll write a couple of chapters on them and come up with some tenuous connections to something else in the book."

And sometimes the file numbers are so lightly filed off it seems like he really should have just written historical fiction and saved some time and wordcount.

EDIT: I'd also say his treatment of female characters is ... not great, though (a) the worst of that is in Fionavar, and (b) he's hardly unusual among male fantasy authors in this regard and at least makes an effort.

There's a pretty clear gradient in his early works, from Tigana to The Lions of Al-Rassan, with each successive work having much less magic and generally being closer to it's historical source. The Sarantine Mosaic actually bounces back toward slightly more magic than Al-Rassan, but is even closer to it's historical source*. I'm not familiar enough with the latter stuff to comment (I did read The Last Light of the Sun, but I don't remember it very well...). 

I wouldn't actually put The Fionavar Tapestry on this gradient. Technically, I'm pretty sure it does have more magic than Tigana while being less modeled on a historical source (to the extent of not being modeled on one at all...), but it's very much it's own thing, from before the author found his voice. But then, I bounced off of it, and didn't give it a very thorough reading (standing in the book store...).

Love it or hate it, Fionavar is VERY different from his other stuff, so your opinion of it probably shouldn't affect your opinion of the rest.

It really reminds me more of Stephen R. Donaldson's Thomas Covenant than Lord of the Rings - it's complex and sprawling and uneven and has stuff that's weirdly dark and graphic that sits awkwardly next to the noble high fantasy stuff. The difference is Thomas Covenant was more thematically and tonally consistent - all the stuff that clashed weirdly with the high fantasy was a deliberate subversion - while Fionavar seemed like Kay just having a lot of ideas and cramming them all into one story, like it was all building up while he was working on The Silmarillion and then he had to get them all out in one burst. Not that the clashing tones weren't deliberate, but it was Kay saying, "these things can all exist side by side without contradicting each other". Rather than the high fantasy being a mask and the subversion being genuine, both the high fantasy and subversions in Fionavar were earnest.

On the other hand the language on a paragraph to paragraph level in Fionavar was much more beautiful and poetic than in Thomas Covenant, which is something that Kay refines even further in his other books. And the grab bag of stuff in Fioalmavar means that it's not as repetitive as Thomas Covenant.

Personally I HATED Covenant, and forced myself to slog through it until the end of the first trilogy, when I finally got where Donaldson was going with it, felt like it was a fairly satisfying ending, but wasn't worth the journey for me. Fionavar I found really uneven and it had a lot of moments that I thought were really awesome but also a lot of subplots that I disliked. So I guess in the whole I liked it more? It felt more like an experiment that had it's good points but ultimately failed, whereas Thomas Covenant felt like an experiment that succeeded at hitting what Donaldson was aiming at, I just didn't like what he was aiming at.

I'd argue strongly against the idea that Fionavar is JUST a Tolkien clone, though. It's clear that Kay thought a lot about the meaning of the myths he was drawing on and was trying to do something else through the lens of Tolkien. I think it's worth looking at for academic interest even if it's not ultimately successful (like Covenant and unlike most extruded fantasy product of the 80's and 90's.) It's not the first Kay I'd recommend, though.

On the other hand, I found it really interesting how for the first half or so of his career, each book made the magic more and more subtle. Fionavar was gonzo Dark Lord and Elves and unicorns, Tigana was dueling sorcerers and a although the setting has clear inspirations from Italian history the story is shaped by a powerful spell. After that each book becomes recognizably a time and place in real history with some magic added, and it's less magic each time until The Sarantine Mosaic, where the only magic (IIRC) is a mystical vision that may have been a real supernatural visitation or an ecstatic hallucination. But then the trend reverses and it starts getting more magical again.

Personally my favourites are Tigana and Lions of Al-Rassan, but I'm not sure if that's just because I read the others later in life when I wasn't absorbing books the way i did in high school so I wasn't able to give them my full attention.

I hear a lot of complaints about Last Light of the Sun but I didn't find it any worse than any others. (I like all of them a lot, it's just that Tigana, Lions of Al-Rassan and slightly behind them The Sarantine Mosaic stand out as being really moving. You'll notice those are also three of the first four after Fionavar which might not be coincidence.)
