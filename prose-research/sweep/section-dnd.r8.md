## I. The house guide and what it actually governs

**1. A written house style guide exists, in three versions, inside a bundle; the public copy has not moved since 2019.**
Support: 20 rows, 11 works. v1.04a itself 49; v1.08a itself 50, 241, 242; the 2021 docx 296, 297; DMs Guild tweets 52, 371; Crawford 370; EN World thread 58; Witchlight Dungeoncraft 57; Dungeoncraft v1.9c 363, 365; Critical Hits freelancer 225; Kobold Press 64; Half-Cover and Old Dungeon Master 54; Mearls 2025 (P) 240; the 2025 forum question (P) 383; POCGamer (P) 56; EN World bundle (P) 380.
Versions: v1.04a, ©2013, nine pages, Chicago 16 (49); v1.08a, ©2018 on every footer, eleven pages, Chicago 17, the January 2019 date only in the mirror's filename (50, 241); a Word revision created by Kim Mohan 2016 and last modified by Judy Bauer 2021-07-06 that ships inside the Adventurers League Dungeoncraft Pack of 2023-12-04 (296) and cites merriam-webster.com rather than the printed Collegiate (297). The general rules apply to every D&D product, "whether RPG books, board games, novels" (242). Organized-play authors are told they "should be familiar with the D&D Style Guide" (57) and "Adventures must use the template provided" (363). A freelancer of 2012 was told Chicago is "your main bible" (225). Community writers who said no guide existed (54) were wrong in fact and right about publicity: the guide reached the public on DMs Guild only in late 2018 (58, 52), and a 2025 asker reports the download "hasn't been updated since 2019" (P 383, the asker's own report).
Rule: The archivist's house is a Chicago house. Where this section says "house law" it means v1.08a as amended by the 2021 revision; a rule found only in v1.04a is history.

**2. The guide is a mechanics-of-style document; it says nothing about read-aloud prose or voice, and its one tone paragraph is the high-fantasy gate.**
Support: 6 rows, 3 works. 49, 50 (zero hits for "box" and "aloud" in either PDF); 72, 245, 246; premise check (P) 392.
True wording: "Avoid overusing sidebars." (50, the opening of a section new in 2019); "ring wraiths aren't used for gags" (72, 245; both versions); "Teamwork and friendship are a huge part" (246).
Rule: The archivist takes typography, terminology and grammar from the house and takes voice from nowhere in the house. Tone is gated once: a thing that would seem out of place in high fantasy does not belong; humour and tragedy are admitted while they do not overtake the general tone. The dossier is a record of a company of people, never of a lone knight.

**3. Second person is the house instrument for gender neutrality in rules text, and only in rules text.**
Support: 3 rows, 2 versions of ONE WORK. 60 (v1.04a: "often achieve"), 248 (v1.08a: "prefer to ... whenever possible"); Grouling Cover on second-person immersion is PARTIAL (P 23, p. 107 not pp. 21/85).
Note: the first sweep's row 2, which stated the 2013 wording and the singular-they ban as house law, is CONTRADICTED by v1.08a.
Rule: Instruction addresses "you"; description never does. The dossier is description and has no second person anywhere. (See features 21 and 22 for the read-aloud side of the same rule.)

**4. The pronoun policy moved three times, and the current law is the 2021 revision.**
Support: 15 rows, 3 versions of ONE WORK plus two designer statements. 2013 ban 293, 294; 2019 permission rationed 249 (and P 61), Crawford 370, DMs Guild 371; 2021 unrationed 299, running text 300, generic creatures 301, ambiguity 302, rare "it" 303, canon pronouns 304; devices to avoid 250; gender identity not assumed 247; dialogue exemption 252.
True wording: "Don't use 'they' or 'their' as singular pronouns" (293, v1.04a, superseded); "but don't abuse this liberty" (P 61, v1.08a); "you must ensure that the pronoun's antecedent is clear" (299, 2021); "In rules text, use 'it.'" (300); "A beholder never keeps its promises." (301); "Such exceptions should be rare." (303); "avoid 'he/she' and 's/he' altogether" (250).
Disagreement across versions: 2013 forbids singular they; 2019 allows it as a last resort; 2021 allows it freely with a clear antecedent. Not a disagreement between sources but a dated sequence; cite the year.
Rule: A person of unknown standing takes they and their with an unmistakable antecedent; where two people share a sentence the noun is repeated or the sentence goes plural. Humanoids and other sapient folk of unknown gender take they; beasts, cosmic horrors and things from other planes take it; a sapient creature takes it only as a rare deliberate cruelty. He or she is used sparingly, he/she never, alternation never. A named canonical figure keeps the pronoun canon gives. The archivist does not assume the reader's gender.

**5. Man and woman, gendered suffixes, and god: the 2019 rule and the 2021 reversal.**
Support: 6 rows, 2 versions of ONE WORK. 251 (2019: humans only), 298 (2021: any people), 253 and P 75 (suffixes), 254 (god), 252 (dialogue exemption).
True wording: "Man and woman reserved for describing humans only" (251, v1.08a); "we can write 'dwarf man' or 'gnome woman.'" (298, 2021); "Avoid gender-specific suffixes whenever possible." (P 75); "use 'god' whether the deity is male or female" (254).
Disagreement across versions: the 2019 restriction is reversed in 2021; the 2021 wording is current law.
Rule: Priest, actor, god, whatever the person's sex; priestess and goddess only inside a title the world itself uses. Dwarf woman and gnome man are permitted. In reported speech and first-person narrative a character may break any of this, because a character does not follow editorial style.

**6. Numerals belong to rules text; prose spells its numbers out.**
Support: 7 rows, 3 versions of ONE WORK. 4, 65 (v1.04a); 266, 276 (v1.08a); 267 (percent); 2021 examples still lowercase "hit points" (P 312); Chicago 9.2 named (266).
True wording: "In rules writing, we use numerals more often." (4); "we spell out whole numbers from zero to one hundred" (266); "The two cities are fifty miles apart." (276); "Use the word 'percent,' rather than the symbol %" (267).
Rule: The dossier is prose, not rules: every count is a word (forty households, six mills, three and a half leagues), which is also the dossier's own no-digits law arriving by a second road. Numerals live on the DM page beside game quantities and in tables.

**7. Spelling, punctuation and heading mechanics are fixed: American, Webster's first variant, serial comma, negative contractions, run-in heads with terminal punctuation.**
Support: 15 rows, 3 versions of ONE WORK. 9 (v1.04a, six rules in one row); 259, 260, 261, 262, 263, 264, 265 (v1.08a); 255, 256, 76 (headings, new in 2019); 295 (Vertical Lists renamed Bulleted Lists); emphasis (P 257), sidebars (P 258), inline subhead (P 3, P 69), contractions (P 71).
True wording: "We require the serial comma." (9, 260); "we use 'toward,' not 'towards,'" (259); "non-elf, non-dwarf" (262); "Use a heading for a structural reason, not an aesthetic one" (255); "Use a period, an exclamation point, or a question mark" (256); "Use bold for such emphasis, unless it's in the heading" (P 257).
Rule: American spelling, the serial comma, contractions formed with not admitted (don't, can't, wasn't) and any contraction that can be misread avoided. A run-in heading closes with a period, not a colon. Headings descend without skipping a level and exist for structure. A sidebar that must follow a passage to make sense is not a sidebar. The dossier chooses the period for its run-in heads; the guide permits any terminal mark, so that choice is a house decision within house law.

**8. Cross-references name a title, never a page.**
Support: 4 rows, 2 versions of ONE WORK. 10 (v1.04a); 268, 269, 270 (v1.08a).
True wording: "We avoid page references as much as possible." (10, 270); "Use cross-references judiciously" (268); "We use letters, not numbers, for appendices" (269).
Rule: The dossier points to another entry by its quoted title, to a chapter by its number, to a table by its unquoted title; it never cites a page, because pagination moves between printings.

**9. Game terms have a typography: capitalise the term classes, italicise spells and items as titles, bold a stat-block name once, and bold means a game object.**
Support: 12 rows, 3 versions of ONE WORK plus four observers. 7, 68 (v1.04a); 273, 274, 275 (v1.08a); 306 (2021); Dragon guidelines 184; 2024 capitalisation 80, 82, 367, 43; 2021 creature types (P 305).
True wording: "The italicized terms are treated as titles" (7); "the wall of fire spell produces a wall of fire" (68, 274); "write 'four goblins,' not 'four loud goblins'" (275); "this indicates the term is a game object" (306); "'Cube' is capitalized, which indicates a term in the Rules Glossary" (P 81); "just being used in their normal sense" (43).
Disagreement: none on the rules; the 2024 books capitalise many more mechanics (Hit Points, Bonus Action) than the 2021 internal guide, whose examples still read "hit points" (P 312).
Rule: In the dossier no word wears game typography, because the dossier contains no game object; the moment a term is capitalised or bolded the reader is told it is a rule to be looked up. Game typography belongs on the DM page, where a creature's name is bold at first appearance and nothing sits between the number and the name.

**10. The verb idiom of the rules is a closed list, and its phrasings are canonical.**
Support: 20 rows, 3 versions of ONE WORD-LIST plus four relays. 62, 63, 66, 67, 6 (v1.04a); 277, 278, 279, 280, 281 (P), 282, 283, 284, 285, 287 (v1.08a); 307, 308, 309 (2021); Kobold Press 64; Half-Cover 78; 2024 conditions 82; verb list (P 5).
True wording: "You make a saving throw, and it succeeds or fails" (62); "We have abandoned that practice." (6, 66, 279, 280); "Avoid referring to points of damage" (67, 282); "We often prefer the word 'total'" (280); "proficient in Elvish and Arcana" (284); "This term is a noun, not a verb" (285); "The word 'attack' confuses many of our readers" (307); "call it a stat block" (309); "Beware of using 'human' as a synonym for 'mortal.'" (287).
Rule: For the DM page only: cast a spell, deal and take damage, make a saving throw that succeeds or fails, make an attack that hits or misses, regain hit points, finish a rest, succeed on a check (never merely make one unless both outcomes follow at once), ten fire damage never ten points of it, an extra 1d4 fire damage, proficient in a skill or language and with a tool or weapon, Dungeon Master a noun. For the archivist one transfer holds: human is a people, not a synonym for mortal, and humanoid is a creature type and stays off the civic page.

**11. The word list fixes spellings and plurals; abbreviations are a fixed set; ordering is letter by letter; the 2024 books renamed core terms.**
Support: 8 rows, 4 works. 288, 292 (v1.08a); 310 (2021); 2024 renames 44, 236, 368, 46; 2021 additions (P 311); species (P 369).
True wording: "mithral (not mithril)" (288); "NPC nonplayer character" (292); "We use letter-by-letter alphabetization." (310); "Cast a Spell action -> Magic action" (44); "a monster that has a personal name" (368); "three paragraphs and a table to roll on" (46).
Rule: Dwarves, not dwarfs; staffs; war band; drow, kenku and yuan-ti unchanged in the plural. The dossier's own lists and registers run letter by letter, spaces ignored. Where the world is a D&D world the current word is species, not race, and a named, characterful monster is an NPC.

**12. Time is written in days and years in rules, and in the Realms the week is a tenday, the hour a bell, and the clock never o'clock.**
Support: 6 rows, 2 works (general guide and Realms guide) plus one reader. 286 (v1.08a; P 70 for v1.04a); 353, 354, 355 (Realms guide); readers on WotC's own breaches 381 (and P 77).
True wording: "say a game effect lasts for 7 days, not 1 week" (286); "'mid morning' or 'nigh sunset.'" (353); "'first day,' 'second day,' 'third day,'" (354); "tenday (never week)" (355).
Disagreement: readers note WotC's own hardcovers still print "week" (381), so the Realms rule is aspirational in the publisher's practice.
Rule: The archivist keeps time in the units the world keeps. Durations in rules are days or years, never weeks or months, because the length of a week varies by world. In-world speech gives loose times (mid morning, nigh sunset), bells where a temple has a clock, and counts the days of the tenday; there is no weekend. This is a setting-specific rule and the dossier adopts only its shape: name the local unit and keep it.

**13. The house keeps an in-world lexicon: forms of address, greetings, the trade tongue, and the word for a party.**
Support: 8 rows, 2 works. 289, 290, 291 (v1.08a, Forgotten Realms Terms and Adventuring Parties, both new in 2019); 356, 357, 358, 359 (Realms guide); P 73.
True wording: "goodsir (a form of address, not capped)" (289, 357); "godsforsaken (replaces Godforsaken)" (356); "use 'group,' 'band,' 'company,' or, especially, 'party.'" (291); "Common is little more than a trade" (358); "Well met. The most often used greeting" (359).
Rule: The dossier's address terms are lowercase when they are forms of address (goodsir) and capped when they are titles (Goodman). A polytheistic world says godsforsaken. A company of adventurers is a party, band or company, never a fellowship, because the word carries Tolkien and D&D parties are fractious. The common tongue is a plain trade language and nuance lives in the older regional tongues, which is a licence for the Herald's pools to be plainer than the chronicle line. These are Realms rules; the dossier borrows the method (a short list of house words with their reasons), not the words.

**14. Setting stewardship and content standards: leave the world as found, keep it G sliding to PG, no stereotypes, no real-world religions as evil analogues, and take setting facts from official sources.**
Support: 14 rows, 4 works. Standards 2020: 330, 331, 332, 333, 334; Realms guide: 348, 349, 350, 352, (P 351); IP guide 361, (P 360); general guide 244, 272, (P 271); Dungeoncraft (P 364), (P 382).
True wording: "D&D is, for the most part, a G-rated game." (331); "Don't use profanity." (330); "Don't attach specific traits or attributes to all members" (332); "their evil should be called out as awful and wrong" (333); "(Pack it in. Pack it out.)" (349); "The Forgotten Realms is a hopeful setting." (348); "Keep it PG." (352); "and their stuff is for taking" (361); "Don't rely on Internet searches" (244); "'She is in Elysium.'" (272).
Rule: The dossier never attaches a trait to all members of a people, culture, religion or sexuality; it never draws an evil faith from a living one; it names a villain's wrong as wrong; it keeps sex and gore at PG; it has no profanity. It leaves established places, peoples and gods restorable. Its facts come from the setting's own record, not from a wiki. A character is on a plane and in Elysium. Note the tone split the guide itself records: hopeful Realms, cynical Greyhawk, tragic Dragonlance; a dossier declares which it is.

## II. The publisher's adventure doctrine, in its own documents

**15. The Adventure Design Guide (Perkins and Bilsland) is where the here-and-now, credible-threat and concision principles actually live; they are not in the style guide.**
Support: 12 rows, ONE WORK (the guide) plus two relays. 335, 336, 337, 338, 339, 340, 341, 342, 343, 344, 345; relays Shea 227 and (P) 18, (P) 226; the guide's own Chicago 16 citation (P 346) and its pointer to the format template (P 347).
True wording: "An adventure is about the here and now." (336); "An adventure needs a credible threat" (335); "to ease the burden on the DM" (337); "present information in a logical order" (338); "features, mannerisms, and quirks" (339); "as long as the adventure can still surprise" (340); "The player characters (PCs) are the heroes" (341); "players will feel 'railroaded' by the plot" (342); "An adventure needs to rise above the humdrum" (343); "Don't decide in advance how the adventure should end." (345).
Correction carried from the chair: the first sweep's "house bullets" (P 18) came from Shea's page and are these principles, not the style guide's; the guide itself is now fetched and verified.
Rule: The dossier's entries are about the here and now; a little history sets a thing up and the less of it the better. Information runs in the order a first-time reader needs it: a person is introduced before being named in passing, and nothing important is buried. Text is as concise as the reader's burden allows, and the licence to linger is reserved for people, dressing and treasure. Every named person carries a distinctive feature, mannerism or quirk that can be played. Familiar things (a tavern, a dragon) are admitted if the place can still surprise.

**16. What the publisher prescribes for boxed text today is the Adventurers League template of 2023: very sparingly, only for an important event, never for rooms or normalcy, one or two brief descriptive sentences.**
Support: 14 rows, 3 works (DC Adventure Format v1.3 2023; the 2019 Crawford .dotx; the 2024 DMG reports). 313, 314, 315, 316, 317, 318, 319, 321, 322, 323, 324, 325, 326; 327, 328, 329; 2024 DMG codifies boxed text and doors 208 (and P 88, P 207); Abadia on the Season 8 template 210, 211; NPC block (P 320).
True wording: "Boxed text should be used very sparingly" (313); "no more than one or two brief and descriptive sentences" (314); "only provide a narrative for exciting events" (315); "'Go away!' the puppet snaps." (316, the template's own two-sentence sample plus a spoken tag); "Keywords should be CAPITALIZED and BOLDED the FIRST time" (317); "Dimensions and Terrain." (321); "Use boldface judiciously." (322); "text that a DM is meant to read to the players" (327); "don't make it a sidebar" (329); "You must read both places" (210); "It exists sort of just to exist" (211).
Disagreement: Abadia shows the rigid Season 8 template scattering one door across three headings and a mandated sidebar adding nothing (210, 211); the 2023 template's own margin notes ration the sidebar and the boxed text. The template is the corrective to its predecessor.
Rule: The voiced block, where the dossier has one at all (the Herald's short pools), is one or two sentences of concrete nouns and, at most, one line of speech; it marks an event, never a room in its ordinary state. The substance of a place lives under its headings. Background names its keywords once, in emphasis, and does not bloat. Each part of a document gets a one- or two-sentence overview. An area is described under Dimensions and Terrain, Lighting, Sounds and Smells; emphasis marks creatures and keyed objects only.

**17. The Paizo-era Dungeon and Dragon guidelines (the licensed official magazines, 3.5 era) are the fullest written read-aloud law in the corpus.**
Support: 17 rows, 2 works (Dungeon Submission Guidelines; Dragon Writers' Guidelines). Dungeon: 11, 173, 12, 174, 175, 14, 180, 177, 178, 179 (P 13, P 176 for the template); Dragon: 181, 182, 183, 184, 185, 186, 187.
True wording: "it does not make any reference to the viewer" (11, 173); "should only rarely run more than a few sentences" (12, 175); "avoid including descriptions of any creatures in the room" (174); "an adventure is not a short story" (14, 180); "no more than 5% of the total length" (177); "Outline surprises and plot twists here" (178); "no more than 300 words in length" (179); "we will not publish an article written in the first person" (181); "no more than 200 words long" (182); "Especially avoid passive writing, particularly 'to be' verbs" (183); "failure to present information in D&D styles" (186); "interesting enough to stand alone without their rules element" (187).
Rule: Read-aloud is a bare-bones description of the area that never references the viewer and never presumes an action; creatures are left out because where they stand depends on circumstance; anything longer than a few sentences becomes a handout. Backstory is capped at a twentieth of the whole. The synopsis gives the twists away to the reader up front. No first person outside a character's mouth. Active voice, few to-be verbs. Flavour must stand without its rules. For the dossier: the archivist's entries never address a viewer, never presume a reader's action, keep the living out of the fixed description, and put the surprises in the summary, not in the entry.

**18. The RPGA Living Greyhawk editor's column (2004) set the same law for organized play: no "you", no assigned feelings, no NPC monologue, bare-bones essential, a consistent style so judges can find things, PG-13.**
Support: 6 rows, ONE SOURCE (Craig Heir, two columns). 168, 169, 170, 171, 172 (and P 167 for "you").
True wording: "read aloud text does not run the character" (168); "this is roleplaying not story time" (169); "Get a feel for what is bare-bones essential" (170); "allows judges to quickly find information they need" (171); "keep your writing PG-13" (172); "Avoid the word 'you' like the plague including derivations" (P 167).
Rule: Consistency of form across entries exists so a stranger can find things quickly; that is the dossier's reason for fixed slots. Cut to what is essential by running the thing; too much text constrains, too little strands.

**19. The publisher's own field study (Noonan and Decker, Gen Con 2005): a DM reading aloud gets two sentences; boxed text turns conversation into oration; players miss the load-bearing facts; boxed text had already shrunk.**
Support: 21 rows, ONE WORK (the 2005 column) plus two relays and its 2006 sequel. 157, 400, 670 (two sentences); 158, 401, 672, 673, 854, 855 (conversation and oration); 159, 403 (facts missed); 160 (cold reading); 161, 402, 676 (used to be longer); 162, 675 (ditch it); 671, 860 (the caveat); 674 (the hypothesis); relays Dyson 94, touc 95; (P 163) alternatives; sequel 164, 165 (and P 166).
True wording: "If you're the DM, you get two sentences. Period." (157); "Boxed text replaces that conversation with oration." (158); "basic stuff, like room dimensions, how many doors exit the room" (159); "Not a recipe for compelling delivery." (160); "Boxed text in adventures used to be a lot longer" (161); "ditch the boxed text and use your own words" (162); "this kind of 'research' is pretty qualitative" (671); "In short, they read good, played bad." (165); "Reading a roleplaying book just lets you make an educated guess" (164).
Caveat carried from the source: the authors call their own method "quite possibly bogus" (671, 860); the two-sentence figure is an observation, not a measurement.
Rule: Anything meant to be spoken carries its load-bearing fact in its first two sentences or loses it. The dossier is read, not spoken, so its budget is different, but the reason transfers: a fact that matters sits first, never among the ornaments. Text that reads well can play badly; the dossier is judged at the table, not on the couch.

**20. The designers on their own craft: documentarian not novelist, lawyerly mechanics beside gapped setting, text that must stand on its own, entertain the DM, overwriting as the standing sin.**
Support: 31 rows, 12 works. Mearls 190, 191 (and P 192, P 193, P 194, P 85), 86; Crawford 232, 233, 234, 239; Perkins 17, 198, 199, 200, 201, 203, 205, 197 (and P 195, P 89, P 152); Wyatt 206 (and P 202, P 204); Baur 214, 215, 217, 218, 219, 220 (and P 216); Noonan 221 (and P 222); Baker 223 (and P 224); Winter 188, 189; Greenwood 228, 229, 230, 231; Vallese via Gnome Stew 131 (and P 113, P 130); Gray (P 87).
True wording: "the writer as a documentarian of an imaginary world" (190); "the kind of exposition that can make a novel boring" (191); "It's much more of a lawyerly approach to writing." (P 192); "The text is forced to stand on its own." (232); "no set of rules could reasonably account for every contingency" (233); "are not official rulings; they are advice" (234); "make it entertaining" (198); "Put puns in your adventure." (199); "Somewhere in there is a cautionary tale about overwriting." (197); "the language needs to be direct and clear" (188); "I don't believe it can do both." (189, on teaching versus reference); "Much time lavished on history and background is wasted energy." (P 216); "too much useless backstory" (217); "Save that for sourcebooks." (218); "I didn't want the roles to creep into game text" (221); "perhaps even to a particular prose style" (228); "I'm smelling what the characters are smelling" (229); "Just be sure you understand the rules before you break them" (131).
Disagreement: Perkins wants the reading DM entertained with embedded humour (17, 198, 199); Winter wants flowery phrase and clever wordplay struck (188); the high-fantasy gate (feature 2) arbitrates: humour that would sit in the source properties, never a gag on a wraith. Baur's beginners want everything and veterans want bullets (214): one text cannot serve both densities, which is Winter's teach-or-reference point (189) from the other side.
Rule: The archivist is a documentarian: the props of the world (places, people, creatures, past events) and not a plot. Its entries carry exposition a novel could not bear, and carry it plainly. The setting may leave gaps for the reader's imagination; a rule may not. The written record must stand on its own without its author's intent. Design labels (a monster's tactical role, a slot name) never creep into the world's text. The one standing fault is overwriting; the cure is cutting, and the first draft is never perfect.

## III. The read-aloud craft rules, where official and community converge

**21. Third person, never the viewer; the dissent is one essay that wants second person for engagement.**
Support: 14 rows, 8 works. Merwin 104, 414 (and P 105); Paizo 11, 173; Heir (P 167); Lynch's readers 111; Alexander 106, 378, 661, 662; Dyson 107; BAATAG 109; DM's Workshop dissent 108, 407; compass directions dispute 479.
True wording: "Move from the second-person point of view to the third person" (104); "The bloated corpse of a headless dragon dominates the center" (414); "it does not make any reference to the viewer" (11); "Don't tell them how they're reacting to it." (662); "Write without assumption of perspective" (109); "addressed to them instead of the empty air" (108).
Disagreement, explicit: DM's Workshop (108, 407) offers the reverse shift, third-person objective to second-person subjective, conditionally, for a DM whose players lose focus; every other witness, official and community, forbids the viewer. Compass directions are urged by BAATAG (109) and Designing Dungeons (110) and disputed in a forum exchange where relative placement is preferred and then shown to break when the party enters by another door (479).
Rule: The dossier's subject is always the thing, never the reader: the mill stands, the corpse dominates, the gate is barred. No "you", "your", "you see", "as you enter". Perspective is not assumed; a door is on the east wall, never on the far side.

**22. No assigned feelings, thoughts, actions or movements; the record shows the scene and leaves the reaction to the reader.**
Support: 11 rows, 7 works. Merwin (P 105), 104; Alexander 106, 378, 661, 662; Heir 168; Dyson 107; Gus L 123; Designing Dungeons 110; Paizo 11.
True wording: "declaring the thoughts or feelings or (worse yet!) actions" (106, 661); "read aloud text does not run the character" (168); "predicts the future for the party (upon approaching...)" (107); "Don't tell the players what to feel" (110, a heading).
Rule: The archivist never writes that a visitor is afraid, steps forward, smiles or approaches. It never predicts. Emotion in the dossier belongs to the townsfolk on the record (the miller is bitter), never to the reader.

**23. Describe what can be sensed and nothing that cannot; strike "seems" and "appears" from perception; but an archivist's hedge is a different instrument and is kept for inference.**
Support: 9 rows, 6 works. Merwin 114, 115, 16, 417; Dausuul 441; Alexander's lorebook practice 563, 564; Volo device 523 (and 40, feature 42); Noonan 159.
True wording: "Describe everything the characters can sense, but not what they cannot." (114); "Everything they sense 'appears to be' what it is." (16); "For the characters, perception is reality." (417); "'Appears as' is just instinctive DM pedantry" (441); "It appears that the carach" (564); "which one can only hope are exaggerated" (563).
Disagreement, and it is a category difference not a quarrel: the read-aloud rule bans seems and appears because at the table perception is reality and the hedge telegraphs a trick (115, 417); Alexander's in-world lorebook hedges its source's boasts and marks its inferences with "It appears" because a document written by someone can be wrong (563, 564, 523). The first governs spoken description of what is in front of the party; the second governs a written record.
Rule: For the Herald's spoken pools: no seems, no appears; what is perceived is stated as fact. For the archivist: fact is stated as fact, and a hedge appears only where the record is drawing an inference or reporting a claim it cannot check, and then it is marked plainly (by report, it appears, the ledger claims). A hedge is never used as a trick-telegraph or as filler.

**24. Concrete and specific over generic and evaluative: one telling detail, the filler adjectives purged, three of five senses, the noun and verb carry it.**
Support: 24 rows, 13 works. Merwin 116, 415; Gus L 123, 451; Skerples 122, 144; Conowitch 124; Alexander 125; Brown via Gaming Ballistic 126; Lynch via Miller 119, Lynch 430, 431, 435, 724; Radiating Gnome 439, 440, 444; Dausuul 442; Hartlage 601, 454 (P), 602; Shea 128; dScryb review 129; Hewitt 619.
True wording: "a party comprised solely of elves or florists" (116); "sharp edged obsidian" (123); "Every sentence needs to fight for its life" (122); "keep it simple with a few powerful details" (124); "Try to include three of them" (125); "the telltale sensory detail, rather than the generic abstract trait" (119); "the adjectives and adverbs were not used very well" (430); "the prim farmers wife tells you" (431); "what the PC's EXPERIENCE when they meet them" (435); "In this context detail is bad. It is trivia." (724); "those words are actually our mortal enemies" (439); "a flap of skin flaps under an old woman's arm" (440); "the ramp needs to climb" (444); "Scaliness was pure fluff from the start." (442); "focus on telling details and plot-critical information" (601); "sounds like it came from Ikea instructions" (128); "is a mystery to be unraveled" (451); "gothic atmosphere is clearly set through the daunting presence" (619, of the statue).
Disagreement: Lynch's cure for a generic town is one added modifier before a role noun (431); Radiating Gnome and Dausuul call modifiers the enemy and cut them (439, 442). Both agree on the test: does the word conjure a specific thing. Lynch himself separates specificity from detail and calls detail trivia (724). Shea wants fantastical language over instruction-manual flatness (128); Lux wants the classics' "expressive but functional language" (feature 33); the reconciliation is in the noun choice, not in ornament.
Rule: An idol is not evil; it has a toothy glare or is sharp-edged obsidian. A wife is prim, or a granny, or lusty, and the one word conjures the woman. Every adjective and adverb must earn its place or go; little, big, grand, tall and dark are hunted and replaced. Each entry touches more than sight (a smell, a sound, a texture) and gives one fact that implies a story. The verb is chosen for its exact motion (a causeway climbs, it does not rise). Atmosphere comes from the objects named, never from naming the mood.

**25. No novelist's urge: no soliloquy, no cutscene, no story in the box; an adventure is not a short story and the record is not a novel.**
Support: 9 rows, 6 works. Merwin 117; Heir 169; DM's Workshop 404, 405; Paizo 14, 180; Perkins 200; Baur (P 216); Gearing 759 (the words are meat on a skeleton).
True wording: "Avoid the novelist/dramatist urge" (117); "structuring the narrative like it's a book" (404, on a boxed speech carrying "he says"); "Paraphrase Bruenor's speech as needed" (405, the module's own instruction); "an adventure is not a short story" (14); "write an adventure like a novel - you can't do that" (200).
Rule: No speech longer than a line in any spoken pool; no attribution tags (he says) inside text meant to be spoken; no coronation scene the reader must watch. The archivist writes a record, not a narrative: no scene, no rising action, no witness placed at the event.

**26. Read it aloud to test it; the ear catches what the page hides; cadence is the delivery problem.**
Support: 8 rows, 5 works. Merwin 118, 418; Hartlage 154, 453, 667; Radiating Gnome 445; Kobold guide via Shea 227; DM's Workshop (P 156).
True wording: "Read your boxed text aloud to check readability" (118); "funny-sounding phrases, unintended alliteration" (418); "their voice takes a different tone and cadence" (154); "which will resonate in the listener's ear" (445); "Small beats large. Keep it brief. Be specific. Read aloud." (227).
Disagreement, explicit: Merwin lists unintended alliteration as a fault (418); Radiating Gnome keeps "craggy causeway" on purpose for the ear (445). The split is intent: accidental sound is a fault, chosen sound is a device.
Rule: Every pool line is read aloud before it is kept; tongue-twisters and accidental alliteration go. The archivist's line is also read aloud, because an entry that cannot be spoken cleanly is not plain.

**27. Length: every witness wants it short and no two agree on the number; the official documents give one or two sentences (2023) and two sentences (2005); the four-sentence, under-one-hundred-word figure is Merwin's alone.**
Support: 22 rows, 15 works. Official: AL template 314 (one or two); Noonan 157, 400, 670 (two); Paizo 12, 175 (a few); Perkins (P 152, "tight and short"). Community: Merwin 97, 15, 416 (four, under one hundred); Revivify 98 (three lines, never five); el-remmen 96, 477 (three to five); GM Lent 476 (two, recalled); Shea 99 (under two hundred fifty words, cut a quarter); DM's Workshop 100 (a paragraph break is too long; P 406); Gus L (P 102, five or six); Marks (P 374, a paragraph or so); mellonbread 379, 399, 663 (three, a recollected and lost WotC post); Lynch 721, 722, 103; Noonan's prior hypothesis 674 (a paragraph); Merwin's own account of paragraphs 97.
True wording: "no more than one or two brief and descriptive sentences" (314); "Four sentences, and less than 100 words." (97, 416); "typically three lines, and never longer than five" (98); "3 to 5 sentences at most" (96); "a tiny story to tell and less than 250 words" (99); "Read-aloud should never be long" (721); "almost certainly going to be playing with their phones" (103); "should never be longer than three sentences" (379, a recollection; the surviving 2005 source says two).
Flags: the exact "four sentences, under one hundred words" is ONE SOURCE (Merwin's 2019 column, three rows of one page), as the chair noted; it is Merwin's count of his own model opening, held up as the standard. The three-sentence WotC rule is a reader's memory of a deleted post, unverified (379, 663). The official 2023 figure is the shortest in the corpus.
Disagreement, explicit: the ceilings run from one sentence to a paragraph. Ordered by authority: the current official template says one or two sentences; the publisher's 2005 observation says two before attention goes; the licensed magazine said a few; the columnists say three to five; only the blog of one editor allows a paragraph.
Rule: A Herald pool line is one or two sentences; nothing spoken ever passes four, and a paragraph break in a spoken block is a fault. The archivist's entry is not spoken and is governed by feature 36 (length to purpose), but its opening sentence carries the load-bearing fact and its paragraphs are short.

**28. Boxed text: what it is for, when it is rationed, who defends it and who rejects it, and the reaction point.**
Support: 30 rows, 13 works. Definitions 397, 645, 632, 19, 327; rationing 313, 425, 598, 473 (and P 90, P 599); defenders 455, 668, 657, 703, 478, 135, 615, 616, 21, 614; rejecters 637, 682, 675, 162, 466, 716 (and P 393, P 146, P 719); reaction point 377, 398, 659, 660 (and P 101); inflexibility 658; shrinkage 161, 402, 676, 223; market and term 135, 372 (P), 375 (P), 134, 376.
True wording: "a prewritten narration designed to be read to the players" (397); "should be immediately shared with any PC entering the area" (632); "Read the boxed text when you're ready to start." (19, the adventure's own cue); "Boxed text is usually not a good thing." (637); "So, OSR adventure writers, get rid of the boxed text." (682); "No one has shown a better way to summarize" (455); "will virtually guarantee that it's not accidentally omitted" (657); "not convinced that we should completely eliminate the boxed text" (703); "The result is inherently less flexible." The Herald's pools inherit the device's law: a pool line never carries the world past the point where the reader could act, is delivered in actionable chunks, and consolidates only what must be conveyed on arrival. **29. The date of boxed text: prewritten description arrives with Lost Tamoachan for the Origins tournament of 1979 and is first boxed in the 1980 reissue; the purpose was a consistent competitive experience.**

## IV. The key: order, format, density

**30. Information order follows what is met first and moves from general to specific: the seen-at-a-glance block leads, the salient thing (an ogre in the open) comes first, the hidden thing does not, and the load-bearing fact is never mid-list.**
Support: 21 rows, 10 works. Alexander 137, 652, 653, 136, 428, 635, 647 (and P 463, P 464, P 651); Designing Dungeons 138; Gus L 139; Skerples 144; Radiating Gnome 446; Hartlage 452, 666, 597; Alexander's prep habit 474; Perkins and Bilsland 338; Doyle via Gaming Ballistic (P 127); the forum's monsters-first players (P 480); Necropraxis 716, 717; Campbell 692.
True wording: "focus on the sequencing of information" (652); "move from the general to the specific" (653); "should talk about the ogre first" (635); "Complete sentences are overrated" (137); "Write a paragraph that describes everything immediately observable" (138); "That seems like a lot of redirection" (446); "the text mentioned the runes alongside other, less important details" (452); "buried in the middle of a paragraph" (474, of details essential to the adventure); "After the bar are the immediately visible items!" (692); "take in the area with a glance" (717).
Disagreement, explicit: Designing Dungeons puts monsters last in the first description and first in later keying (138); Gus L starts with the room's inhabitants (139); one poster's players want the creatures before the furnishings (P 480); Paizo keeps creatures out of the read-aloud altogether (feature 17). The agreed principle above the quarrel is Alexander's: order by what is salient on arrival, not by a template slot.
Rule: An entry opens with what is seen at a glance and holds everything a visitor would know on arrival; it then moves from the general to the specific in the order a reader would ask. A conspicuous thing comes first; a hidden thing is never first. A fact that matters is never buried among ornaments mid-paragraph. The eye is not sent up and down (feet, cliff, causeway, tower); the progression is even.

**31. Against filling the format: a fixed slot order makes writers write where there is nothing to say and lands information out of sequence; the delve format is the extreme, and its one good idea is that everything needed is on the page.**
Support: 16 rows, 6 works. Alexander 136, 427, 634, 649, 648, 633, 656, 708, 709 (and P 636); Abadia 210, 211; Paizo template (P 176); Slavicsek's definition 786; Deathtrap on 3e and 5e signposting 790, 789; Hartlage 607.
True wording: "tends to encourage writers to 'fill the format'" (136, 649); "you might as well write two or three paragraphs about it" (634); "they appeared in that order and no other" (633); "a rigid format ends up having the opposite effect" (648); "Everything you need is on the page." (656); "it artificially isolates the 'encounter'" (709); "a compact series of encounters appropriate for a specific level" (786); "They still signpost traps and treasure with bold italics" (789).
Rule: The dossier's slots are fixed for the reader's sake (feature 18) but empty slots are omitted, never filled: a place with no trade has no trade line. Within a slot the order is the scene's, not the template's. Everything needed to use an entry is in the entry; the reader is not sent elsewhere for a fact that belongs here.

**32. Typographic signposting: bold the interactable and the keyed object, a few per entry, first appearance only; the dissent keeps plain paragraphs as the reference form.**
Support: 15 rows, 10 works. Designing Dungeons 138; Gus L 139; Campbell 690, 692, 467; Necropraxis 715, 717; Alexander 654; BAATAG 140 (dissent); Cygnus 141; AL template 317, 322; style guide 275, 306; Warren D on OSE 781; Prince on T1's split 483; Davis on Campbell (P 142, P 143 on the seven words).
True wording: "3 or 4 bolded words maximum" (139); "the bold title makes it easy to find the information" (654); "I bold the immediately relevant features." (715); "When describing things, I only use the bolded words!" (467, 690); "Plain text paragraphs still need to serve as reference documents" (140); "Room descriptions have important object in bold with parenthesis containing details" (781); "divided between common information and bold text" (483, on T1 splitting public from hidden).
Disagreement, explicit: BAATAG defaults to unformatted paragraphs with no bullets and no bolding, each later paragraph one topic recognisable from its first words (140); every other witness bolds. Necropraxis bolds inside running prose and keeps the prose (715); Campbell strips the prose to the bolded nouns (690); the AL template bolds creatures and keyed objects only, judiciously (322).
Rule: The dossier's entry is prose, and emphasis marks at most three or four things in it: the objects a reader will ask about, the persons of record, the keyed thing. Each emphasised word points to a following paragraph that explains it. Emphasis is never decoration and never a second heading. What is hidden is set apart from what is common knowledge.

**33. Economy: terse, evocative, functional; cut mercilessly; every word must be important; the dissent says ultra-minimalism strips voice and an outline lacks poetry.**
Support: 30 rows, 16 works. Lynch 394, 638, 639, 461, 462, 686, 687, 737 (and P 120, P 151); the second reviewer's scale 641 (P), 642; Skerples 144, 122; Campbell 468, 691; Radiating Gnome 443; Necropraxis 718, 711, 712, 713 (and P 714); Lux 693, 694, 695, 696, 698, 699, 720; Davis 701, 704; Laws 112; BAATAG 133; Dragon 183; Bittman via Gaming Ballistic 148; Arnold K 150; Cook on subtext 149; Mentzer via Peterson 565, 566, 570; Gearing 756; Prince 465, 466; Vallese via Gnome Stew 131.
True wording: "Terse writing style" (394, 638); "Evocative atmosphere." (639); "Short, evocative writing communicates the feel of a place" (465); "Sprawl is the enemy of utility" (144); "Any word that is not important, is not used." (468); "everything I need to run the encounter successfully in seven words" (691); "That's 23 words verses the originals 81." (443); "cut down by more than half" (718); "terse simplicity, expressive but functional language, play-oriented presentation" (693); "it also matters how it gets told" (694); "Ultra-Minimalism is stripping games of their flavour, depth and inner complexity" (695); "it doesn't replace style" (698); "Two, it lacks poetry." (P 714); "a skillful application of the principle of economy" (701); "obviously padded" (565); "extremely repetitive" (566); "It's better unsaid." (149); "and no more" (148); "It's better to have a small, excellent dungeon" (150); "Austere. Uncurtained windows let the gloomy light" (756).
Disagreement, explicit: the ultra-minimalist key (Campbell's seven words, bolded nouns after a bar) is answered by Necropraxis (usable, but it lacks poetry: P 714) and by Lux (the classics were terse and had a voice; stripping to the mechanism strips the flavour: 693, 695, 696). Davis names the middle way: economy skillfully applied (701). Gearing's one-word opening sentence (756) shows economy and voice in the same line. Cook adds the one exception: rules text takes no subtext, adventure text leaves the solution unsaid (149).
Rule: The archivist's sentence is short and every word in it is load-bearing; a page becomes a paragraph, a paragraph a sentence, a sentence a table where a table serves. But the record keeps a voice: a chosen adjective, a one-word opening (Austere.), a dry aside in the chronicle line. Economy is a principle applied with skill, not a word count. The archivist states the facts of a matter plainly and leaves conclusions to the reader.

**34. Padding and the wall of text: the custom of long description makes authors write with nothing to add, and the important bits are lost in the length.**
Support: 17 rows, 9 works. Hartlage 421, 422, 595, 597, 743; Lynch 536, 537, 535 (and P 437); Prince 484, 528, 466; Alexander 629, 650, 426, 627; Necropraxis 711, 704 (Davis); Idle Cartulary 507, 508; Wikipedia relay on the 2014 spell chapter 35; Mentzer 565.
True wording: "pads it like a school paper stretched to an assigned length" (421, 595, 743); "write something even when they have little to add" (422); "An avalanche of description does more than squander page count" (597); "Three sentences are used when one will do" (536); "The entries are so long that the important bits are lost" (537); "Room descriptions in thick, indigestible paragraphs" (484); "a staple at TSR for the better part of a decade" (629, the Gygax wall of text); "Areas are described in lengthy, proper english prose." (711); "being overwritten is a signature of 5th edition module writing" (507); "the location keys in published adventures are almost universally terrible" (426); "an unrelenting wall of text" (35, Wikipedia relaying Abbott on the 2014 spell chapter).
Rule: No sentence exists because the slot looked empty. An entry is not stretched to a customary length; where there is one good middle sentence, that sentence is the entry. Long undifferentiated paragraphs are broken by the entry's slots and its emphasis, never by more words.

**35. Backstory is rationed: the here and now first, history only where it connects to present action and can be learned, a twentieth of the whole, and anything longer belongs in a sourcebook or an appendix.**
Support: 16 rows, 10 works. Paizo 14, 180, 177; Baur 217, 218 (and P 216); Perkins and Bilsland 336; AL template 318; Lynch 395, 640, 643; Alexander's GM tag 655; Alexander's lorebooks 549, 550; Nelson's iceberg 411; Merwin's appendix 588; Gus L 123 (no room history).
True wording: "no more than 5% of the total length" (177); "too much useless backstory" (217); "Save that for sourcebooks." (218); "An adventure is about the here and now." (336); "doesn't bloat the background with backstory that isn't ultimately important" (318); "I LOATHE the overly long backstory" (395, 643); "I don't care about your epic backstory." (640); "It should be brief, to the point, and infrequently needed." (655); "make the setting richer even if little of it is presented" (411); "not meant to be seen by the players" (588).
Disagreement: Nelson praises Hommlet's households for secrets the players will never meet, the iceberg (411); Lynch and Baur call unmet history waste (395, P 216). The reconciliation is placement: the iceberg lives in the record (the dossier) and not in the entry the DM must run (the DM page), which is Baur's own rule that history belongs in sourcebooks (218).
Rule: The dossier is the sourcebook, so it may hold the iceberg; but each entry leads with the present state and gives history only where the present shows it, briefly, in its own slot, and the ledger of the deep past is the chronicle line's, not the entry's. Nothing on the record is written that a resident could not in principle learn.

**36. Description is sized to a place's purpose in play; a location that will never be entered gets no inventory, a home base gets its buildings and its rumours.**
Support: 12 rows, 6 works. Hartlage 423, 596, 147, 592, 594, 424, 593, 600, 601; Lynch 535, 545, 546; Merwin (P 590).
True wording: "no one needs an item-by-item inventory of her kitchen" (423, 596); "he usually wrote a 1-line note for each room" (147, 592, Gygax's own Castle Greyhawk key); "set the pattern for virtually every adventure to follow" (594, G1's generous descriptions); "The homes of notable NPCs get pages of room descriptions." (424); "a very skeletal framework" (593, what referees told Judges Guild they wanted); "the minimum amount of information needed to run the dungeon" (600); "Rooms are described in detail for the DM" (535, detail that has no impact); "including the garrison, inn, jeweler, church and general store" (545); "We also get a decent sized rumor table" (546).
Rule: The length of an entry is set by what will happen there. A place people will visit and use (the inn, the garrison, the shrine) is described; a place they will not enter is named and left. A settlement that serves as a home base carries its major buildings and a rumour pool; a waystation carries a line.

**37. Present tense; drop the future "will"; active voice; replace to-be with the verb of the thing.**
Support: 6 rows, 6 works. Laws 112; BAATAG 133; Dragon guidelines 183; Vallese via Lynch (P 130) and via his own page (P 113); Ronny's compiled guide (P 79); Lynch on expletives (P 130).
True wording: "If the agents reach the safe house, Keletny burns the car." (112); "Especially avoid passive writing, particularly 'to be' verbs" (183); "check for 'to be' verbs and replace them with something" (133, more active); "Write in the present tense." (P 79, which goes on to bar the future tense); "After: Its wormlike body is thin and scaly" (P 130, the stacked-modifier reorder).

## V. The place and the record: how official D&D writes a settlement

**38. The gazetteer form: history, politics and society, then the important locales and persons; everything around the adventures rather than the adventure sites; the failure mode is an encyclopedic recitation.**

Rule: A settlement dossier is a gazetteer: it carries the place's history in broad strokes, its politics and society, its calendar with the world's own names for months and days, its laws and taxes and guilds, its neighbourhoods and main streets and seat of rule, its imports, exports and trade routes, its history of conflict, and then its important locales and persons, each with a reason to visit. Every lesser settlement is placed intelligently on the routes. The register is neither casual nor encyclopedic: it is a record with a voice.

**39. The town entry's facts: population and predominant people first, how residents treat strangers, a web of named relationships, residents each with something going on, one sentence per person of record, and what one town thinks of the next.**

Rule: The entry opens with the count of residents and who they mostly are, then how they receive strangers, and by kind of stranger. It names its council, its merchant, its places of rest and worship. It carries a web of stated relationships among its named residents so the place exists apart from any visitor, and each resident of record has something going on. Persons of record get a sentence each: who, where found, what wanted. A rumour pool of about ten, each adding local colour. One to three fantastic features define the place. **43. Names: simple names that imply a strange world beat strange names that imply a familiar one; a list of names with a quest behind each is useless; unnamed persons still have motives.**
