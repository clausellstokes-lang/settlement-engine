[[J. R. R. Tolkien]]'s ''[[The Lord of the Rings]]'' has been translated, with varying degrees of success, [[List of translations of The Lord of the Rings|into dozens of languages]] from the original English. He was critical of some early versions, and made efforts to improve translation by providing a detailed '''"Guide to the Names in ''The Lord of the Rings''"''', alongside an appendix "On Translation" in the book itself.

The complexity of the book, the nature of [[Tolkien's prose style]] with its archaisms, and the many names of characters and places combine to make translation into any language a challenge. A specific difficulty is the elaborate relationship between some of the real and [[Languages constructed by J. R. R. Tolkien|invented languages used in the book]]. [[Westron]], the common speech of Middle-earth, is "[[Pseudotranslation in The Lord of the Rings|translated]]" as modern English; this stands in relation to [[Rohirric]], an archaic language, which is represented by [[Old English]], and the language of Dale, translated as [[Old Norse]]. The three real languages are related. The scholar of literature [[Thomas Honegger]] gives possible solutions for this in French and German, but suggests that the small amount of Old English is probably best left untranslated.

Tolkien, an expert in [[Germanic languages|Germanic]] [[philology]], scrutinized those that were under preparation during his lifetime, and made comments on early translations that reflect both the translation process and his work. To aid translators, and because he was unhappy with the work of early translators such as [[Åke Ohlmarks]] with his Swedish version,<ref>{{harvnb|Carpenter|2023|loc=#229 to Allen & Unwin, 23 February 1961}}; cf. Martin Andersson ''[https://sswftapa.blogspot.com/2007/01/lord-of-errors-or-who-really-killed.html "Lord of the Errors or, Who Really Killed the Witch-King?"]''</ref> Tolkien wrote his "Guide" in 1967; it was released publicly in 1975 in ''[[A Tolkien Compass]]'', and again, retranscribed, in the 2005 book ''[[The Lord of the Rings: A Reader's Companion]]''.

Linguists have examined translations into several languages, noting the specific difficulties in each case, and the choices and errors that translators have made. Later versions in each language have benefited from the choice of adapting and correcting early versions, or of starting afresh. For instance, [[Margaret Carroux]]'s careful German version was criticised by [[Wolfgang Krege]], who made a new translation, for using a similar linguistic style for the speech of both elves and hobbits, despite the marked differences in the original, while Luis Domènech rendered the working class hobbits' non-standard English into accurate but standard Spanish. Translations have sometimes adopted a domesticating approach: for instance, the first Russian version to be printed substitutes secret police and armed escort for Tolkien's far gentler English policemen.

== Context ==
{{further|J. R. R. Tolkien|The Lord of the Rings {{!}} ''The Lord of the Rings''}}

[[J. R. R. Tolkien]] (1892–1973) was an English [[Catholic Church|Roman Catholic]] writer, poet, [[philology|philologist]], and academic, best known as the author of the [[high fantasy]] works ''[[The Hobbit]]'' and ''[[The Lord of the Rings]]''.<ref>{{cite book |last=Carpenter |first=Humphrey |author-link=Humphrey Carpenter |title=J. R. R. Tolkien: A Biography |title-link=J. R. R. Tolkien: A Biography |year=1978 |orig-year=1977 |publisher=[[Unwin Paperbacks]] |isbn=978-0-04928-039-7 |pages=111, 200, 266 and throughout}}</ref>

''The Lord of the Rings'' was published in 1954–1955; it was awarded the [[International Fantasy Award]] in 1957. The publication of the [[Ace Books]] and [[Ballantine Books|Ballantine]] paperbacks in the United States helped it to become immensely popular with a new generation in the 1960s. It has remained so ever since, judged by both sales and reader surveys.<ref>{{cite news |last=Seiler |first=Andy |date=16 December 2003 |url=https://www.usatoday.com/life/movies/news/2003-12-12-lotr-main_x.htm |title='Rings' comes full circle |newspaper=USA Today |access-date=12 March 2006 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20060212081213/http://www.usatoday.com/life/movies/news/2003-12-12-lotr-main_x.htm |archive-date=12 February 2006 }}</ref>

== Challenges to translation ==

=== Relationships between Tolkien's purportedly translated languages ===
{{further|Pseudotranslation in The Lord of the Rings {{!}} Pseudotranslation in ''The Lord of the Rings''}}

Because ''The Lord of the Rings'' [[Pseudotranslation in The Lord of the Rings|purports to be a translation]] of the fictitious ''[[Red Book of Westmarch]]'', with the English language in the translation representing the [[Westron]] of the original, translators need to imitate the complex interplay between English and non-English (such as [[Elvish languages (Middle-earth)|Elvish]]) nomenclature in the book. An additional difficulty is the presence of proper names in [[Old English]] and [[Old Norse]]. Tolkien chose to use Old English for names and some words of the [[Rohan (Middle-earth)|Rohirrim]], for example, "[[Théoden]]", King of Rohan: his name is simply a transliteration of Old English ''þēoden'', "king". Similarly, he used Old Norse for "external" names of his [[Dwarf (Middle-earth)|Dwarves]], such as "[[Thorin Oakenshield]]": both ''Þorinn'' and ''Eikinskjaldi'' are Dwarf-names from the ''[[Völuspá]]''.<ref name="Shippey 2005 p131"/>

<gallery class=center mode=nolines widths=350 heights=250>
File:Linguistic Map of Middle-Earth.svg|Tolkien accidentally created a linguistic puzzle by using three different [[Pseudotranslation in The Lord of the Rings|pseudo-translated]] Germanic languages for peoples in his story, namely English, [[Old Norse]], and [[Old English]].<ref name="Shippey 2005 p131">{{harvnb|Shippey|2005|pp=131–133}}</ref>
</gallery>

The relation of such names to English, within the [[history of the English language|history of English]], and of the Germanic languages more generally, is intended to reflect the relation of the purported "original" names to Westron. The Tolkien scholar [[Tom Shippey]] states that Tolkien began with the words and names that he wanted, and invented parts of [[Middle-earth]] to resolve the linguistic puzzle he had accidentally created by using different European languages for those of peoples in his legendarium.<ref name="Shippey 2005 p131"/> The relationships cannot easily be replicated in translations of ''The Lord of the Rings''.<ref name="Smith 2006"/>

Honegger notes that while this type of solution works linguistically, it cannot hope to capture cultural aspects. The people of Rohan, the Rohirrim, speak a Mercian dialect of Old English, and their culture is Anglo-Saxon, despite Tolkien's denial of this in "On Translation".{{sfn|Honegger|2011b|p=14}}<ref name="On Translation">{{harvnb|Tolkien|1955|loc=Appendix F2 "On Translation"}}</ref> Medieval Latin does nothing to suggest Mercian Anglo-Saxon culture. Honegger suggests that in consequence, the best answer is probably to leave the Old English names and quoted speech untranslated, noting that Tolkien's "Guide to the Names" seems to concur with this approach.{{sfn|Honegger|2011b|p=14}} He comments, however, that "a bit of linguistic adaptation to the target language" is sometimes feasible. [[Éomer]] could perfectly well, Honegger proposes, shout his greeting to the King in German translation not as "{{lang|ang|Westu, [[Théoden]], hal!}}" but as "{{lang|goh|Sî dû, Thioden, heil!}}" in [[Old High German]].{{sfn|Honegger|2011b|p=14}}

=== Archaism ===

Another difficulty for translators is Tolkien's use of archaism. [[Allan Turner (scholar)|Allan Turner]] gives the example of fronting, where Tolkien puts the object at the start of a sentence rather than after the verb: "Weapons they have laid at your doors". Turner points out that this cannot be done in French, while in German it remains a common construction and doesn't give an archaic effect.<ref name="Smith 2006">{{cite journal |last=Smith |first=Arden Ray |author-link=Arden R. Smith |title=Translating Tolkien: Philological Elements in "The Lord of the Rings" (review) |journal=Tolkien Studies |volume=3 |issue=1 |date=2006 |issn=1547-3163 |doi=10.1353/tks.2006.0031 |pages=228–231}}, citing {{harvnb|Turner|2005|loc="Philology and archaism"}}</ref>

=== Idiolect ===

A specific challenge is [[Gollum]]'s distinctive [[idiolect]], with its simple but ungrammatical syntax and its "schizophrenic" character. His oft-repeated keyword "precious" means both the [[One Ring]] and Gollum himself. His use of pronouns is non-standard: he uses both "I" and "We" to mean himself, and when Gollum talks to his [[alter ego]] Sméagol, "You" also effectively means himself.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Parra López |first=Guillermo |last2=Bartoll Teixidor |first2=Eduard |title=El tesoro lingüístico de Gollum. El uso del idiolecto en la caracterización de la identidad de los personajes de ficción y su traducción para el doblaje |journal=MonTI. Monografías de Traducción e Interpretación |issue=4 |date=2019 |doi=10.6035/MonTI.2019.ne4.12 |pages=343–370 |<!--In English:--> url=https://rua.ua.es/dspace/bitstream/10045/121551/1/MonTI_Sp_4_12_Parra_%26_Bartoll_EN.pdf <!--not redundant to DOI-->|hdl=10234/184489 |hdl-access=free }}</ref> Gollum's incorrect plurals, subject-verb agreement, and verb tenses impact translation.{{sfn|Reinders|2024|p=144}} In Chinese, for example, the trend is to ignore Gollum's excessive pluralisation (as in his "pocketses" or "goblinses").{{sfn|Reinders|2024|p=144}}

== Translations in dialogue with Tolkien ==

{{see also|List of translations of The Lord of the Rings {{!}} List of translations of ''The Lord of the Rings''}}

The first translations of ''The Lord of the Rings'' to be prepared were those in [[Dutch language|Dutch]] (1956–1957, [[Max Schuchart]]) and [[Swedish language|Swedish]] (1959–1960, [[Åke Ohlmarks]]). Both took considerable liberties with their material, apparent already from the rendition of the title, ''In de Ban van de Ring'' "Under the Spell of the Ring" and ''Härskarringen'' "The Ruling Ring", respectively.<ref name="Strömbom 2009"/><!--for the Swedish only-->

In both the Dutch and the Swedish cases, Tolkien objected strongly while the translations were in progress, especially about the adaptation of proper names. Despite lengthy correspondence, Tolkien did not succeed in convincing the Dutch translator of his objections, and was similarly frustrated in the Swedish case.<ref name="Strömbom 2009"/><ref name="Letter 190 (Dutch)">{{harvnb|Carpenter|2023|loc=#190 to [[Rayner Unwin]], 3 July 1956}}</ref>

Schuchart's translation has been revised repeatedly, such as in 1965 and 2003 to correct mistakes; and it was revised in 1997 to modernise its vocabulary. Modernisations included losing much of Schuchart's variation of style to suit different speakers such as Elrond and Aragorn in "[[The Council of Elrond]]". Since Dutch still inverts word order, Elrond's inversions such as "Fruitless did I call the victory of the Last Alliance?" do not give an archaic effect when translated. Instead, an archaic feeling can be conveyed by word choice, such as Schuchart's 1956 {{lang|nl|viel}} for "fell" (meaning "was killed"); the 1997 revision used the plainer {{lang|nl|sneuvelde}}. As another example, Tolkien's archaising "ere" (meaning "before") was the cognate {{lang|nl|eer}} in 1956, but this was replaced in 1997.<ref name="Vink 2006">{{cite journal |last=Vink |first=Renee |title=In de ban van de Ring: Old and New Fashions of a Translation |journal=Lembas Extra <!--Dutch Tolkien Society--> |date=2006 |url=https://www.academia.edu/11614045}}</ref>

=== Swedish (Ohlmarks) 1959–1961 ===

{{main|Translation of The Lord of the Rings into Swedish {{!}} Translation of ''The Lord of the Rings'' into Swedish}}

[[File:ÅkeOhlmarks SvD A4 1938-09-26.jpg|thumb|upright|left|Tolkien and many readers objected to [[Åke Ohlmarks]]'s Swedish translation.<ref name="Strömbom 2009"/>]]

[[Åke Ohlmarks]] was a prolific translator, who had produced Swedish versions of [[William Shakespeare|Shakespeare]], [[Dante Alighieri|Dante]] and the [[Qur'an]], as well as works in Old Norse.<ref name="Beregond 2011">Beregond (Anders Stenström), "Tolkien in Swedish Translation: From ''Hompen'' to ''Ringarnas herre''", in {{harvnb|Honegger|2011a|pp=49–63}}</ref> His experience with Old Norse recommended him to the publisher [[Rabén & Sjögren|Gebers Förlag]], and enabled him to find suitable old Swedish words for some of Tolkien's terms: the Tolkien scholar Beregond praises his choices of {{lang|sv|alv}} for "elf", and of {{lang|sv|väströna}} (modelled on {{lang|sv|norröna}}, North Sea coast people of the heroic age) for "Westron". On the other hand, Beregond notes, Ohlmarks failed to use {{lang|sv|harg}} for the [[cognate]] "harrow" in [[Dunharrow]], or {{lang|sv|skog}} for "shaw" in [[Trollshaws]], things that "should have been obvious".<ref name="Beregond 2011"/>

A much more widespread issue was Ohlmarks's "propensity for embroidering every description". Thus, Beregond notes, Tolkien calls Bilbo's wealth "a legend"; Ohlmarks went beyond Tolkien's text to assert that "his travels are {{lang|sv|sägonomsusade}} ('legendary') as well."<ref name="Beregond 2011"/>

Tolkien intensely disliked Ohlmarks' translation of ''The Lord of the Rings'' (which Ohlmarks named ''Härskarringen'', 'The Ruling Ring'), more so even than Schuchart's Dutch translation.<ref name="Strömbom 2009"/<ref name="Letter 228 (Swedish)">{{harvnb|Carpenter|2023|loc=#228 to Allen & Unwin, 24 January 1961}}</ref><ref name="Letter 229 (Swedish)">{{harvnb|Carpenter|2023|loc=#229 to Allen & Unwin, 23 February 1961}}</ref> Ohlmarks' translation remained the only one available in Swedish for forty years, and until his death in 1984, Ohlmarks remained impervious to the numerous complaints and calls for revision from readers.<ref name="Strömbom 2009">{{cite journal |last=Strömbom |first=Charlotte |url=http://vetsaga.se/?p=50 |title=God åkermark eller fet och fruktbar mylla? – Om Erik Anderssons och Åke Ohlmarks översättningar av J.R.R. Tolkiens ''The Lord of the Rings'' |language=sv |trans-title=Good arable land or fertile and fruitful soil? – On Erik Andersson's and Åke Ohlmarks' translations of J.R.R. Tolkiens ''The Lord of the Rings'' |date=29 January 2009 |journal=Vetsaga |issn=1654-0786 |archive-date=19 March 2014 |access-date=26 May 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140319233926/http://vetsaga.se/?p=50 |url-status=dead }}</ref>

[[File:Usnea cavernosa - Flickr - pellaea.jpg|thumb|upright=0.6|Ohlmarks rendered "Treebeard" as the Swedish for "Lichenbeard".<ref name="Strömbom 2009"/> [[Beard lichen]] illustrated]]

After ''[[The Silmarillion]]'' was published in 1977, [[Christopher Tolkien]] consented to a Swedish translation only on condition that Ohlmarks have nothing to do with it. After a fire at his home in 1982, Ohlmarks incoherently charged Tolkien fans with arson, and subsequently published the book {{lang|sv|Tolkien och den svarta magin}} ("Tolkien and Black Magic"), attempting to connect Tolkien with [[black magic]] and [[Nazism]].<ref>{{cite book |last=Ohlmarks |first=Åke |author-link=Åke Ohlmarks |title=Tolkien och den svarta magin |year=1982 |publisher=Sjöstrand |isbn=978-91-7574-053-9}}</ref>

Ohlmarks' translation was superseded only in 2005, by a plainer and more concise translation by Erik Andersson, written in reaction to it.<ref name="Löfvendahl SvD 2003"/> Where Ohlmarks had rendered Rivendell as {{lang|sv|Vattnadal}} ("Waterdale"), or Treebeard as {{lang|sv|Lavskägge}} ("Lichenbeard"), Andersson used {{lang|sv|Riftedal}} and {{lang|sv|Trädskägge}}, far closer in meaning to Tolkien. Andersson cuts down Ohlmarks's name for Mount Doom, ''Domedagsberget'' ("Judgement Day Mountain") to the simpler and more direct translation {{lang|sv|Domberget}}.<ref name="Strömbom 2009"/> The poems in Andersson's version were translated by Lotta Olsson.<ref name="Löfvendahl SvD 2003"/> She was bemused by the demands of young Swedish Tolkien fans to keep the names as they were (in Ohlmarks's version) – or else to use the original English names. Andersson rather agreed, but noted that names like "Old Forest" plainly needed to be translated, and that Tolkien had explicitly said so.{{sfn|Andersson|2015|loc="1 juli 2003"}}

The work was retitled {{lang|sv|Ringarnas herre}}, "Lord of the Rings", making it, like the text, closer to the English original.<ref name="Löfvendahl SvD 2003">{{cite news |last=Löfvendahl |first=Bo |title=Vattnadal byter namn i ny översättning |url=http://www.svd.se/vattnadal-byter-namn-i-ny-oversattning-5bvB |newspaper=[[Svenska Dagbladet]] |access-date=7 September 2016 |language=sv |trans-title=Waterdale changes name in new translation |date=30 December 2003}}</ref>

== Tolkien's "Guide to the Names in ''The Lord of the Rings''" ==

Frustrated by his experience with Schuchart's Dutch and [[Translation of The Lord of the Rings into Swedish|Ohlmarks's Swedish]] translations, Tolkien asked that<ref name="Letter 204">{{harvnb|Carpenter|2023|loc=#204 to [[Rayner Unwin]], 7 December 1957}}</ref>

{{blockquote|when any further translations are negotiated, [...] I should be consulted at an early stage. [...] After all, I charge nothing, and can save a translator a good deal of time and puzzling; and if consulted at an early stage my remarks will appear far less in the light of peevish criticisms.|Letter 204 to [[Rayner Unwin]], 7 December 1957}}

With a view to the planned Danish translation, Tolkien decided to take action in order to avoid similar disappointments in the future. On 2 January 1967, he wrote to Otto B. Lindhardt, of the Danish publisher [[Gyldendal (Denmark)|Gyldendals Bibliotek]]:<ref>Tolkien-[[Allen & Unwin|George Allen & Unwin]] archive, [[HarperCollins]], cited after {{harvnb|Hammond|Scull|2005|p=751}}</ref>

{{blockquote|I have therefore recently been engaged in making, and have nearly completed, a commentary on the names in this story, with explanations and suggestions for the use of a translator, having especially in mind Danish and German.|Tolkien-[[Allen & Unwin|George Allen & Unwin]] archive, [[HarperCollins]], cited after Hammond and Scull 2005}}

Photocopies were sent to translators of ''The Lord of the Rings'' by Allen & Unwin from 1967. The first translations to profit from the guideline, and the only ones that did so to appear in Tolkien's lifetime, were those into Danish ([[Ida Nyrop Ludvigsen]]) and German (Margaret Carroux); both appeared in 1972. After Tolkien's death, it was published as "Guide to the Names in ''The Lord of the Rings''", edited by [[Christopher Tolkien]] in [[Jared Lobdell]]'s 1975 ''[[A Tolkien Compass]]''.{{sfn|Lobdell|1975|pp=153–201}} In 2005, [[Wayne G. Hammond]] and [[Christina Scull]] newly transcribed and slightly edited Tolkien's typescript; they re-published it under the title of ''Nomenclature of The Lord of the Rings'' in their book ''[[The Lord of the Rings: A Reader's Companion]]''.<ref name="Hammond Scull 2005 Nomenclature">{{cite book |last1=Hammond |first1=Wayne G. |author-link=Wayne G. Hammond |last2=Scull |first2=Christina |author2-link=Christina Scull |chapter=Nomenclature of The Lord of the Rings |title=The Lord of the Rings: A Reader's Companion |title-link=The Lord of the Rings: A Reader's Companion |year=2005 |publisher=[[Houghton Mifflin Harcourt|Houghton Mifflin]] |isbn=978-0-00-720907-1 |pages=750–782}}</ref>

Tolkien uses the abbreviations ''CS'' for "[[Westron|Common Speech]], in original text represented by English", and ''LT'' for the target language of the translation. His approach is the prescription that if in doubt, a proper name should not be altered but left as it appears in the English original:{{sfn|Lobdell|1975|pp=155–157}}

{{blockquote|''All names'' not in the following list should be left ''entirely'' unchanged in any language used in translation (LT), except that inflexional ''s, es'' should be rendered according to the grammar of the LT.{{sfn|Lobdell|1975|pp=155–157}} }}

The names in English form, such as ''[[Dead Marshes]]'', should in Tolkien's view be translated straightforwardly, while the names in [[Elvish languages (Middle-earth)|Elvish]] should be left unchanged. The difficult cases are those names where{{sfn|Lobdell|1975|pp=155–157}}

{{blockquote|the author [Tolkien], acting as translator of Elvish names already devised and used in this book or elsewhere, has taken pains to produce a CS name that is both a translation and also (to English ears) a [[phonaesthetics|euphonious]] name of familiar English style, even if it does not actually occur in England.{{sfn|Lobdell|1975|pp=155–157}} }}

Examples are ''[[Rivendell]]'', the translation of [[Sindarin]] ''Imladris'' "Glen of the Cleft",{{sfn|Lobdell|1975|pp=155–157}} and ''[[Westernesse]]'', the translation of ''[[Númenor]]''.{{sfn|Lobdell|1975|p=195}} The list gives suggestions for some "old, obsolescent, or dialectal words in the Scandinavian and German languages".{{sfn|Lobdell|1975|pp=155–157}}

== Translations in dialogue with Tolkien and his "Guide to Names" ==

=== Italian (Alliata) 1967 ===

The first Italian version, initially of ''The Fellowship of the Ring'' only, by [[Vittoria Alliata di Villafranca]], aged only 17 at the time, was brought out by Mario Ubaldini's ''Edizione Astrolabio'' in 1967. She studiously followed Tolkien's ''Guide'', and chose to begin with the appendices, so as to gain a deeper understanding of the book. As for her approach to translating names, she translated (rather than renamed or transliterated) [[Lobelia Sackville-Baggins|Sackville-Baggins]] as {{lang|it|Borsi-Sacconi}} ("Bags-Sacks"). For terms that seemed "exotic", she "adopted Greek or even Arabic etymologies; if it had to be familiar or evocative, Latin or Italian etymologies: always, however, [I sought to create] Italian origins that were plausible".<ref name="Raffi 2022"/> Alliata stated that Ubaldini sent the first pages of her translation to Tolkien.<ref name="Raffi 2022"/> Tolkien seems to have asked for an opinion on the sample, as he wrote in a letter that "someone ... whose opinion he respects" had praised the translation.<ref name="Raffi 2022"/><ref>{{cite book |last1=Scull |first1=Christina |author1-link=Christina Scull |last2=Hammond |first2=Wayne G. |author2-link=Wayne G. Hammond |year=2006 |title=[[The J. R. R. Tolkien Companion and Guide|The J. R. R. Tolkien Companion and Guide: Chronology]] |publisher=HarperCollins |page=718}}</ref> 
<!--The volume had a drawing of a flying dragon by R. Conti for its cover, rather than [[J. R. R. Tolkien's artwork|artwork by Tolkien]] or [[Pauline Baynes]]. It included two of [[Tolkien's maps]], omitting the third one covering [[Gondor]], Mordor, and Rohan.-->The translation lost some of the "refined style" of Tolkien's writing, but it was largely "accurate and faithful" to the original.<ref name="Raffi 2022"/> The attempt to market it was unsuccessful, and the volume sold only some hundreds of copies, leading Ubaldini to abandon the project. The other two volumes of Alliata's translation were eventually brought out by other publishers.<ref name="Raffi 2022"/><ref name="Savelli 2020">{{cite journal |last=Savelli |first=Marcantonio |title=Translating Tolkien. The thin line between translation and misrepresentation. An Italian case-study |journal=[[Journal of Tolkien Research]] |volume=11 |issue=1 |date=2020 |at=article 3 |url=https://scholar.valpo.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1198&context=journaloftolkienresearch}}</ref>

A second Italian translation, made by Ottavio Fatica, was published by ''Bompiani'' in 2019 to 2020. Fatica had access to Alliata's text; she became offended by his remarks about her translation, and a legal dispute followed. The Tolkien scholar Roberto Arduini commended Fatica's version, stating that it cast "a generous, exuberant, playful spell". The literary critic Cesare Catà on the other hand compared it unfavourably to Alliata's version, saying that it was dry and humdrum, to be categorised not as epic but as Young Adult fiction.<ref name="Raffi 2022"/>

Marcantonio Savelli identifies two "recurring trends" in Fatica's version, namely an unjustifiably "low and strongly colloquial tone" not reflecting the original, and the equally unjustifiable use (in his view) of modern technical terms. Among numerous examples that he analyses, Savelli objects, for instance, to the phrase {{lang|it|organizzate attività di sussistenza}} ("organized subsistence activities"), which in his view is far too modern and technical for the context:<ref name="Savelli 2020"/> The Italian Association for Tolkien Studies comments that Fatica's version has triggered years of debate between supporters of the two versions, but that this has had the merit of getting people in Italy to think about Tolkien's style of writing.<ref>{{cite web |title=Tre anni con Fatica… |url=https://www.jrrtolkien.it/2023/02/04/tre-anni-con-fatica-lost-in-translation/ |publisher=Italian Association for Tolkien Studies |access-date=12 May 2024 |date=4 February 2023}}</ref>

Savelli comments that ''ben conservato'' is an over-literal rendering into an Italian phrase that never refers to a person. He is especially critical Fatica's use of "the adjective ''pittati''" (a plural form, roughly meaning "painted"). In his view, this "grammatically incorrect colloquial term ... deserves to be highlighted as one of the most cacophonous mistakes in the entire translation. It is a term that a reasonably well-educated Italian person would avoid even in spoken language, let alone in a written text of any nature."<ref name="Savelli 2020"/> As for Fatica's ''vecchio intronizzato'', Savelli writes that ''vecchio'' is disrespectful where a term of praise is required by the context, while ''intronizzato'' is both technical and obscure, and wholly unsuitable for the poetic context.<ref name="Savelli 2020"/>

Francesca Raffi writes that the two Italian translations, made many years apart, differ because of the changed sociocultural context, with changed conceptions of Tolkien's writing and of how visible a translator ought to be. These give the translated text "a new image, new meanings, and a new reception."<ref name="Raffi 2022">{{cite journal |last=Raffi |first=Francesca |title=Paratexts and retranslation |journal=Trans-kom |volume=15 |issue=1 |date=2022 |pages=44–62 |url=http://www.trans-kom.eu/bd15nr01/trans-kom_15_01_03_Raffi_Paratexts.20220705.pdf}}</ref>

Savelli concludes that Fatica may have been attempting to transform Tolkien's text into something else to make it "more 'digestible' by detractors of the Fantasy genre" or of Tolkien's "'unfashionable' reactionary mentality", so as to create "a modernized Tolkien" to replace the "'medieval' one".<ref name="Savelli 2020"/>

=== German (Carroux) 1969–1970 ===

{{further|Margaret Carroux#Translating Tolkien}}

[[File:Hobbit holes reflected in water.jpg|thumb|upright=1.35|"[[The Shire]]" proved awkward to translate into German, as the obvious historic word [[Gau (country subdivision)|''Gau'']] had, as Tolkien suspected, become associated with [[Reichsgau|its usage under the Nazis]]. Carroux invented a compound word instead.<ref name="Stopfel 2005"/> ]]

Tolkien's "Guide to the Names in The Lord of the Rings", gained him a larger influence on translations into other [[Germanic languages]], namely [[Danish language|Danish]] and [[German language|German]]. The [[Frankfurt]]-based [[Margaret Carroux]] qualified for the German version published by Klett-Cotta on the basis of her translation of Tolkien's short story "[[Leaf by Niggle]]", that she had made solely to give him a sample of her work. In her preparation for ''The Lord of the Rings'' ({{lang|de|Der Herr der Ringe}}), unlike Schluchart and Ohlmarks, Carroux even visited Tolkien in Oxford in December 1967 with a suitcase full of his published works and questions about them. Tolkien and his wife both had a cold at the time, resulting in a "chilly" and inhospitable meeting, with Tolkien "harsh", "taciturn" and "severely ill".<ref name="Stopfel 2005"/><ref>{{Cite journal |last=Maletzke |first=Elsemarie |date=13 September 1977 |title=Gegen die 'Lektoratseinheitssoße': Schon 87 Bücher übersetzt - von Tolkien bis zur Sagan |language=de |trans-title=Against 'editing uniformity sauce': 87 books already translated - from Tolkien to Sagan |journal=[[Börsenblatt|Börsenblatt für den deutschen Buchhandel]] |volume=33 |issue=73 |pages=65–67}}</ref> Later correspondence with Carroux was much more encouraging; Tolkien was delighted with Carroux's work, with the sole exception of [[Poetry in The Lord of the Rings|the poems and songs]]. Carroux had had them translated by the poet Ebba-Margareta von Freymann, but was unsatisfied by the result.<ref name="Stopfel 2005"/>

On several instances Carroux departed from the literal, e.g. for the ''[[Shire (Middle-earth)|Shire]]''. Tolkien endorsed the {{lang|nl|Gouw}} of the Dutch version and remarked that German [[Gau (country subdivision)|''Gau'']] "seems to me suitable in Ger., unless [[Reichsgau|its recent use]] in regional reorganization under [[Adolf Hitler|Hitler]] has spoilt this very old word." Carroux decided that this was indeed the case, and opted for the more artificial compound {{lang|de|Auenland}} "meadow-land" instead.<ref name="Stopfel 2005"/> The Tolkien scholar Susanne Stopfel describes the translation as a whole as treating its source with "enormous respect".<ref name="Stopfel 2005"/>

In 2000, Klett-Cotta published a new translation by [[Wolfgang Krege]], to accompany Carroux's. It focuses more on the differences in [[Tolkien's prose style|linguistic style that Tolkien employed]] to set apart the more biblical prose and the high style of elvish and human 'nobility' from the more colloquial 1940s English spoken by the Hobbits, something that Krege thought Carroux's more unified version was lacking; the poems were unchanged. The translation met a mixed reception; critics felt that he took too many liberties in modernising the language of the Hobbits into the linguistic style of late 1990s German. They commented that this subverted the epic style of the narrative, departing from Tolkien. The 2012 edition of Krege has reverted the most controversial of his decisions.<ref name="Stopfel 2005">{{cite journal |last=Stopfel |first=Susanne |title=Traitors and Translators: Three German Versions of 'The Lord of the Rings' |journal=[[Mallorn (journal)|Mallorn]] |issue=43 |year=2005 |pages=11–14 |jstor=45320518}}</ref> The linguist Rainer Nagel contrasts the versions, writing that Krege corrects many errors in Carroux, but "often overshoots its target of modernising" as Krege seeks to "assimilat[e]" the text into a style unsuitable for Tolkien, creating a "translator-centred approach".<ref name="Nagel 2011">Nagel, Rainer. ""The New One Wants to Assimilate the Alien" — Different Interpretations of a Source Text as a Reason For Controversy: The 'Old' and the 'New' German Translation of ''The Lord of the Rings''", in {{harvnb|Honegger|2011a|pp=19–47}}</ref>

=== Icelandic ===

Tolkien did not live to see an Icelandic version of his novel, but in 1973 he told Ungfrú Aðalsteinsdottir that he was delighted she was working on one. He stated that he "had long hoped" for such a thing, as he felt that the language "would fit it better than any other I have any adequate knowledge of".{{sfn|Carpenter|2023|loc=#352 to Ungfrú Aðalsteinsdottir, 4 August 1973}}

== Other translations ==

=== French (Ledoux) 1972–73 ===

The first French translation came out in 1972–73: Francis Ledoux's ''Le Seigneur des Anneaux'', published by Christian Bourgois. Tolkien was largely unknown to French readers at the time: ''Bilbo le Hobbit'' had only appeared in 1969. Even the fantasy genre was poorly understood: ''fantaisie'' translates to "whim or fancy", while ''The Lord of the Rings'' is not ''fantastique'', fantastical, Ferré insists, but ''merveilleux'', full of wonders.<ref name="Ferré 2011"/>

The French Tolkienist [[Vincent Ferré]] and colleagues, noting Tolkien's words in "[[On Translating Beowulf|On Translating ''Beowulf'']]{{-"}} that "therein lies the unrecapturable magic of ancient English verse ...: profound feeling, and poignant vision, filled with the beauty and mortality of the world, are aroused by brief phrases, light touches, short words resounding like harp-strings sharply plucked."<ref name="Ferré 2011"/><ref name="Tolkien 1997">{{cite book |last=Tolkien |first=J. R. R. |author-link=J. R. R. Tolkien |chapter=[[On Translating Beowulf|On Translating ''Beowulf'']] |title=The Monsters and the Critics |date=1997 |publisher=[[HarperCollins]] |isbn=0-261-10263-X |url=https://archive.org/details/monsterscriticso00tolk }}</ref> Ferré comments that ''The Lord of the Rings'' is so much like ''[[Beowulf]]'' that its translation is forbiddingly difficult.<ref name="Ferré 2011">[[Vincent Ferré|Ferré, Vincent]]; Lauzon, Daniel; Riggs, David. "Traduire Tolkien en Français: On the Translation of J.R.R. Tolkien's Works into French and their Reception in France", in {{harvnb|Honegger|2011a|pp=45–67}}. The authors' translation of ''The Lord of the Rings'' was published by Christian Bourgois in 2014–2016; a single-volume edition appeared in 2018 as {{cite book |last=Tolkien |first=John Ronald Reuel |translator-last=Lauzon |translator-first=Daniel |title=Le Seigneur des Anneaux Intégrale |date=2018 |publisher=Pocket |isbn=978-2-266-28626-8 |language=fr}}</ref>

[[File:Gollum s journey commences by Frederic Bennett (detail).jpg|thumb|Francis Ledoux's translation "diminishes the peculiarities" of [[Gollum]]'s distinctive style of speech.<ref name="Ferré 2011"/> Painting of Gollum by Frederic Bennet, 2014]]

In Ferré's view, Ledoux, a skilful translator, had been a little incautious in some of his choices, such as on matters of race. He used the French word ''race'' which is already a pejorative. He chose {{lang|fr|noiraud}}, "a very dark-skinned person", for Tolkien's "black-like" and again for Tolkien's "black chap", but the hobbits concerned had seen only the Black Rider's clothing. Ferré writes that {{lang|fr|un type en noir}}, indicating only that the clothing was black, would have been better. A generation later, French [[Far-right politics|far-right]] readers might, Ferré comments, read a wholly unintended meaning into the text.<ref name="Ferré 2011"/> Ledoux makes another mistake, in Ferré's view, in explicitly mentioning God where [[Christianity in Middle-earth|Tolkien carefully avoids doing that]], as in {{lang|fr|Dieu le bénisse}} for "Bless him".<ref name="Ferré 2011"/>

A special challenge is in translating the carefully-crafted varieties of speech of Tolkien's more idiosyncratic characters. Ledoux renders [[Gollum]]'s<ref name="Ferré 2011"/>

: "'What had it got in its pocketses?' he said. 'It wouldn't say, no precious. Little cheat...'"

as what Ferré calls the prosaic<ref name="Ferré 2011"/>

: {{lang|fr|'Qu'avait-il dans ses poches?' disait-il. 'Je ne pouvais le dire, pas de trésor. Petite tricherie...'}}

Ferré comments that the translation "diminishes the peculiarities" of the speech of both [[Gaffer Gamgee]] and of Gollum; he suggests that Ledoux should have chosen something like (Ferré's emphasis)<ref name="Ferré 2011"/>

: <em>'<strong>Qu'est-ce que ça</strong> avait dans ses poches<strong>s</strong>?' disait-il. '<strong>Ça ne voulait</strong> pas le dire, non, trésor. <strong>Petit tricheur</strong>...'</em>

The choice of French terms for placename elements is especially tricky, as, Ferré remarks, "some names have, besides a purely historical depth, a connection with literary history, and take on additional resonances."<ref name="Ferré 2011"/> Thus, "Brandy Hall" uses the Beowulfian term "hall", a word "full of echoes". Ledoux selects {{lang|fr|château}}, castle, which to Ferré's ear "sounds too '[[Arthurian]]' and 'grand'". Worse, Ledoux uses {{lang|fr|château}} also for "Bucklebury" with {{lang|fr|Châteaubouc}}. Ferré comments that since Tolkien glosses "-bury" as coming from the Old English word ''burg'', a fortified place, Bucklebury could better have been rendered {{lang|fr|Fertébouc}}, using {{lang|fr|Ferté}}, a similarly antique French word for fortified town still used in some French placenames.<ref name="Ferré 2011"/>

=== Russian (multiple authors) 1966 onwards ===
==== Samizdat versions ====

Interest in Russia awoke soon after the publication of ''The Lord of the Rings'' in 1955, long before the first Russian translation.
A first effort at publication was made in the 1960s, but in order to comply with literary censorship in [[Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic|Soviet Russia]], the work was considerably abridged and transformed. The ideological danger of the book was seen in the "hidden allegory 'of the conflict between the individualist West and the totalitarian, Communist East'", while, ironically, Marxist readings in the West conversely identified [[Environmentalism in The Lord of the Rings|Tolkien's anti-industrial ideas]] as presented in the Shire with [[primitive communism]], in a struggle with the evil forces of [[technocracy|technocratic]] [[capitalism]].<ref name="Markova 2004">{{cite journal |last1=Markova |first1=Olga |translator-last=Hooker |translator-first=Mark T. |title=When Philology Becomes Ideology: The Russian Perspective of J.R.R. Tolkien |journal=[[Tolkien Studies]] |date=2004 |volume=1 |pages=163–170|doi=10.1353/tks.2004.0011 |s2cid=51684428 |doi-access=free }}</ref><ref>See also {{cite book |last=Hooker |first=Mark T. |title=Tolkien Through Russian Eyes |publisher=[[Walking Tree Publishers]] |year=2003 |isbn=3-9521424-7-6}}</ref> Russian translations of ''The Lord of the Rings'' circulated secretly as [[samizdat]] and were published only after the [[collapse of the Soviet Union]], but then in great numbers.<ref name="Markova 2004"/> [[Tolkien fandom]] grew especially rapidly during the early 1990s at [[Moscow State University]]. Many unofficial and incomplete translations are in circulation.<ref name="Hooker 2011"/>

=== Hebrew (Livnit) 1977===

[[File:Lilith (John Collier painting).jpg|thumb|upright|The rendering of "Elves" in the Hebrew translation as "Children of Lilith" was changed to the transliteration "Elefs" to avoid the connotations of [[Lilith]], mother of all demons.<ref name="Livnit endnote"/> Painting of ''[[Lilith (painting)|Lilith]]'' (1887) by [[John Collier (painter)|John Collier]] ]]

The first translation of ''The Lord of the Rings'' into Hebrew (שר הטבעות, ''Sar ha-Tabbaot'') was made by [[Canaanism|Canaanite movement]] member Ruth Livnit, aided by the poet Uriel Ofek for [[Tolkien's poetry|the verse]], in 1977. It was revised by Emanuel Lottem using the second English edition, which retained the names of the previous translators. Lottem was described as the editor, as the least costly option for the publishers.<ref name="Orbach 2011"/> Lottem's version replaced Livnit's in bookshops, helping to stoke a heated controversy.<ref name="Orbach 2011"/> Danny Orbach writes that older Tolkien fans like Livnit's "old, traditional, and literary Hebrew" full of mythological, "mostly Biblical" terms. Thus, Livnit translates "horn" as ''[[shofar]]'', a [[synagogue]]'s ceremonial ram's horn, where the ordinary word would be "keren" ({{lang|he|קרן}}). Or again, she uses "ha'arelim" (הערלים), "uncircumcised", for "heathen", again with religious overtones. Such language gave the translation an impressively "high register" which many Israelis found beautiful and even magical.<ref name="Orbach 2011"/>

Lottem, charged with editing Livnit's version, mixed his preferred, rather technical, modern Hebrew with Livnit's "archaic and rich diction", creating an "odd" result. He found and corrected many mistakes, but introduced a much lower style, including slang. Orbach writes that new, mainly teenage readers prefer Lottem, whether because they "cannot appreciate the beauty of Livnit's Hebrew, or ... prefer accuracy to beauty in translation".<ref name="Orbach 2011">Orbach, Danny. "The Israeli Translation Controversy – What About and Where To?", in {{harvnb|Honegger|2011a|pp=49–63}}</ref><ref name="Kfir 2006">{{cite web |last=Kfir |first=Yuval |translator-last=Shoulson |translator-first=Mark |title=Alas! The Aged and Good Translation! |url=http://web.meson.org/lang/alas.php |publisher=Meson |year=2006 |access-date=20 May 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231020153506/http://web.meson.org/lang/alas.php |archive-date=20 October 2023 |url-status=live}}</ref> Another difference between the two versions is the handling of names. Livnit translated "Elves" as "בני לילית" (Bneyi Lilith, i.e. the "Children of [[Lilith]]"). Lottem transcribes it as "Elefs", maintained through Yiddish as "עלף". The change was made because "Bneyi Lilith" recalls the Babylonian-derived Jewish folklore character of Lilith, mother of all demons, an inappropriate context for Tolkien's Elves.<ref name="Livnit endnote">The new version, Editor's endnote.</ref> Livnit did not know of Tolkien's rules for translating names, resulting in many errors. Lottem translated the appendices and followed Tolkien's instructions there. Livnit made no reference to Tolkien's legendarium, and therefore either inaccurately translated or dropped parts of the story relating to events in ''The Silmarillion'' and ''[[The Hobbit]]''. She created major inconsistencies in transcription and in repetitions of similar text within the story; Ofek made similar errors in the verse.<ref name="Orbach 2011"/>

Orbach states that it is necessary when translating an epic to use the mythology of the target language: "a Japanese translation must use Shinto and Samurai terminology (as ... Teji Seta actually did)". Hebrew was extinct for nearly 2000 years except for religious use until the 20th century, and the very young language of modern Hebrew "lacks the richness of its ancestor", making it unsuitable for translating an epic like ''The Lord of the Rings''. In his view, therefore, Livnit was right to choose a high style.<ref name="Orbach 2011"/>

=== Spanish (Domènech) 1977–80 ===

The first Spanish translation was made under the [[pseudonym]] of Luis Domènech (Francisco Porrúa) in 1977–1980. The linguist Sandra Bayona writes that the translation often renders the speech of characters such as [[Working class|working-class]] hobbits in grammatically correct Spanish, where Tolkien used non-standard English. Bayona comments that Tolkien uses styles of speech both to distinguish individual characters and to indicate which group they belong to. He does this, she notes, completely regularly, even if this means repetition.<ref name="Bayona 2011">Bayona, Sandra, "''Begging your pardon, Con el perdón de usted'': Some Socio-linguistic Features in ''The Lord of the Rings''", in {{harvnb|Honegger|2011a|pp=69–90}}.</ref>

In the case of the working-class hobbits, she shows that characters including Ham Gamgee, Sam Gamgee, Cotton, Hob Hayward, farmer Maggot, and Robin Smallburrow all make use of multiple non-standard English forms. These include duplicating the subject ("Mr. Drogo, he married..."), double negatives ("Talking won't mend nothing"), altered subject-verb agreement ("'Elves and dragons!' I says to him"), elision of sounds ("[My dogs] won't harm you – not unless I tell'em to."), and coined word-forms ("jools" for jewels, "trapessing" for traipsing).<ref name="Bayona 2011"/> Further, they use certain expressions, such as "begging your pardon", "I reckon", and "if you understand [me]" repeatedly.<ref name="Bayona 2011"/> The translation, Bayona explains, either omits these effects altogether, or, as with double negatives, renders them accurately in standard Spanish (where double negation like "{{lang|es|Con hablar '''no''' remediamos '''nada'''}}" (her emphasis) is grammatically correct). A rare exception, writes Bayona, is the coinage {{lang|es|medoreando}} instead of standard Spanish {{lang|es|merodeando}} to represent "trapessing"; she comments "it certainly marks Ham [Gamgee] in the eyes of the reader."<ref name="Bayona 2011"/>

The effect of the standard Spanish rendering is to continuously tone down Tolkien's construction of differences in style of speech. Bayona concludes that this does not change the plot, nor how the reader sees the characters, but it does sacrifice some of the additional cues that Tolkien provides about each character's social class and individuality.<ref name="Bayona 2011"/>

=== Chinese versions (Chu; Ding et al) 2001–02 ===

Li Hong-man writes that there are multiple obstacles to creating a Chinese version of ''The Lord of the Rings''. These include the large distance between the languages and cultures, and lack of familiarity with the work's themes and subject matter. Li compares two Chinese versions: that of [[Lucifer Chu]] (Zhu Xueheng) published by Linking Books in [[Taipei]] (Taiwan) in 2001–02, and that of Ding Di ({{lang|zh|丁棣}}), Yao Jingrong ({{lang|zh|姚镜镕}}), and Tang Dingjiu ({{lang|zh|汤定九}}), published by [[Yilin Press]] ([[Nanjing]], China) in 2001. Li states that Ding et al's version may be more literary, at the cost of making it "sound like a traditional Chinese ''[[wuxia]]'' [martial arts fiction] story".<ref name="Li 2010"/> In contrast, Chu, more familiar with Western fantasy, has written a far more popular version. Taking the example of the [[Rhyme of the Rings]], Ding et al use a modern Chinese poetry format, whereas Chu uses a classical format likely to be familiar to a Chinese readership. Chu translates the chapter title "A Knife in the Dark" rather directly (黑暗中的小刀, ''Hēi'àn zhōng de xiǎodāo''), whereas Ding et al render it more elaborately (夤夜劍光, ''Yínyè jiàn guāng'', "The light of the sword in the night").<ref name="Li 2010"/>

A different tradition is of the ''[[youxia]]'', a Chinese analogue of Europe's chivalric [[Knight-errant|knights-errant]]. Ding et al's version renders "The Passing of the Grey Company" as {{lang|zh|游侠骑士}} ''youxia qishi'', "the wandering ''xia'' riders". Eric Reinders comments that Aragorn fits this mould well, while others like Boromir, Gandalf, Legolas, and Gimli "loosely" approximate it; but the anti-heroic hobbits absolutely do not.{{sfn|Reinders|2024|pp=43–44}}

The translations approach names very differently, too. Chu transliterates the name [[Rivendell]] (瑞文戴爾 ''Ruì wén dài'ěr'', sounding something like the original), whereas Ding et al follow Tolkien's advice in his "Guide to the Names" and translate its meaning (林谷 ''língǔ'', "Forest Valley").<ref name="Li 2010">{{cite journal |last=Li |first=Hong-man |title=Fantasy in translation: A study of two Chinese versions of ''The Lord of the Rings'' |journal=Cross-Cultural Communication |volume=6 |issue=4 |year=2010 |at=article 20 <!--http://cscanada.net/index.php/ccc/article/view/j.ccc.1923670020100604.011/1018-->}}</ref>
