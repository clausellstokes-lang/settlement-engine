 "The Beauty of this Game Is How It's Never What Anyone Expects..." D&D's James Wyatt & Chris Perkins Talk 2024 DM's Guide

 With 2024 crawling toward its final months, there is still plenty for Dungeons & Dragons fans celebrating the 50th anniversary of the world's greatest roleplaying game to get excited about. With the release date for the D&D 5e 2024 Dungeon Master's Guide just weeks away, (November 12, 2024,) DMs can't wait to begin pouring through the pages and upping their game with strategies and tips from the best of the best in the business. There are plenty of new features to explore, alongside some tried and true methods that have driven the game for five solid decades. CBR recently had the chance to sit down with co-lead designers for the 2024 D&D 5e Dungeon Master's Guide . James Wyatt and Chris Perkins share their thoughts about some of their favorite elements of the book, as well as how their experiences as Dungeon Masters shaped the foundation of this core rules update.

 By  
 Guillermo Kurten 
 CBR: Over the years, I'm sure at various conventions and so on, you've come in contact with a lot of other Dungeon Masters. What is a really common ask or request that you get from DMs whenever there has been talk about a new or updated version of the Dungeon Master's Guide ? 
 Chris Perkins: One of the things that springs to mind right away is I'll often be in conversation with DMs, and I'll be describing something that was in the 2014 DMG, and they didn't even know it was there. Which leads to a conversation of, "I can't find what I need in the Dungeon Master's Guide." So, I think one of the things we talked about early on was just making sure that we're surfacing the information that's in the guide in a better way, so that people actually know it's there and can find what they're looking for. 
 James Wyatt: In a sort of general sense, the Fourth Edition DMG was so good at introducing people to the game, and it's very sad that the 2014 DMG wasn't as good. So, part of what I did, particularly in working in the various chapters of the book, was to try and bring in some of that, not even so much as the content, but the helpful tone to ease you in to the amazing and wonderful world of dungeon mastering. 
 CBR: Hand-in-hand with that, how important did you both feel it was to make sure there was information included in the 2024 DMG to guide new DMs on how to make sure that everybody at their table was comfortable and having a good time, and that they all felt like they were in a safe place to explore? 
 JW: Super important. 
 CP: Yes, yes. So, as the D&D audience grows, and we bring more people into the tent, these topics become much, much more important, I think. The game has been around for 50 years, it's multi-generational, so there are different perspectives. You could have a very, very mixed group of folks, so it was important to us to have some guidelines to help DMs understand what their players like, what their players don't like, what makes them comfortable, what makes them uncomfortable, what sorts of games they're into, what sorts of games they're not into, that kind of thing. Building on material that we had in Tasha's Cauldron of Everything , which was well-received, we imported that material and then massaged it. And James can probably talk a little bit more about that. 
 J.W.: Yes, as well some material from Van Richten's Guide to Ravenloft , which explored some more sensitive topics. The 2014 DMG also had a section on what we called "Table Rules," which has all sort of blended together into a general section of, "Hey, talk to your players." Part of that comes from my own experience watching my daughter and her girlfriend navigating the social aspects of the game. So often, the answer to the problems that they're facing is, "You all need to have a conversation. You need to talk openly about this." 
 CP: Yeah, when I was first learning to DM, and first playing, I played with a group of friends that didn't really change. It was just the four of us for years and years and years and years, but, with online play and virtual tabletops, it's much easier to bring a stranger, basically, into the fold of your game. Arming the DM with guidance on how to make that person feel comfortable, I think, is more important now than ever. 
 CBR: As an example, having neurodivergent players, it's really important to know how to navigate a game where not everyone thinks the same, and I think having this is a really great asset for all DMs to make sure they're keeping everyone comfortable. It's an adventurous game, and there are a lot of opportunities for uncomfortable situations.

 By  
 Jennifer Melzer 
 CP: Yes, particularly when you're kind of embracing a role and getting out of yourself in a way and just acknowledging that's part of the experience. 
 JW: Some of the feedback we got from a couple of our consultants really helped in the area of dealing with neurodivergent folks. Like the game for many years has advised things like, "Ask your players to limit the use of their phones or laptops at the table," and a couple of folks that's not good advice for everyone. Some people need to be distracted by one thing so they can focus on another thing. So, we softened that advice to make it more friendly to a wider range of people. 
 CBR: I think that's amazing. Now, as Dungeon Masters when you're planning adventures because I know you all play all the time, because it's your job and that's amazing. I'm super jealous! But when you're planning a session or a game, what are your favorite parts about that process, and how does this new Dungeon Master's Guide embrace your methods for planning and your favorite parts about it? 
 CP: My favorite is thinking of the conflicts that are going to drive the adventures of the campaign, and then even more fun is finding ways to hook those conflicts into the characters and their backstories. I think about that a lot. Often, I'll think about the next adventure, like the one I'm running next week, and it's like, "Oh, you know, Anna's character really hasn't had a focus yet. How can I tie what's happening into her character, so her character can kind of grow and explore?" 
 One of the things we added in the new Dungeon Master's Guide, and I'd love for James to talk about this, because I know he constructed it, and very quickly, is this page (DM's Character Tracker sheet from the 2024 DMG ) that a DM can copy and keep in their campaign journal, which sort of captures the essence of each character in the party. What the characters' goals and ambitions are, what magic items they have, has the player defined any of the family of the character... All this information is fodder that the DM can use. James calls them knives that the DM can use to stick the players as conflict is created. 
 JW: Knives is a term I picked up from my kids, who are more active in the online world of D&D fandom than I am these days. That tracking sheet came about very late in the process because we realized in the printout reviews as we were getting ready to send it to the printer that we had a lot of telling you to keep track of this information, but this was one place where we didn't actually give you a sheet to keep track of this information. That just made it so much clearer, and I think, hopefully, much more useful to the DM. 
 I was going to sort of twist your question around and say that my favorite part of preparing is what happens that I couldn't possibly prepare for. More relevant to your actual question is then picking up those things for the next session and finding ways while I'm preparing to pull back to the weird, unexpected things that happened before. Making players realize that something was significant that they didn't think was significant, or take something they thought was significant that I didn't think was significant and make it significant. That, to me, is just the beauty of this game, is just how it never really is what anyone expects. 
 CBR: It really isn't, and that's really the beauty of this game. Now, Chris, you and I were talking over the summer at Gen Con about your early experiences with the game, and how you later discovered you weren't even playing it right. How has that shaped your philosophy when it comes to working on things like the DM's Guide and making sure things are clear, but also that people can just sort of take it and run with it? 
 CP: That's a great question. So, my first experiences playing D&D was that I had a Monster Manual and nothing else, and I thought it was a game where the monsters fight each other. My friend and I got some dice and we picked monsters, and we basically just fought each other. We did that for months, until we found another rule book that gave us more to hold onto. But... we had a fun time, even though that wasn't the intended way of playing. We still managed to make it a lot of fun, and there were more examples of that down the road. I ran adventures wrong, I did everything wrong, and it was okay because everybody was having a good time. 
 It did, sort of over time, help shape me as a DM, and I think one of the things we wanted to strike on early in the book is this idea that every DM is unique. You're gonna have your own style. You may learn things through examples of play or by reading DM advice, or whatever, but you're gonna take it and twist it and make it your own. And as long as everybody at the table is having a good time, then you're kinda doing it right. So, I think that those early missteps, if you can call them that, or discoveries, really kind of led to this idea that we wanted to drive the understanding that: DM's we don't expect you all to be the same, and that you can extract a lot by reading, by watching, and then just take it and make it your own. 
 JW: My first exposure to D&D was when my older brother received a copy of the original box set as a Christmas present, and I had read The Lord of the Rings and was really into fantasy. I spent hours reading those little books and trying to figure out how on earth that was a game. So, I worked on the examples of play in both the Player's Handbook and the Dungeon Master's Guide, which to me are really strong introductions to what this game is, and how you play it. As a narrative exercise, it's all dialogue, all the conversations that the players and the DM have around the table, and presenting the scripts of that unfolding in play, I think, is the answer to my eight-year-old self saying, "How is this a game?"

 CBR: I love that! I love that we've taken storytelling and camaraderie and turned it into this epic game that we all share. I really think that's a beautiful thing. Now, to close things out, I did want to ask you what you could tell us about bastions, and what made you decide to include them, because it's such an epic game changer, in a way. It's gonna make things a lot more long term, I think, and there's that potential to make games even more worthy of player investment. 
 CP: Yeah, I think you just hit it. The idea that bastions are this player engagement activity, like character creation, in a way, it's just another way for players to engage with the campaign. I hope, as players take custody of their bastions and build them out, they might draw little maps of their own bastions, they might give the NPCs who work there names, and really kind of flesh out the identity. Before they know it, they're like DMs. They're creating their own pocket of this world and breathing life into it. I think one of the reasons why it's great that bastions are sitting in the Dungeon Master's Guide is this idea that if a player really grooves on building a bastion, they may groove on other DM activities, and see more in the Dungeon Master's Guide that might get them over that hurdle and be like, "You know what, I might want to DM a game someday." 
 I think that's super cool because DMing is so rewarding, and it's such a wonderfully creative venture for anybody to step into that role. We just wanted to have something to help players think about that, while at the same time giving them something that's meaningful in the game. Now the trick is, how do we let players loose on their bastions without creating a headache for the Dungeon Master? And so, one of the glorious things is that Bastions are completely player-managed. DM's have to unlock it, basically say that the campaign is braced for it, but once they allow their players to go off and build bastions, all the DM really has to do is have check-ins, and say, "How's the bastion-building going?" Maybe give players advice if the player doesn't know what to do with their bastion. 
 JW: One of the themes that runs through the book, particularly in the campaigns chapter, is the idea of really hooking characters into the world, making sure that they feel like they're a part of the world, they're connected to the world, they care about the world. They're invested in the stakes of the adventure that they're on, and bastions are just a really good way to do that. And we've seen that at play in adventures like Waterdeep: Dragon Heist , where characters end up in possession of this tavern, and what that does to the flavor of a campaign when you're like, this is my place. I care about this place. So, we're really excited about the potential of bastions to affect the way we write adventures in the future. Where we can integrate the character's bastions into the story of a campaign adventure. 
 CBR: Actually, one of the very first things I thought of when I was reading through that section was Critical Role Campaign One, when they had Castle Greyskull, and then the Chroma Conclave came and destroyed everything. And that was really the best because they were so invested in this castle they had, and I just thought wow, this is so perfect. That is a great tool to really get players invested. 
 JW: The Xhorhaus in Campaign Two was on my mind a lot as we worked on that chapter, particularly the material about characters combining bastions. Like there's the tree up on top of the house, which is the druid's place. 
 CBR: Yes, yes! Exactly, such a cool concept. That's gonna wrap things up, but I really appreciate you both taking the time to sit down and talk with me today. It was really great. Thank you.
