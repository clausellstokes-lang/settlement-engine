## Claims (feature — claim — source — quote)

### Drout (own words)
- register modulation / narrator intrusion — The fox's thoughts in FotR ch.3 are a failure of tone, an intrusive narrator jarring after ch.2's seriousness — Wormtalk 2007-04-12 — "moving us back towards the intrusive narrator of The Hobbit"
- ornamental vs functional archaism — "thrawn" is decoration, whereas eyot, laving, louver, dwimmerlaik are precise technical words — same — "merely an ornamental archaism"
- landscape density — Drout estimates over half the novel is landscape description and asks whether fewer words would do — same — ""
- apo koinou syntax — "speeding along with little hope of stopping or turning" reads two ways at once, forcing small re-parsings — same — "tiny re-constructions of sentence meaning"
- deliberate repetition as myth — the rain-as-grey-curtain image is justified by recurrence, not by novelty — same — "The recurrent image of the rain as a curtain is significant"
- critical method — blanket "depth" talk is worthless; critique must be specific and detailed — same — "useless as criticism"
- lexical depth by reference — Tolkien's rare words are precise terms that link back to earlier literature — Wormtalk 2007-04-08 — "precise technical terms"
- syntactic archaism as allusion — "Come not between the Nazgûl and his prey" invokes larger literary contexts by traditional referentiality — same — "makes use of traditional referentiality"
- modernist misreading — the prose has depth; its referents are simply not the ones the critic knows — same — "not the depths that the critic knows"
- annalistic vs novelistic — The Children of Húrin is a hybrid of the Silmarillion's annalistic and LotR's novelistic style — Wormtalk 2007-04-17 — "historic/annalistic style of The Silmarillion"
- mythopoeic bricolage — reusing annals, stock phrases and tableaux from earlier texts makes the writing feel like myth — same — "bricolage of previously existing texts"
- sentence-level neglect — the biggest failing in Tolkien criticism is the absence of study of word choice and syntax — Drout & Wynne 2000 p.123 — "his sentence-level writing, his word choice and syntax"
- shifting voice — Author of the Century addresses head-on LotR's archaism, diction and shifting voice — same p.123 — "the issues of archaism, diction, and shifting voice"

### Shippey, The Road to Middle-earth (direct)
- per-speaker register (baseline) — inside Gandalf's Council monologue the Gaffer's speech is the base-line of normality and emptiness — pp.107–8 — "a kind of base-line of normality"
- political register (Saruman) — Saruman's balanced phrases and empty words ('deploring', 'ultimate', 'real') mark him as a politician — p.108 — "Saruman talks like a politician"
- archaic syntax (Elrond) — Elrond's speech is full of inversions and words like weregild, esquire, shards — p.109 — "full of old-fashioned inversions of syntax"
- authenticating archaism (Gandalf) — Gandalf uses older vocabulary than usual; Isildur's scroll adds 'glede', 'fadeth', 'loseth' — p.109–10 — "as if to authenticate himself"
- Aragorn vs Boromir — Aragorn's language is deceptively modern and wide-ranging; Boromir's is stiffly magniloquent — p.110 — "Boromir's slightly wooden magniloquence"
- dwarvish understatement — dwarf dialogues delight in polite surface over passionate interior ('not too long' = 'extremely rapidly') — p.110 — "passionate interior and polite or rational expression"
- language variation as ethics — stylistic variation is Tolkien's economical way of dramatising ethical debate — p.111 — "a thorough and economical way of dramatising ethical debate"
- book as Council — the whole book works like the Council chapter, by relished stylistic contrast and tableaux — p.111 — "the relishing of stylistic variation"
- loose semantic fit — Tolkien emulates Malory's tolerance of semantically loose phrasing ('by stock or by stone', echoing Pearl), which puzzles modern-trained readers — pp.160–1 — "His emulation of 'loose semantic fit'"
- register rise in elvish speech — Galadriel's farewell suddenly turns less plain, with an elvish place-name — p.161 — "sudden change to less plain language"
- archaic effect without archaic words — inversion of nouns/adjectives and adverbs like 'yet', 'seldom' produce archaism where only 'naught' is archaic — (chapter on style, "hovering between styles") — "by inversion of nouns and adjectives"
- modern rewrite would be bogus — modern usage over archaic thought would create a disunion of word and meaning — same — "would simply have sounded bogus"

### Hostile critics, as directly quoted by primaries
- overworked cadence (Manlove 1975) — Manlove condemns the Treebeard/Galadriel farewell for droning, monotonous, over-worked cadence — Manlove quoted verbatim in Road p.160 — "The overworked cadences, the droning, monotonous pitch"
- elegiac register (Manlove) — Manlove sets Ector's lament and The Wanderer against it as real elegy — same — "This is real elegy"
- "not literature" (Raffel 1968 p.218) — Raffel grants magnificence but denies LotR the status of literature — K&S Introduction n.11 — "is not literature"
- professorial amateurishness (Wilson 1956) — prose and verse are on the same amateurish level — Wilson quoted in Siepmann 2017 — "the same level of professorial amateurishness"
- "dreadful prose style" (Jenny Turner 2001) — dismissal in passing — Curry — "dreadful prose style"
- unironic high style (Anthony Lane 2001) — a high style unused unironically since Tennyson's Idylls — Curry — "Hardly anyone had used it unironically since Tennyson"
- archaic "high" style as taste barrier — Curry's own concession — Curry — "its occasional archaic and 'high' style"
- ordinary yet dignified diction (Kirk) — Siepmann characterises Kirk's verdict — Siepmann 2017 — "Tolkien's ordinary yet dignified diction"

### Corpus / stylometric
- three lexical causes of archaicity — over-use of 19th-c fiction words, avoidance of modern-world words, dense coinages/rare words/unusual grammar — Siepmann 2017 — "three simple lexical causes"
- 19th-century keyword sets — landscape; position/direction (whither, thither, thence, yonder, afar); warfare; descriptive verbs/adjectives; many obsolete when written — Siepmann — "so many of the keywords used to describe its settings"
- Beowulf-translation borrowings — valour, valiant, corslet, stretched to new uses ('Branched lightning smote…') — Siepmann — "Tolkien borrows a host of words from his translation of Beowulf"
- modern words in old patterns — e.g. 'a black smoke' with indefinite article — Siepmann — "tends to use many of these words in nineteenth-century patterns"
- descriptive verbs (modernist streak) — clutch, cower, flicker, gleam, loom, peer, plod, stride — Siepmann — "abundant use of descriptive verbs"
- LotR-exclusive keywords — dingle, fen, glades, starless, lore-masters, wolf-riders, yesteryear, yuledays, along/among/outwards — Siepmann — "an atmosphere of medievalism or otherworldliness"
- homely collocation + hyperbole — preference for the most homely collocations; semantic domains (speed, size, loudness) suggest hyperbole — Siepmann — "a marked preference for the most homely collocations available"
- linguistic postmodernist — old words to new uses and vice versa — Siepmann — "he is a linguistic postmodernist"
- 19th-c novel dependence — corpus keyword/collocation method shows the style relies heavily on the 19th-c novel and the Beowulf translation — K&S ch.2 — "relies heavily on the nineteenth-century novel"
- collocational subtlety unnoticed — K&S ch.2 — "escaped both the casual reader's and the literary critic's notice"
- hobbit focalisation — the narrator gives a group's point of view, usually hobbits, in ordinary language — K&S ch.4 — "usually hobbits"
- archaic diction for epic characters — other passages carry an archaic, Ossianic diction conveying omniscience, tied to Aragorn-type figures — K&S ch.4 — "archaic diction, reminiscent of ancient epic poetry"
- 19th-c focalisation practice — limited point of view follows 19th-c novel practice — K&S ch.4 — "follows nineteenth-century novelistic practice"
- self-effacing narrator (Thomas 2000) — narrator "self-effacing", "unintrusive", "vanishing"; ten viewpoints in ch.1 — Thomas quoted in K&S ch.4 — "ten different viewpoints"
- intrusive → self-effacing arc (Thomas) — Hobbit opens intrusive, recedes; LotR impartial and multi-viewpoint; measured on Booth's scale — kate_nepveu notes — "impartial and self-effacing"
- discourse juxtaposition in The Hobbit — fairy-tale/epic discourse set against everyday speech through irony and parody — Kullmann 2009 abstract — "juxtaposed with everyday speech through irony and parody"
- realism + archaic rhetoric in LotR — 19th-c circumstantial realism and pathetic fallacy combine with archaic rhetorical patterns — Kullmann 2009 abstract — "circumstantial realism and pathetic fallacy combine with archaic rhetorical patterns"

### Le Guin
- Elfland register in dialogue — the Aragorn/Boromir/Elrond exchange is quieter English, timeless, with sobriety, wit and force — Le Guin 1973 — "It has sobriety, wit, and force"
- plain style with range — Tolkien writes plain clear English whose virtue is flexibility, commonplace to stately — 1973 — "ranges easily from the commonplace to the stately"
- unstriking vocabulary — no 'ichor'; direct, concrete, simple — 1973 — "Tolkien's vocabulary is not striking; he has no ichor"
- slide into metre — prose slides into metrical verse (Bombadil) unnoticed — 1973 — "can slide into metrical poetry"
- archaism as distancer — the archaic manner distances perfectly but is a high wire; Eddison did it — 1973 — "The archaic manner is indeed a perfect distancer"
- register alternation fails — Leiber/Zelazny's colloquial/formal alternation breaks character coherence — 1973 — "the characters lose coherence in my mind"
- fake plainness — journalistic fantasy prose is flat, inexact, sensorily vague — 1973 — "is not really simple, but flat"
- two-beat rhythm — the narrative rhythm is stress/release, walking gait, at the scale of the whole book — 2001 via nepveu — "as simple as a rhythm can be: two beats"
- polarities — Barrow-downs chapter pulses between darkness/daylight, confusion/clarity, trap/freedom — 2001 — "a pulsation back and forth between polarities"
- sentence-level reversals — the chapter's closing sentences reverse dark/light, down/up, hill/village like drumbeats — 2001 — "They are like drumbeats"
- trochaic beat — stable rhythmic pattern built on trochaic stress/unstress — 2001 — "Relying on the irreducible simplicity of the trochaic beat"
- read-aloud cadence — long sentences follow the breath; punctuation falls where one pauses — Le Guin quoted by Fisher — "the cadences are graceful and inevitable"

### Lewis / Auden (1954–56)
- deep time — one cannot step anywhere without stirring the dust of history — Lewis 1954 — "without stirring the dust of history"
- nomenclature — Lewis 1954 — "The names alone are a feast"
- heroic romance register — Lewis 1954 — "heroic romance, gorgeous, eloquent, and unashamed"
- elvish beauty in prose — Lewis 1954 — "piercing, high, elvish beauty"
- interlace — parallel narration adds to pathos, irony, grandeur — Lewis 1955 via nepveu — "adds immensely to the pathos, irony, and grandeur"
- opening archness fades — Auden 1954 — "this initial archness disappears"
- exact description and naming — Auden 1954 — "a wonderfully exact eye for description"
- deep history — Auden 1954 — "as well as one knows one's own childhood"
- hobbit rustic register — hobbits resemble the arcadian rustics of British detective fiction — Auden 1954 — "those arcadian rustics"

### Mendlesohn / Walker / Bratman / Olsen / Halbrooks
- descriptive elaboration of the portal-quest — language elaborate in the anthropologist's or pre-Raphaelite's descriptive way — Mendlesohn intro — "the elaboration of the anthropologist or the pre-Raphaelite painter"
- reader learns from entry — LotR handled in ch.1 as portal-quest; the reader is positioned to learn from a point of entry — Mendlesohn intro — "we learn from a point of entry"
- invitational vagueness — descriptions sound detailed but give an open-ended outline for the reader to fill — Walker interview — "invitational quality"
- style off the radar — critics missed the style because it is not a scholarly concern — Walker interview — "isn't on our scholarly radar"
- multiple versions in differing styles — the legendarium exists, like folk mythology, in many tellings and styles — Bratman (Estate) — "multiple versions in differing styles and levels of detail"
- tone rises with stakes (Olsen) — Olsen tracks changes in tone as The Hobbit's stakes grow; elf groups distinguished by speech — Oberhelman review — "changes in tone as the stakes become greater"
- sentence-by-sentence method (Olsen) — ExLotR is explicitly a sentence-level reading — Mythgard page — "sentence by sentence"
- polyglossia at the Council — Gandalf performs each speaker's voice (Saruman, Gollum, the Gaffer, Butterbur) — Halbrooks — "of polyglossia"
