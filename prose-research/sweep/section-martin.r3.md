# Martin: the prose of George R. R. Martin, reconstructed from verified claims only

Basis. The martin sweep carried 330 claims across four angles (academic and scholarly; craft essays and writing advice; the author's own words; close readings and line-level analysis). After merge, 325 claims are VERIFIED (232 verbatim, 93 substance), 5 are NOT FOUND, none contradicted, none blocked. Every feature below rests only on the 325 kept claims, cited by index in square brackets. Every quotation is the verifier's true wording, trimmed to under twelve words. A "voice" is one critic, scholar, outlet or interview; the two Books and Boots posts are one critic (Simon), the eighteen At Sea Journal essays are one critic (Erik Germani), Lev Grossman's three pieces are one critic, and Martin's own words are counted per interview but named as his. A feature that rests on one voice is flagged SINGLE SOURCE. Where the sources disagree, the disagreement is stated in place and collected again in the ledger at the end.

The target of every reconstruction rule is a settlement dossier written by a calm archivist: present tense, concrete civic nouns, no digits, no em dash. The rules are written in that register where they can be.

## The one Martin mode that maps onto a dossier

Martin has said that his history book was easier than the novels because it has one voice, the Archmaester's: "And the history book, I only have one voice to do." [210]. The novels' engine (a rotating cast of tight third-person narrators, chapter-end cliffhangers, dialogue that outshines narration) does not transfer to an archivist's dossier. What transfers is the ground beneath it: the native-root lexicon, archaism used as salt, sensory nouns of food, cloth and blazon, proverbs as the marks of a culture, names bent from real ones, a world that has plagues and pageantry at once, and consequences traced from every departure from the real. Each feature below says which side of that line it falls on.

## A. Lexicon

### 1. Native-root diction: Anglo-Saxon words in, Latinate words out

Sources: 6 voices. Books and Boots, two posts [0] [7] [77] [215] [219] [237]; the Saskatchewan History of English blog [320]; Greenlight Coverage [148]; Koeksal [149]; Rivera's readability figures [68] [321]; Spathis via Winter Is Coming [69] [322]; AutoCrit's Flesch score [323].

True wording: "a high frequency of words of Anglo-Saxon origin" [0]; "he consistently chooses words of Anglo-Saxon origin" [215]; "a systematic exclusion from his vocabulary" [219]; "Martin hits a sweet spot between Modern and Middle English" [320]; "An author doesn't need big, fancy words to make his/her point" [149].

What the sources say. Simon's two posts make the strongest and most detailed case: the vocabulary systematically excludes almost all words from Latin, Greek and French, and the feel of antiquity is cumulative, produced by word choice rather than by any pseudo-medieval marker [6] [7]. The Saskatchewan blog independently finds accurate late Middle English words used sparingly under modern syntax, with no thou or thee [320]. The measured corpus figures agree from the other side: a sixth-grade automated readability index and a lexical density near four [68] [321], a Flesch reading ease of eighty-seven with few long sentences [323], and a reading age of about ten [69] [322]. Caution: the "systematic exclusion" claim itself is one critic's; the other five voices corroborate plainness and reading level, not the etymological census.

Reconstruction rule. The archivist writes in the old stock of the language. Hall, ward, hearth, kin, tithe, ford, mill, reeve, oath, fold, field, bread, hide, harvest. Words that end in tion, ment, ity and ance are held back; population becomes folk or households, administration becomes the council or the reeve's office, infrastructure becomes roads, wells, walls and mills, regulation becomes the town's rule or custom. The sentence stays short enough for a child to follow, however grave the matter.

### 2. Native affixes: un, be, the positional a, and the Old English suffixes

Sources: 1 voice. Books and Boots 2013 [1] [2] [79] [219] [238]. SINGLE SOURCE.

True wording: "Almost none of these are found in Martin's work." [1] (of the Latin and Greek prefixes); "atop, abed, ahorse" [238]; "convey a deep sense of their Anglo-Saxon provenance" [2].

What the source says. Prefixes are almost exclusively un and be, with the archaic positional a conspicuously used (atop, abed, ahorse); the suffixes that recur are craft, dom, hood, ness, less, ling, ship and ward, each with an Old English pedigree; the Latinate able, ate, ion and ous are rare [1] [2] [238]. Latin prefixes such as sub, super, inter and post are "hardly if any" present [79].

Reconstruction rule. Where the archivist needs a derived word, the suffix is native: kingship not monarchy, craft not industry, hardihood not resilience, folkdom not populace, wardship not custody, homeward not in the direction of. The positional a is allowed but rationed: atop the motte, abed with fever, afield at harvest. One such form to a paragraph, or it becomes a mannerism (see feature 7).

### 3. Compounds and coinages: two short native roots make the world

Sources: 5 voices. Books and Boots [3] [10] [78] [88] [220] [239] [240] [245]; Bednarska [54] [55]; Greenlight Coverage [148]; Martin on invented words via IGN [165]; Martin on Dothraki rhythm, Not a Blog [287].

True wording: "coin scores of wonderful and evocative neologisms" [3]; "the greater the sense of moving into his otherworld" [220]; "derives not from a handful of obvious stock phrases" [240]; "most neologisms have the lexical character" [54]; "created its name on the basis of the phrase 'dire wolf'" [55]; "He invented entire languages, I just fake it." [165]; "playing with the syntax and sentence rhythms a bit" [287].

What the sources say. Existing compounds of the warlike and feudal kind (battleground, bedchamber, breastplate, drawbridge, longship, warhammer) sit beside coinages cast in the same mould (sellsword, godswood, smallfolk, greenseer, dragonglass, skinchanger, weirwood, ironborn) [78] [239]. Simon argues that the sense of an otherworld comes from the frequency of such compounds on every page and not from any single stock medievalism such as prithee [220] [240]. Bednarska classifies the neologisms as lexical, made by compounding and affixation, mostly toponyms and proper names, and shows direwolf built from the paleontological phrase so a reader infers a very large wolf [54] [55]. Martin himself invents only the words he needs (about eight Dothraki words) and fakes the rest [165]; a foreign tongue is conveyed by bending English syntax and rhythm, not by a made language [287]. Wholly new medieval-sounding words (maester, septon, warg) exist but are few [10] [88].

Reconstruction rule. The dossier's institutions are named by compounding two plain roots: moot-hall, tithe-barn, ward-reeve, fish-wharf, salt-road, kin-feud, oath-day, watch-fire. Each compound must decode itself from its parts in the way direwolf does. Invented single words are rationed to what the setting truly needs and are used in the same sense every time. A foreign people's speech, where reported, is marked by word order and rhythm, never by an invented vocabulary.

### 4. Register keyed to rank: learned words for learned mouths

Sources: 3 voices. Books and Boots [4] [80] [219]; Rivera [66] [321]; Tosina Fernández [52].

True wording: "technocratic and legalistic mindset of medieval religious inquisitors" [4]; "in the context of legal or religious or courtly matters" [80]; "A sort of dialect ruled by rank rather than region" [66]; "used chiefly by \"upper-class\" characters" [52].

What the sources say. The rare Latinate words (fornication, adultery, intercession) are reserved for maesters, septons and courts, and stand out against the native backdrop [4] [80]. Low-born speakers say m'lord where the high-born say my lord, a dialect of rank rather than region [66] [321]. Proverbs are used chiefly by the upper class, against the usual folk association [52].

Reconstruction rule. The archivist's own voice stays native. When the dossier quotes or paraphrases a charter, a court, a priest or a physician, a Latinate word may appear as the mark of that office, and only there. Rank shows in the form of address the dossier reports, not in adjectives about rank.

### 5. Familiar words bent, and archaisms that have real dates

Sources: 6 voices. Books and Boots [89] [245]; Pulford at OUPblog [228] [318]; the Saskatchewan blog [320]; Germani [257]; nobodysuspectsthebutterfly [311]; Martin at the Nottingham signing [163].

True wording: "the slight deformation of existing standard words or phrases" [245]; "Martin's archaic-sounding amendment of turncoat" [318]; "date from 1451, 1488, and 1400 respectively" [228] (ser, southron, craven); "a few linguistic relics to give a historical veneer" [257]; "It's totally an AFFC and onward thing" [311]; "he feared a \"forsooth\" would follow shortly" [163].

What the sources say. Ser for sir, flowered for a girl's first blood, turncloak for turncoat: ordinary words are slightly deformed and, by repetition, come to seem the norm [89] [245]. A lexicographer confirms that ser, southron and craven are attested fifteenth-century forms and that turncloak is Martin's own remake [228] [318]. Germani lists the relics (must needs, soon or late, near as) that supply the veneer [257]. The archaic-ing thickened from the fourth book (nuncle), to some readers' annoyance [311]. Martin's editor feared a forsooth would follow mayhaps, so mayhaps was kept for the old (Aemon) and perhaps or maybe given to the young (Bran) [163].

Reconstruction rule. Each archaism the dossier uses must be a real word with a real date, chosen once and kept: reeve, hundred, tithe, wergild, glebe, assart, toll-bar. Bend a familiar word only where the bend is a rule of the world (ser) and then never vary it. Age the vocabulary by the speaker: an old charter or an elder's saying may carry the older form; the archivist's own sentence carries the modern one.

### 6. Archaism as salt: modern prose, no pop culture, an old word now and then

Sources: 4 voices. Martin at Nottingham [162] [163] [164]; Books and Boots [6]; the Saskatchewan blog [320]; Greenlight Coverage [148].

True wording: "modern prose, but omitting all pop culture references" [162]; "to understand the syntax and words" [164] (the Fevre Dream method, reading period novels); "The default setting of Martin's style is lucid and functional" [6]; "combines archaic expressions with modern language" [148].

What the sources say. Martin's stated compromise is modern prose with all pop-culture reference stripped out and an occasional archaic word, compared to a pinch of salt in soup [162]; for Fevre Dream he instead read period novels to absorb their syntax, a different strategy [164]. Simon locates the antiquity in cumulative word choice, not in the occasional oft or elsewise [6]. The Saskatchewan blog and Greenlight both read the blend as successful [320] [148]. Disagreement: the same critic who grants the lucid default (Simon) calls the salt annoying when it recurs (feature 7); Martin calls it deliberate. Both are verified; the difference is one of dose.

Reconstruction rule. The archivist's syntax is modern and plain. Nothing in the dossier names an object, idiom or measure that belongs to the reader's century. One old word in a paragraph is salt; three is a costume.

### 7. Register instability and cod medievalism (the standing criticism)

Sources: 6 voices. Books and Boots 2014 [5] [13] [81] [83] [84] [217] [241] [246]; Gelzer-Govatos [90] [92] [223] [324]; a commenter at Overthinking It [226]; Germani on anachronism [258]; Pulford [318]; nobodysuspectsthebutterfly [311].

True wording: "prose larded with fake medievalisms" [5]; "veers from purely functional modern thriller prose" [241]; "would-be medievalisms which become annoying mannerisms" [246]; "faux-medieval flourishes go a bridge too far" [92]; "words and phrases he rides like a hobbyhorse" [223]; "GRRM has a penchant for pseudo-archaic language" [226]; "Martin shows you the seams of his work" [258].

What the sources say. Simon's thesis is that the style is unstable: on one page fifteenth-century oft and elsewise, and then things divvied up and someone asking what that is all about as if in modern New York [5] [84] [217]; the most persistent mannerism is the dropped ly (like to be angry, near finished) [13] [83]. Gelzer-Govatos names jape for joke and mummer's farce for every hostile council as pretension [90] [324] and rates the prose at best serviceable [92]. Germani dates the leaks the other way: pug-nosed, longshoreman, condone, yen, burp and snark are modern words in Westeros [258]; Pulford adds that butt and ass jar against the fifteenth-century words [318]. Disagreement with feature 6 is explicit: the archaisms are salt to Martin and to the Saskatchewan blog, mannerism to Simon and Gelzer-Govatos, and the modern leaks are a separate fault that no source defends.

Reconstruction rule. The two failure modes are named and both are barred. No manufactured archaism: no oft, elsewise, mayhaps, jape, mummer's farce, must needs, dropped ly. No modern leak: no word or idiom the reader would date to a factory, a newspaper or a screen. The dossier's register is checked page by page for both faults, because the fault is the swing between them, not either alone.

## B. Sentence, paragraph and figure

### 8. The default register is lucid, functional and quick

Sources: 11 voices. Books and Boots [6] [82] [216] [242]; Koeksal [149]; Wasson via the Christian Science Monitor [37] [234]; Hartinger [235]; the Sword and Laser thread [313]; Rivera [68] [321]; Spathis via Winter Is Coming [69] [322]; AutoCrit [323]; Germani on readability [221]; Padol and Jeffery via Wikipedia [33] [34]. Against: Orr [35] [234], Brown [36] [233], Germani [222], Maenpaa [116].

True wording: "The default setting of Martin's style is lucid and functional" [242]; "Martin's prose is concise but pithy" [37]; "fast where Kay is slow; gritty where Kay is lyrical" [313]; "ASOIAF has an almost hypnotic readability" [221]; "about as good as structure gets" [235] (of the individual chapters); "aspires to be a page-turner" [33]; "widescreen epic fantasy, well delivered and competently told" [34]. Against: "too much repetition, unexceptional prose" [36]; "it hurts Martin's prose and his plot mechanics" [234]; "The sentences aren't beautiful." [222].

What the sources say. The praise and the blame agree on the description and differ on the verdict. The baseline is crisp and clear with occasional flashes of limpid beauty [82] [242]; a reading group calls it high definition, crisp and fast [313]; an editor finds chapter structure exemplary and the prose tight [235]; the figures say sixth grade, Flesch eighty-seven, few long sentences, few slow paragraphs [68] [323]. The first book's early reviewers rated it a competently told widescreen epic that aspires to page-turner status more than to epic fantasy [33] [34]. Grossman calls the prose deft and a pleasure in itself [45] [120]. Disagreement: Orr says the length hurts the prose [35] [234]; Brown says the prose is unexceptional and the idioms uniform [36] [233]; Germani says no sentence is beautiful [222]; Maenpaa says the books are never concise, being more about response and decision than action [116]. Wasson's "concise but pithy" [37] and Maenpaa's never-concise are a direct contradiction of adjective, resolved only by noticing that Wasson describes sentences and Maenpaa describes books.

Reconstruction rule. The archivist's sentence is short, declarative and plain: the mill stands at the ford; the reeve holds the key; the harvest fails in the wet year. Beauty, where it comes, comes from the plainness (feature 14), not from ornament. The dossier is concise at the sentence and generous at the whole, which is the shape both camps describe.

### 9. Adverbs are scarce, except the judging kind

Sources: 3 voices. Spathis via Winter Is Coming [69] [322]; AutoCrit [323]; Germani [253].

True wording: "That George R.R. Martin hates adverbs." [69]; "a Flesch Reading Ease score of 87" [323]; "Stupidly, Jon argued." [253].

What the sources say. A phrase-frequency chart across four series finds Martin the sparest with adverbs [69] [322]. AutoCrit's automated report nevertheless flags adverbs inside dialogue tags [323]. Germani shows an evaluative adverb in narration (stupidly) fusing narrator and character judgment, though he poses it as a question rather than a verdict [253]. Disagreement of degree: few adverbs overall, but the ones that remain do point-of-view work.

Reconstruction rule. The archivist uses almost no adverbs of manner. Where one appears it carries a judgment the town itself holds (the levy is raised unwisely; the well is sunk too near the midden), which is the archivist reporting what the settlement thinks, not decorating a verb.

### 10. Low-density sentences that work by volley; flow before polish

Sources: 1 critic for the analysis, with Martin's own words on both sides. Germani [97] [100] [222] [248] [259] [260]; Martin on cutting fat via Wikipedia [25]; Martin on line-by-line trimming via Hidden Gems [290]; Martin on his cured purple [170]. The analysis is SINGLE SOURCE.

True wording: "no single sentence is that effective" [248]; "a full cartridge of it gets the point across" [97]; "modular sentences that can snap together like LEGO" [259]; "Martin has a fair number of one-lines" [260]; "he stresses rhythm" [100]; against: "cutting out the fat and leaving the muscle" [25]; "trimming and tightening line by line and word by word" [290]; "if one adjective was good, three were better" [170].

What the sources say. Germani calls it shotgun writing: a steady flow of low-density sentences, none strong alone, effective by the cartridge [97] [248]. A passage of one thousand eight hundred and ninety-seven words compresses to four hundred and twenty-seven using only topic sentences, because the sentences are modular and deletable [259]; single-sentence paragraphs are frequent [260]. He reads Martin as a flow-first writer who stresses rhythm rather than machining sentences [100]. Disagreement: Martin describes a final pass that cuts the fat, taking almost eighty pages out of one book [25], and a line-by-line, word-by-word trim carried over from television [290]; he also says journalism school struck out his piled adjectives [170]. The flow-first drafting and the trim-last habit are compatible in sequence, but Germani's evidence is that the trim leaves the density low.

Reconstruction rule. The dossier's paragraph is built so that its first sentence could stand alone and the rest could be cut without loss of sense. Each sentence carries one fact. Momentum comes from the succession of plain facts, not from any single sentence's weight. The archivist trims: no sentence restates the one before.

### 11. Stock phrases, epithets and refrains: the Homeric machinery, and its cost

Sources: 11 voices. Germani [95] [96] [98] [221] [247] [249] [251]; Grossman in Time [118]; Gelzer-Govatos [91] [324]; Whitehead [107]; Overthinking It [225] with its commenter [226] [227]; Orr via Wikipedia [35]; Brown [36] [233]; Winter Is Coming on food phrases [137]; Martin on intentional repetition [198] [282]; Books and Boots [9] [86] [243]; Hughes's satire [296].

True wording: "stock phrases are thermals" [247]; "we are cognitively lazy with the familiar" [249]; "almost a shorthand for description" [98]; "to give their audience time to absorb" [96]; "those faces still as masks, and the faces dark with anger" [251]; "Like Wagner, he gives each of his characters leitmotifs that recur" [118]; "no writer uses a phrase thirteen times by accident" [225]; "I have learned this technique from Stephen King" [198]; "but I do it intentionally" [282]. Against: "the phrase comes up at least ten times in every book" [324] (mummer's farce); "Tyrion 'waddles' at least 12 times here" [35]; "'Words are wind,' is oft-repeated, probably a little bit too much" [107]; "warm bread, fresh from the kitchens" [137]; undecided: "haste, or careful repetitions designed to evoke the lazy, torpid atmosphere" [9].

What the sources say. Germani's thesis is that Martin is an American Homer: fixed epithets and stock phrases (black as night four times nearly unchanged, faces still as masks, men corded with muscle) that keep pace by triggering recognition rather than fresh sight, at the cost of memorable sentences [95] [98] [247] [249] [251]; the phrases are the minstrel's breathing room [96]. Grossman reads the recurring phrases as Wagnerian leitmotifs [118]; Overthinking It counts words are wind thirteen times in one book across every faction and calls it motif [225]; Martin says the repetition of sayings is intentional and learned from Stephen King, despite reader annoyance [198] [282]. Disagreement, and it is the sharpest in the file: Orr, Brown, Whitehead and Gelzer-Govatos read the same recurrence as excess and haste [35] [36] [107] [324]; a commenter grants the leitmotifs and still flags the pseudo-archaic tics [226] [227]; Simon cannot decide whether cool and still recur by haste or design [9] [86] [243]; a satirist's recipe has someone in every chapter declare that a thing "isn't worth a mummer's fart" and reads the italicised fixations as padding toward a word count [296]. The fixed phrase is verified as a real device and a real fault.

Reconstruction rule. The dossier may own a small number of fixed formulae, each tied to one civic thing and repeated in the same words every time it recurs: the bell that calls the moot, the ford that floods at thaw, the saying the town keeps. These are the settlement's epithets. Everything else is said once. No adjective of appearance recurs as a tag (no corded, no still as masks); no phrase is reused for convenience. A formula the reader will meet more than twice must be a saying the town itself repeats, so its recurrence is the world's and not the writer's.

### 12. Repetition of one word for cohesion or atmosphere

Sources: 2 voices. Rivera [67] [321]; Books and Boots [9] [86] [243].

True wording: "In just a few paragraphs, the insult is repeated five times" [67]; "repetitions in this passage – of 'cool' and 'still'" [243].

What the sources say. Bastard is repeated five times across consecutive paragraphs to bind them and to press Jon's stigma [67]; cool and still recur through the Water Gardens passage and may build its torpor [243]. Simon leaves the second case undecided between haste and design [9].

Reconstruction rule. The archivist may repeat one plain word through a short passage to hold it together, and the word is a noun the passage is about (the wall, the debt, the flood), never an adjective of mood.

### 13. Simile is the dominant figure; the figures are bodily and sometimes misfire

Sources: 4 voices. Ehiosun [144]; Weed [300] [301]; Germani [102] [103] [105] [261] [263] [267] [275]; Attewell [306].

True wording: "simile was the most predominant figurative language used" [144]; "Fear filled his gut like a meal he could not digest." [300]; "like an animal screaming in pain" [301] (the sound of swords, with no ring of metal); "cruel iron rams lapping at the water" [275]; "The day was warm and cloudless, the sky a deep blue." [263]; "iron rose, thorns gleaming" [306]; against: "Snows melt slowly, over weeks and months." [102]; "Jon told his uncle in a low, quiet voice." [261]; "Anger is sometimes cold too. Except when it is flaring hot." [105].

What the sources say. Simile predominates, with personification of natural forces [144]. The best figures make emotion bodily (fear as an undigested meal) [300] and defamiliarise a stock sound (swords screaming like an animal) [301]. Germani counts about two hundred and fifty zeugmatic sentences in roughly one hundred and forty-eight thousand, most about the body and about sky or weather [263], praises the homonym in rams lapping at the water [275] and the personified sword with time stopped [267], and lists the misfires: brigands melting like snow (snow melts slowly), glowering through a beard, a low quiet voice, anger both cold and hot [102] [103] [105] [261]. Attewell notes the ornate figure (iron rose) that tips into gore [306].

Reconstruction rule. The archivist's figures are few and drawn from the town's own stock: the granary, the ford, the forge, the herd. A simile must be true to the physics of its vehicle (snow melts slowly, so nothing quick melts like snow). Paired near-synonyms are barred (low, quiet). One figure to a section, and it should be a plain noun's likeness, not a personification of an abstraction.

### 14. Plain beauty in quiet, purple reserved for green and red

Sources: 4 voices. Books and Boots [8] [85] [242]; Germani [266] [267]; Attewell [306]; Martin on his cured purple [170].

True wording: "GRRM's limpid style can have a powerfully simple beauty" [8]; "the tone can be very casual and very purple" [266]; "time slept when swords woke" [267]; "if one adjective was good, three were better" [170].

What the sources say. Simon locates the beauty in the quiet passages, when nobody is swearing or beheading, and especially in Arya's Braavos chapters [8] [85] [242]. Germani finds the ornate register reserved for wilderness (green) and bloodshed (red), an ecstatic response to nature and violence [266] [267]; Attewell sees a battle open lyrical and descend into gore [306]. Martin reports that his early adjective-piling was struck out by journalism professors [170]. Mild disagreement of framing: Simon's beauty is the still moment; Germani's purple is the wild or violent one. Both are verified and they describe different passages.

Reconstruction rule. The archivist's ornament is rationed to two places only: a description of the land the town stands in (the green) and the record of a disaster or a killing (the red). Everywhere else the plainness is the beauty. Even in those two places the ornament is a single stronger verb or a single image, never a stack of adjectives.

### 15. The moving sentence: prepositions chained to carry the reader through space

Sources: 1 voice. Germani [273]. SINGLE SOURCE.

True wording: "beneath the gatehouse, over the drawbridge, through the outer walls" [273].

What the source says. Movement sentences chain prepositional phrases, a structure Germani names the moving sentence and pairs with Mansfield [273].

Reconstruction rule. When the dossier walks the reader through the settlement, it does so in one sentence of chained prepositions in the order a body would take: down from the gate, along the market street, past the well, to the moot-hall. This is the archivist's one permitted long sentence, and it is a route.

### 16. Punctuation: the semicolon was misused early and mastered late

Sources: 1 voice. Germani [265]. SINGLE SOURCE.

True wording: "Seems like he's gotten the semicolon figured out by now" [265].

What the source says. In the first book semicolons and colons are used interchangeably, three on one page; by the fifth the habit is corrected [265].

Reconstruction rule. The archivist uses the full stop. No semicolon, no colon inside a sentence, no em dash. Two clauses that want joining get a comma and a plain conjunction, or two sentences.

### 17. Dialogue is terse and charged; it outshines narration and does exposition's work

Sources: 6 voices. Books and Boots [12] [87] [218] [244]; Germani [99] [250] [264] [277]; Martin on Hollywood dialogue [171] [172] [173] [138]; Roberts [315]; Grossman's leitmotifs in speech [118]; Hurley on reminders in dialogue [71].

True wording: "terse, charged dialogue" [12]; "it's no surprise that dialogue would shine brightest" [250]; "other 60% of the text is forgettable" [99] (the narration); "These tags, when strategically placed, can serve as extra punctuation" [264]; "Martin's dialogue does a great job of revealing social dynamics." [277]; "I think working in Hollywood sharpened my dialog." [172]; "It's all structure, structure and dialogue." [173]; "much shorter back and forth kind of, kind of dialogue" [171]; "Being there improved my sense of structure and dialogue" [138].

What the sources say. Confrontations are carried by terse, charged exchanges whose implications land as shock [12] [87] [218] [244]. Germani's Reddit-grounded finding is that readers remember the dialogue and forget the narration [99] [250]; tags and action beats are placed as extra punctuation and the ideal exchange is untagged [264]; dialogue exposes hierarchy [277]. Martin credits television with shorter back-and-forth and sharper lines [171] [172] [173] [138]. Roberts praises exposition delivered in scene and dialogue [315].

Reconstruction rule. A dossier has no scenes, but it has reported speech: the town's sayings, a charter's clause, an elder's remembered line. Each is short, plain and charged, given once, and it reveals standing (who may say what to whom). The archivist never explains a reported line; the line does the work.

## C. Viewpoint and narration

### 18. Tight third person, one focaliser per chapter, chapter titled by name; no omniscience

Sources: 13 voices. Martin [14] [15] [150] [151] [157] [279] [284]; Wikipedia [26]; Rieken [46]; Refined Robot [70]; Sladiková [75]; Shmoop [142]; Lennox [143]; Błaszkiewicz [61]; Katz [314]; Roberts [315]; Hughes [294]; Martin on Fire and Blood's one voice [210]; McFarland's essay roster on narration [76].

True wording: "I've always written from a tight third-person point of view" [14]; "None of us are omniscient." [15]; "a limited but very tight third person point of view" [151]; "but I actually hate the omniscient viewpoint" [284]; "seeing everything through the eyes only of the viewpoint characters" [157]; "the character after whom they are named" [75]; "Each chapter is named after its POV character" [143]; "giving each character a clearly titled chapter solves the problem admirably" [315]; "\"The Iron Captain,\" \"The Princess in the Tower\"" [294] (the descriptive titles from the fourth book); "And the history book, I only have one voice to do." [210].

What the sources say. Every voice agrees on the mechanism: a heterodiegetic figural narrator [46], third-person narration heavily focalised with free indirect discourse [70], one character per chapter, the chapter named for that character [61] [75] [143] [314] [315], the roster growing from nine to thirty-one across five books [26], schemers denied a viewpoint to keep their mystery [142], and from the fourth book some chapters titled descriptively as a character's identity is unmade [61] [294]. Martin's reason is perceptual: none of us is omniscient, we see only from our own eyes [14] [15] [150] [151] [279] [284]. He also says the one-voiced history book was easier [210].

Reconstruction rule. The dossier is written in the Archmaester's mode, not the novels': one calm voice throughout. But that voice keeps the discipline of the limited narrator: the archivist reports what the settlement knows and records, and marks what it does not know (the cause of the fire is not recorded; the family's origin is disputed). The archivist never sees into a private mind; a person's motive appears only as what the town says of it.

### 19. Each viewpoint has its own voice and vocabulary; switching costs days

Sources: 6 voices. Martin [16] [18] [152] [153] [154] [280] [283] [140] [24]; Grossman [41] [119]; literaryanalysis [73] [316]; Maenpaa [115]; VanderMeer via Wikipedia [28]. Against: Brown [36] [233].

True wording: "I switch voices every time I switch chapters." [152]; "its own voice and vocabulary" [283]; "it's a whole different voice" [153]; "struggle for a few days trying to get back the voice" [16]; "write consecutively two, three or four chapters" [154]; "I get into a character's voice" [280]; "written in its own voice" [41]; "each point-of-view having a distinct palette and purpose" [115]; "creates the unstoppable momentum in his novels" [28]; against: "characters who use the same idioms" [36].

What the sources say. Martin drafts chapters out of reading order in runs of one character, because recovering a voice after a switch takes days [16] [18] [154] [280]; each viewpoint has its own vocabulary [283]; when one stalls he writes another [140]; the chapters are then rearranged for intercutting and suspense [24]. Critics grant each storyline its own rhythm and voice [41] [119] [73] [115] [316], and VanderMeer credits full inhabitation of characters with the momentum [28]. Disagreement: Brown finds characters using the same idioms whatever their class or continent [36] [233]. Both are verified; the per-chapter voice is real and the shared idiom pool is real, which is exactly the pool-uniformity failure the prose program has measured in our own corpus.

Reconstruction rule. The archivist has one voice, so this feature transfers as a rule about the people the dossier quotes and describes: each household, guild or faith the dossier reports must draw on its own small stock of words and sayings, and no saying may cross from one to another. The uniform-idiom fault is the one to guard against.

### 20. The focaliser is unreliable; contradictions between accounts are deliberate

Sources: 6 voices. Wikipedia [29] [31] [32] [292]; Rieken [47]; Martin in the Atlantic [156]; Katz [314]; Shmoop [142]; Richards via Wikipedia [29].

True wording: "may therefore not necessarily be true" [29]; "actively suppress their thoughts to conceal information" [32]; "Being so focalised, the implied author becomes unreliable" [47]; "the point of view structure and the unreliable narrator" [156]; "the reader only knows what the main character knows" [314]; "The viewpoint characters, who serve as unreliable narrators" [292].

What the sources say. Viewpoint characters give unreliable accounts [31]; some suppress their own thoughts to keep a secret from the reader [32]; because narration adopts the focaliser's flawed memory, the implied author becomes unreliable [47]; Martin says the apparent contradictions between chapters are intentional and belong to the point-of-view structure [156].

Reconstruction rule. The dossier's sources are people, and people misremember. Where the town's accounts differ, the archivist records both in the present tense and does not adjudicate: the guild says the charter is older than the church; the church says otherwise. A secret the town keeps is marked as kept, not revealed.

### 21. Psychic distance: close at the shoulder, with deliberate steps back

Sources: 3 voices. Germani [252] [253] [254] [255] [256] [271] [274] [278]; Refined Robot [70]; ASOIAF University [309] [310].

True wording: "only to take a big step backwards at odd times" [252]; "Martin cycles through every possible nomination for Ned" [255]; "Even is being used as an intensifier" [256]; "Only with temporal distance will he permit himself some narrative distance" [271]; "A little free indirect!" [274]; "Is a 6 percent increase that big a deal?" [254] (italicised thought, growing by book); "every sentence is a declaration of Tyrion's will" [309]; "part of him wanted to call out" [278]; "absolutely SILENT" [310].

What the sources say. The narrator hovers at the character's shoulder and steps back at odd moments, mixing the character's word for a person with the narrator's full name in one paragraph (her father, Ned Stark, Father, Eddard Stark) [252] [255]; a word like even marks the character's noticing without a thought tag [256]; a judging adverb fuses the two [253]; free indirect discourse slips into the character's idiom [274] [70]; italicised thought grows by about six percent across the series and is weakest when it states the obvious [254]; narratorial distance is permitted only when temporal distance justifies it [271]; moral grey is two impulses in one paragraph [278]. Fan close readers find that full names in narration assert agency while the dwarf appears ten times in one depressed chapter [309], and that an inner voice going silent before a violent act forces the reader to notice [310].

Reconstruction rule. The archivist stands at a fixed middle distance and does not move. One name for one person throughout a dossier, chosen by the person's standing in the town (the reeve, Aldous Reeve, Aldous) and never cycled. No italic thought, no interior. What the novels do with distance the dossier does with silence: the fact the record does not give is the loudest.

### 22. Every sentence is checked against what the knower could know

Sources: 3 voices. Martin [17]; Katz [314]; Błaszkiewicz on the prologue [60].

True wording: "does he understand what's happening?" [17]; "crucial information which is beyond the grasp of a focalising character" [60].

What the sources say. Writing a child, Martin checks every sentence against what the boy could understand and what words he would think [17]; the reader knows only what the character knows [314]; the prologue exists to supply what no focaliser can reach and to put the reader a step ahead [60].

Reconstruction rule. Every sentence of the dossier is tested against one question: could the town's record hold this? A rainfall total, a private thought, a cause no one witnessed fail the test. What the town could know is written; what it could not is either omitted or attributed (travellers say; the survey of the older lord records).

### 23. Many sides at once: viewpoint multiplied for moral grey

Sources: 5 voices. Martin [155] [201]; Grossman [44] [231]; Refined Robot [70]; Germani [278]; Bernard Cornwell's interview [19] [159].

True wording: "Having multiple viewpoints is crucial to the grayness of the characters." [155]; "The hero of one side is the villain" [201]; "from all sides at once" [44]; "Martin shoots the action from many angles, with a dozen narrators" [231]; "much more sympathetic once we get their point of view" [70]; "Sometimes I employ the private's viewpoint, very up close and personal" [159].

What the sources say. Multiple viewpoints exist so the reader sees a war from both sides' self-justifications rather than from a cartoon Dark Lord [155]; the Iliad is the model, where each side's hero is the other's villain [201]; villains become sympathetic in their own chapters [70]; a battle is told sometimes from the private's carnage and sometimes from the general's height [19] [159].

Reconstruction rule. The dossier gives every party its reason. A feud is recorded with the grievance of both houses in the same paragraph, each in its own terms. A war is recorded twice: once as the levy that marched and did not return, once as the lord's count of the field.

## D. Exposition, worldbuilding and texture

### 24. Exposition is embedded, prompted, paired with tension, and never restated by the author

Sources: 7 voices. Hurley [71]; Auerbach [126] [298] [299]; Oliver [127] [128] [129]; Germani [268] [276]; Dark Jackel [304]; Roberts [315]; Weed [303].

True wording: "These reminders are choreographed by creative dialogue with other characters" [71]; "the ninth year of summer, and the seventh of Bran's life" [298]; "amazing how many hints G.R.R.M. dropped in one paragraph" [126]; "pairing the exposition with the dramatic" [127]; "his extreme (over)use of meaningful nicknames" [128]; "obvious foreshadowing with a direwolf who's been killed by a stag" [129]; "It is a flashback dressed up as a monologue" [268]; "Stannis is mentioned 98 times before he appears" [276]; "The blade was Valyrian steel, spell-forged and dark as smoke." [299]; "some character makes a comment or observation, which prompts memories" [304]; "the wealth of specific, concrete detail" [303].

What the sources say. Facts are re-planted through other characters' mouths rather than authorial restatement [71]; time stamps are folded into the opening sentences [126] [298]; exposition rides on a small tension so the reader is not lost [127]; names and nicknames carry information across a large cast [128]; foreshadowing is planted openly and tiny asides are recontextualised later [129] [299]; backstory arrives as a remark prompting memory [304] or as monologue dressed as dialogue, assigned to a talkative character as a trait [268]; a character is built by others' talk long before appearing [276]; the important moment is slowed and crowded with concrete specifics [303]; exposition in scene holds attention [315].

Reconstruction rule. The dossier never announces a fact as background. A date is folded into an event (the mill burns in the ninth year of the present reeve); a family's standing is given through what the town calls them; a founder is known by the sayings still attached to the name before the founding is told. The disaster and the founding are the crowded paragraphs, thick with concrete nouns; the ordinary years are one sentence each.

### 25. Proverbs and sayings as the marks of a culture

Sources: 3 voices. Tosina Fernández [50] [51] [52] [53]; Pulford [229] [319]; Martin on repeated sayings [198].

True wording: "as well as creates his own, ad hoc" [50]; "to be stubborn as an auroch" [51]; "thus improving their paremiological competence" [53]; "Dark wings, dark words" [229]; "portrayed through a number of colloquial phrases" [319].

What the sources say. The first book mixes real proverbs, real proverbs adapted to Westeros, and proverbs invented as fictional folklore [50]; the invented idioms are anchored in in-world animals, occupations and beliefs (stubborn as an auroch, take the black, seven hells) [51]; proverbs spread by word of mouth, characters repeating what they heard from others [53]; recurring sayings characterise cultures and dialogue stays in English with culture-specific idiom rather than an invented tongue [229] [319]; the upper class uses them most [52].

Reconstruction rule. Each settlement owns a few sayings, and each saying is built from a thing the settlement has: its river, its beast, its trade, its saint. The archivist records a saying as the town's, in the town's words, and shows it passing from mouth to mouth (the ferrymen say it first; the council repeats it). No saying is borrowed from the reader's world.

### 26. Names bent from real ones, chosen for sound and for tone

Sources: 6 voices. Books and Boots [11]; Martin [139] [166] [167] [168] [169]; Sanderson via Hallak [317]; Oliver [128]; Germani [272].

True wording: "of being nearly recognisable but bent or distorted" [11]; "variants of real names -- Eddard for Edward" [166]; "I took actual names we still use today, like 'Robert'" [139]; "He's marvelous with names." [167] (of Jack Vance); "Vance has his voice and I have mine." [168]; "His ear for names, for language." [169]; "The name Stark implies a bleak and grim mood" [317]; "Gregor Clegane: 17 lines" [272].

What the sources say. Names fall into similar, alien and exotic groups that make the world nearly recognisable but bent [11]; Martin makes them as variants of real names (Eddard for Edward) or takes real names still in use (Robert), insists on the right sound, and cannot write a character until the name is found [139] [166]; he points to Vance and Tolkien as masters of naming while keeping his own plainer voice [167] [168] [169]. Reconstruction rule. A settlement's people bear names one step bent from the familiar, with the sound doing the work of a description. The town's name and the founder's name promise the tone of the dossier. A minor figure is fixed by one byname the town gave (the Late Lord, Old Nan) and needs no more.

### 27. Food, cloth and blazon: dense sensory catalogue, defended as never gratuitous

### 28. The language of dreams: colour, taste and texture as the reason for the register

Sources: 5 voices. Martin's essay On Fantasy [141] [196] [197] [285] [286]; Martin in January Magazine [176]; Martin via Azevedo [288] [289]; Rieken [48]; Martin in the cookbook foreword [182]; Whitehead on places coming alive [114].

What the sources say. ### 29. Plagues and pageantry: wonder and grit in the same world, rooted and then amplified

Every wonder is rooted first in a plain cause and then made a little larger than life. The dossier answers who held the office before, and before that.

## E. Structure and composition

### 32. Chapter endings as act breaks; a volume closes like a movement

Sources: 5 voices. Martin [23] [174] [291] [175]; Sheehan via Wikipedia [38]; Whitehead [111]; Hartinger [235].

True wording: "a tense or revelational moment, a twist or a cliffhanger" [23]; "just a twist point or a new revelation" [174]; "a movement of the symphony has wrapped up" [175]; "Filled with vividly rendered set pieces, unexpected turnings, assorted cliffhangers" [38]; "breaks off the book on a series of titanic cliffhangers" [111].

What the sources say. Each section of the dossier ends on its most consequential fact, the one the next section answers (the charter is granted; the tithe is doubled; the ford silts). ### 33. Chapters open in the middle of a small crisis, hop back, and return

True wording: "past perfect melds into the simple past tense" [270]; "The narrative cycles between these types of scene." Reconstruction rule. Each dossier section opens on the settlement's present state in one plain sentence, hops back to how it came to be in the past tense, and returns to the present for the close. The archivist opens on the concrete thing (the mill, the gate, the bell), never on a summary.

### 34. Interlacement, time jumps and rearranged chronology

Sources: 8 voices. Błaszkiewicz [59]; Rieken [49]; Whitehead [109] [112] [113]; Wikipedia [24]; Gessey-Jones and colleagues [63] [64]; Martin on Tolkien's shape [161] and the mosaic novel [160]; Grossman [42]. Against: Verhoeve via Wikipedia [39]; Brown [40]; Hartinger [235].

True wording: "interlacement and the diptych, or bipartite, division" [59]; "they jump in between interlacing storylines" [49]; "rotating-POV structure can lead to frustration, as you are just settling" [112]; "weeks passing between chapters, armies covering hundreds of miles" [113]; "rearranged to optimize character intercutting, chronology, and suspense" [24]; "it is geometric rather than power law" [63]; "Tolkien begins very small, in the Shire" [161]; "sort of like a Robert Altman film in prose" [160]; "an orchestrator of narrative" [42]; against: "the same structural problems, being sprawling and incoherent" [39]; "bleak and plodding" [40].

What the sources say. The series is organised by two medieval romance structures, interlacement and the diptych [59]; readers jump between interlacing storylines with anticipation built and then broken [49]; weeks pass between chapters and armies cover hundreds of miles offstage, which Whitehead frames as a flaw of pace [113], as is being wrenched from a viewpoint just when settled [112]; the lesser viewpoints' chapters, by contrast, have "little to no time for filler" and feel concise where the major ones sometimes dwell too long [109]; chapters are rearranged after drafting [24]; significant deaths are memoryless in chapter order but not in calendar time, a decoupling of plot from chronology that the authors read as defamiliarisation [63] [64]; Martin took Tolkien's accumulate-then-scatter shape [161] and the mosaic novel's braid [160]. Disagreement: Grossman rates the orchestration above almost any literary novelist [42]; Verhoeve finds the later books sprawling and incoherent [39], Brown finds one bleak and plodding [40], and Hartinger says the overall story has been lost while the chapters stay perfect [235].

Reconstruction rule. The dossier interlaces its strands (the households, the guilds, the faith, the lord) by section, each strand carried forward a little at a time, so that a fact planted under one heading is answered under another. Time may jump between sections, and the jump is stated in plain words (in the next reeve's time; a generation on). The archivist keeps the whole in hand: every strand is closed in the present year.

The disaster section is written to be felt: the names of the dead, what they held, who buried them.

### 36. The screenwriter's inheritance: cut to the muscle, then be expansive

Sources: 1 voice, Martin, across seven outlets. Wikipedia on the cut [25]; Rolling Stone [173]; January Magazine [172] [176]; Outland [202]; Beatrice [203]; NYT ArtsBeat [204]; Hidden Gems [290]; TIFF [170] [171] [174].

True wording: "cutting out the fat and leaving the muscle" [25]; "something that was big and rich and grand in scale" [202]; "cutting and trimming and making scripts into a very tight fit" [203]; "You're the director, you're the special effects coordinator" [204].

What the sources say. Ten years of cutting scripts to forty-six minutes left him wanting breathing room, something big and rich [202] [203]; returning to prose, everything television cannot afford becomes possible because the novelist is director and effects at once [204]; but the final pass is still the screenwriter's cut, which took almost eighty pages out of one book [25] [290]. This is the same tension as feature 10, attested by the author himself.

Reconstruction rule. The dossier is generous in what it covers and strict in how each thing is said. Every civic fact the world holds may enter; no sentence enters that does not carry one.

### 37. Characters are introduced by contrast, fixed by a byname, and known by reputation first

Sources: 5 voices. Weed [302]; Germani [272] [276] [277]; Gelzer-Govatos [93] [325]; the Overthinking It commenter [227]; VanderMeer via Wikipedia [28].

True wording: "slender where Robb was muscular, dark where Robb was fair" [302]; "Gregor Clegane: 17 lines" [272]; "Stannis is mentioned 98 times before he appears" [276]; "can only say his own name" [93] (Hodor); "he does do leitmotifs extremely well" [227].

What the sources say. A character is introduced by paired antitheses against a sibling [302]; bodily detail is psychological shorthand and dialogue reveals hierarchy [277]; a thin character sticks by catchphrase and nickname with very few lines [272]; a great figure is built by others' talk long before appearing [276]. Disagreement: Gelzer-Govatos reads Hodor's and Reek's repeated lines as formula rather than characterisation [93] [325]; the commenter praises the same devices as leitmotif [227].

Reconstruction rule. A dossier's people are introduced by contrast with their kin (the elder brother holds the mill, the younger the ferry, and they do not speak), fixed by the one byname the town uses, and preceded by their reputation: what the town says of the lord comes before what the lord does. No person is given a repeated line as a trait.

### 38. Titles as kennings: two content words, often alliterating

Sources: 1 voice. Neubauer [56] [57] [58]. SINGLE SOURCE.

True wording: "use kenning-like phrases for the titles of his novels" [56]; "four (and two) use alliteration" [57]; "the rather conventional stock of themes and tropes" [58] (of Germanic war verse).

What the source says. Volume titles are two-content-word compounds of action and interaction resembling Old English and Old Norse kennings; four of nine titles and two subtitles alliterate; the imagery draws on the Germanic verse-makers' stock of war themes [56] [57] [58].

Reconstruction rule. A dossier's section headings are two plain nouns joined by a preposition or a conjunction, drawn from the town's own stock, and may alliterate when the alliteration is unforced: the mill and the moot; a tithe of tallow; the ford in flood.

### 39. Cognitive scaling: each viewpoint's world stays near Dunbar's number

Sources: 1 voice. Gessey-Jones and colleagues [62]. SINGLE SOURCE.

True wording: "close to Dunbar's number of 150" [62].

What the source says. The fourteen major viewpoint characters have an average of one hundred and fifty-four contacts within the character network, close to Dunbar's number, which keeps each chapter within a reader's cognitive limit [62].

Reconstruction rule. A dossier names no more people than a reader could hold as a village: the named cast stays within a few score, and each named person is tied to a household, an office or a trade the reader has already met.

## F. Where the register fails (criticism the dossier must design against)

### 40. The sex-scene collapse: cliché arrives when the writer leaves his ground

Sources: 5 voices. Gelzer-Govatos [94] [224]; Streckert at the Portland Mercury [326]; Brown [233]; Martin's own defence via the Mary Sue [209]; CNN via Wikipedia [27].

True wording: "someone being drunk with desire for the other person" [224]; "He is not good at writing about sex." [326]; "I can describe an axe entering a human skull" [209].

What the sources say. The sex scenes drop into clunky, overwrought stock (drunk with desire, the wine of lust) disconnected from the surrounding register [94] [224]; a reviewer finds them fixated on nipple size and crude similes while the violence and heraldry are assured [326]; Brown finds the characters have sex in exactly the same manner whatever their culture [233]. Martin's answer is that only the sex draws complaints, not the axe in the skull [209]; CNN called the descriptions franker than other fantasy [27]. The lesson the sources share is that the cliché appears where the concrete civic and material vocabulary runs out.

Reconstruction rule. The dossier has no such scene, but it has the same hazard wherever its concrete stock runs thin (a faith, a foreign trade, an illness). Where the archivist lacks a material noun he reports the town's practice, not a feeling, and never reaches for a stock figure.

### 41. The prologue as disposable viewpoint, and the refrain the doomed cannot read

Sources: 4 voices. Błaszkiewicz [60]; Shmoop [142]; Attewell [308] [307]; The Fantasy Review [305].

True wording: "perspectives of mysterious people (like Varys or Petyr Baelish)" [142] (denied a viewpoint); "GRRM loves a cheap pun, Chett's downfall is caused by snow" [308]; "the gargoyles that were Bran's handholds transformed into murder weapons" [307].

What the sources say. The prologue supplies what no focaliser can reach through a viewpoint that never returns [60] [142]; a doomed narrator's phrase carries foreshadowing he cannot read, though the phrase is not in fact a refrain on the page [308]; an earlier physical detail is reused as the instrument of later violence [307]; dread is made by negation and double meaning [305].

Reconstruction rule. The dossier's opening paragraph may carry one fact the town itself does not know (the survey that names a spring the town has forgotten), stated in the archivist's calm voice and never referred to again until the section it pays off. A physical detail given early (the gargoyle, the loose stair, the old dyke) is the detail the disaster later turns on.

## G. Measured figures (verified as reported, with their sources)

Sources: 7 voices, each a single reporter of its figure. WordsRated [147]; Rivera [68] [321]; Spathis via Winter Is Coming [69] [322]; AutoCrit [323]; Germani [254] [259] [263] [265]; Gessey-Jones [62] [63]; Voice's corpus abstract [65]; McFarland's roster [76].

Figures. Five books, more than one million seven hundred thousand words in three hundred and forty-four chapters, about five thousand words a chapter [147]. A sixth-grade automated readability index and lexical density between four and four and a half [68] [321]. A reading age of ten against fifteen for Harry Potter [69] [322]. Flesch reading ease of eighty-seven; eight hundred and fifty-one sentences over thirty words; three and a third percent of paragraphs slow; standard and alternative dialogue tags in near-equal number [323]. Italicised thought up about six percent across the series [254]. A passage of one thousand eight hundred and ninety-seven words compressible to four hundred and twenty-seven [259]. About two hundred and fifty zeugmas in one hundred and forty-eight thousand sentences [263]. Three interchangeable semicolons on one page of the first book [265]. Fourteen major viewpoints averaging one hundred and fifty-four contacts [62]; death intervals geometric in chapter time, power-law in story time [63]. A Sheffield corpus study of collocates of she and he across the first four books [65]; a scholarly volume with a section on language and narration [76].

Reconstruction rule. These are the targets a dossier's prose can be measured against: reading ease near eighty-seven, very few sentences over thirty words, adverbs scarce, and a named cast that a village could hold. The engine's own readability walker should report the same figures for a generated dossier.
