Disagreement, explicit: the ultra-minimalist key (Campbell's seven words, bolded nouns after a bar) is answered by Necropraxis (usable, but it lacks poetry: P 714) and by Lux (the classics were terse and had a voice; stripping to the mechanism strips the flavour: 693, 695, 696). Davis names the middle way: economy skillfully applied (701). Gearing's one-word opening sentence (756) shows economy and voice in the same line. Cook adds the one exception: rules text takes no subtext, adventure text leaves the solution unsaid (149). The prose-carries-voice wing now has its pro-length witnesses, verified in round 11. Hartlage argues that a longer description spares the DM inventing details whose first ideas run to the obvious (742), and gives the cutting test: whether the line is the first thing a typical DM would imagine anyway (749). Gearing, rewriting his own adventure three years on, turned its bullet points into prose (754; P 753, the page never says the text had been prose before) and writes a place by closing his eyes and standing in it (757) to find the touchstones that carry its tone (758). Lynch's specificity rules sit on the same side as his terseness and separate specificity from detail: one well-written specific sentence is worth more than a page of details (725), specificity is terse and does not get in the way of the scanning DM (726) and communicates the vibe exactly (727), a parenthetical adjective tells the DM a lot (728), quick adjectives and adverbs on a person land where a paragraph of italic read-aloud fails (729), and more is not better (730); the room text he holds up as the model is cited for its quotation only (P 735: the entry is not verbless throughout). Gus L names the failure of the other extreme: a game text of nothing but fruitful voids is a mediocre prose poem in the shape of an instruction manual or a gazetteer (733). The editor's own testimony is Mohan on Greenwood: for every word given, the reader feels fifty held in reserve (1052), and of every ten he gives, three or four could have been kept back (1053).
Rule: The archivist's sentence is short and every word in it is load-bearing; a page becomes a paragraph, a paragraph a sentence, a sentence a table where a table serves. But the record keeps a voice: a chosen adjective, a one-word opening (Austere.), a dry aside in the chronicle line. Economy is a principle applied with skill, not a word count, and the test for a cut is Hartlage's: a line the reader would have imagined anyway goes; a line only the record could supply stays (749). The archivist states the facts of a matter plainly and leaves conclusions to the reader, and leaves fifty words in reserve behind every one it gives (1052).
