 Should this become the official WikiTree policy on AI generated or enhanced images of people

 Draft Policy: 

 Artificial Intelligence is often used to create imaginative reconstructions of past people or events, in ways that are increasingly difficult to detect. Such images can be widely shared, and there is a real risk that they can be taken as fact. WikiTree is committed to accurate sources and records, and as such requires that the provenance of images of people be labelled as accurately as possible, that AI images be labelled clearly, that originals be included with "enhanced images," and that AI images for decorative purposes be restricted to the close family of profile managers.

 Fully AI generated images of people should never be uploaded as profile images

 AI enhanced images of people are never to be used on pre-1700 profiles

 If an AI enhanced image is uploaded, it needs to be clearly labelled as such, and the original also needs to be uploaded

 Profile managers can add AI enhanced images to their own managed profiles, as long as they are clearly labelled, and an original template image is included 

 AI images of people covered by this policy include wholly AI generated images, as well as "AI Enhanced" or retouched images, especially those that add details and colors, and can change the features of people to have more apparent detail than was present in the original. This is distinct from changing settings like color balance and contrast, but does include enhancements like using Artificial Intelligence tools to interpolate and virtually remove stains and tears, and focus blurred images.

There is a new stated policy on photo manipulation (see comments on the post)

 Brad makes an important technical point that deserves more weight in this discussion.

 Most consumer-facing AI enhancement tools do not work the way traditional photo restoration does. Rather than selectively repairing damaged areas, they use generative models that reconstruct the entire image based on patterns learned from millions of other images. The result is not "the original, minus the damage" — it is a new image containing details that may never have existed. This is not theoretical. A genealogist recently documented a case where AI enhancement of an 1885 family photograph altered a woman's face to the point she was unrecognizable, and removed a brooch and ruffles from her dress — details with genuine genealogical significance.

 This has direct implications for how we evaluate evidence. Elizabeth Shown Mills, in Evidence Explained (4th ed., 2024), establishes that responsible genealogical practice requires tracing every piece of evidence back to its most original form and documenting the chain of custody clearly. An AI-enhanced image is a derivative source — and unlike a transcription error, fabricated visual detail may be completely undetectable without the original to compare against.

 Requiring the original image to accompany any AI-enhanced version on a profile is not an unreasonable burden. It is simply applying the same sourcing standard we already apply to every other kind of evidence on WikiTree.

 I'd welcome responses from those who disagree — particularly whether you are aware of AI enhancement methods that genuinely do not introduce invented detail, and what evidence supports that position.

To insist that such an image must remain blurry forever is to misunderstand the purpose of genealogical work. Genealogy is not museum curation. It is the active reconstruction of family history using every tool available to illuminate the past. If we accept corrections to written documents, why should we reject corrections to visual ones?

 Some critics argue that AI‑enhanced images are “cartoons,” implying that any modification transforms a photograph into fiction. This argument collapses under scrutiny. Photographs have always been interpretive. The moment a shutter clicks, choices are made: lighting, exposure, film stock, camera quality, angle, and timing. A Brownie camera did not capture “truth”—it captured whatever truth its limited technology could manage.

Colorization, too, is not fabrication but interpretation—no different in spirit from historians who reconstruct the colors of ancient statues or artists who restore damaged frescoes. These practices are accepted because they help people understand the past more vividly. Genealogical photographs deserve the same respect.

 Most amateur genealogists—who make up the majority of the field—respond with enthusiasm when they see an improved family photo. A clearer face, a restored smile, or a colorized dress can transform a distant ancestor from an abstract figure into a relatable human being. This emotional connection is not trivial; it is central to why people pursue genealogy at all.

 Purists may insist that only untouched originals are legitimate, but this stance ignores the human dimension of genealogical work. Families want to see their ancestors, not squint at grainy silhouettes. Enhancements help descendants engage with their heritage, share stories, and preserve memories in ways that resonate across generations. A genealogical record that is never viewed, never shared, and never emotionally understood is a record that fails its purpose.

 The fear that enhancement “changes history” misunderstands what genealogists actually do. We do not worship artifacts; we interpret them. When a census taker misspelled a surname, we correct it. When a birth date is wrong, we annotate it. When a photograph is damaged, we restore it. These actions do not corrupt the record—they clarify it.

 AI enhancement is simply the next evolution of this long‑standing tradition. It allows us to correct the limitations of outdated technology just as we correct the limitations of human scribes. The original image remains preserved, documented, and available for reference. The enhanced version is an aid, not a replacement—just as a transcription is an aid to a handwritten document.

 Genealogy thrives when it embraces new tools. Digitization, OCR indexing, DNA analysis, and online databases were all once controversial. Today they are indispensable. AI‑based image enhancement belongs in the same category: a powerful tool that expands access, improves clarity, and deepens engagement.

 To reject enhanced images outright is to cling to technological nostalgia rather than historical accuracy. Genealogy is not harmed by clearer photographs; it is strengthened by them.

 Enhanced photographs—whether colorized, sharpened, or restored with AI—are not “cartoons.” They are legitimate genealogical tools that follow the same principles used to correct written records. They preserve history, increase clarity, and help families connect emotionally with their ancestors. Genealogy is the pursuit of understanding, not the preservation of flaws. A blurry Brownie snapshot may be the starting point, but it need not be the final word.

 Brad,

 Agree. Full disclosure: I am pro-AI. I believe these tools, used ethically and responsibly with a human researcher in the loop, are genuinely valuable as a co-working resource in genealogy. It is precisely because I hold that view — not in spite of it — that I think a thoughtful community statement is so important. Getting the guardrails right matters most when the technology actually works.

 We are not alone in recognizing this need. Wikipedia has already established guidelines for AI use in its collaborative environment ( https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Artificial_intelligence ) — a useful model given WikiTree's own wiki-based, community-driven structure.

 In the professional genealogy space, the Coalition for Responsible AI in Genealogy (CRAIGEN) ( https://craigen.org/ ) has articulated five guiding principles that I believe should inform any WikiTree policy: 

 Accuracy (verifying AI-generated content against source records), 

 Disclosure (acknowledging when AI materially influences research or content), 

 Privacy (safeguarding private information when using AI tools), 

 Education (understanding both the benefits and risks of AI), and 

 Compliance (respecting intellectual property, terms of service, and data privacy regulations). 

 These principles map well onto WikiTree's existing commitment to sourced, collaborative research.

 The Board for Certification of Genealogists (BCG) has also weighed in, specifying that work submitted in new CG certification portfolios must be entirely the applicant's own and may not be written or generated by AI. BCG acknowledges the numerous benefits of AI in genealogy and encourages its responsible use, but draws a clear line around authorship and professional integrity — a distinction WikiTree's statement should also address.

 ICAPGen , by contrast, allows limited AI use for feedback with disclosure in research logs — illustrating that different professional bodies are arriving at nuanced, context-specific positions rather than blanket prohibitions. A WikiTree statement should similarly distinguish between AI-assisted search and transcription tools (generally acceptable), AI-generated profile content (requiring disclosure), and AI-fabricated genealogical conclusions (clearly contrary to our sourcing standards).

 The broader concern — for WikiTree specifically — is the integrity of the shared tree. AI tools that generate plausible but unverified family connections could propagate errors at scale. Any policy we adopt should reinforce, not weaken, WikiTree's core commitment to sourced, human-vetted genealogy .

 I encourage the community to draw on these existing professional frameworks. I fully endorse your proposal. Thank you for taking a stand on this.
