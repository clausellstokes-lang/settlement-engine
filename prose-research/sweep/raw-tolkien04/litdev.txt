The philologist and fantasy author [[J. R. R. Tolkien]] made use of multiple '''literary devices in ''The Lord of the Rings''''', from its [[Narrative structure of The Lord of the Rings|narrative structure]] and [[Pseudotranslation in The Lord of the Rings|its use of pseudotranslation]] and [[Editorial framing of The Lord of the Rings|editorial framing]], to [[Character pairing in The Lord of the Rings|character pairing]] and the deliberate cultivation of an [[Impression of depth in The Lord of the Rings|impression of depth]] while [[Constructing The Lord of the Rings|constructing the novel]]. The narrative structure in particular has been seen as a pair of quests, a sequence of [[Tableau vivant|tableaux]] (static scenes), a complex edifice, multiple spirals, and a [[Interlacing in The Lord of the Rings|medieval-style interlacing]]. The first volume, ''[[The Fellowship of the Ring]]'', on the other hand, has a single narrative thread, and repeated episodes of danger and recuperation in five "Homely Houses". [[Tolkien's prose style|His prose style]], too, has been both criticised and defended.

== Context ==

{{further|J. R. R. Tolkien|The Lord of the Rings}}

[[J. R. R. Tolkien]] (1892–1973) was an English [[Catholic Church|Roman Catholic]] writer, poet, [[philology|philologist]], and academic, best known as the author of the [[high fantasy]] works ''[[The Hobbit]]'' and ''[[The Lord of the Rings]]''.<ref>{{cite book |last=Carpenter |first=Humphrey |author-link=Humphrey Carpenter |title=J. R. R. Tolkien: A Biography |title-link=J. R. R. Tolkien: A Biography |year=1978 |orig-year=1977 |publisher=[[Unwin Paperbacks]] |isbn=978-0-04928-039-7 |pages=111, 200, 266 and throughout}}</ref>

''The Lord of the Rings'' was published in 1954–55; it was awarded the [[International Fantasy Award]] in 1957. The publication of the [[Ace Books]] and [[Ballantine Books]] paperbacks in the United States helped it to become immensely popular with a new generation in the 1960s. The book has remained so ever since, ranking as one of the most popular works of fiction of the twentieth century, judged by both sales and reader surveys.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Seiler |first=Andy |date=December 16, 2003 |title='Rings' comes full circle |url=https://www.usatoday.com/life/movies/news/2003-12-12-lotr-main_x.htm |access-date=5 August 2020 |website=[[USA Today]]}}</ref> In the 2003 "[[Big Read]]" survey conducted by the [[BBC]] in the United Kingdom, ''The Lord of the Rings'' was found to be the "Nation's best-loved book." In similar 2004 polls, both Germany<ref>{{Cite web |last=Diver |first=Krysia |date=5 October 2004 |title=A lord for Germany |url=http://www.smh.com.au/articles/2004/10/04/1096871805007.html?from=storyrhs |access-date=7 August 2020 |website=[[The Sydney Morning Herald]]}}</ref> and Australia<ref>{{cite web |last=Cooper |first=Callista |date=4 December 2005 |title=Epic trilogy tops favourite film poll |url=https://www.abc.net.au/news/2005-12-05/epic-trilogy-tops-favourite-film-poll/754688 |access-date=31 July 2026 |website=[[ABC News (Australia)|ABC News]] Online |archive-url =https://web.archive.org/web/20071221030130/https://www.abc.net.au/news/newsitems/200512/s1523327.htm |archive-date=21 December 2007 |url-status=live}}</ref> also found ''The Lord of the Rings'' to be their favourite book. In a 1999 poll of [[Amazon.com]] customers, ''The Lord of the Rings'' was judged to be their favourite "book of the millennium."<ref>{{Cite web |last=O'Hehir |first=Andrew |date=4 June 2001 |title=The book of the century |url=http://www.salon.com/books/feature/2001/06/04/tolkien/ |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20010610164904/http://www.salon.com/books/feature/2001/06/04/tolkien/ |archive-date=10 June 2001 |access-date=7 August 2020 |website=[[Salon.com]]}}</ref> The popularity of ''The Lord of the Rings'' increased further when [[The Lord of the Rings (film series)|Peter Jackson's film trilogy]] came out in 2001–2003.<ref name="Curry 2020">{{harvnb|Curry|2020|pp=369–388}}</ref>

== Narrative structure ==

{{main|Narrative structure of The Lord of the Rings}}

=== ''The Fellowship of the Ring'' ===

The first volume, ''[[The Fellowship of the Ring]]'', has a different structure from the rest of the novel. It has attracted attention both for its sequence of five "Homely Houses", safe places where the [[Hobbit]] protagonists may recuperate after a dangerous episode,{{sfn|Shippey|2001|p=65}}<ref name="Turner 2001">{{Cite journal |last=Turner |first=Jenny |date=15 November 2001 |title=Reasons for Liking Tolkien |url=https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v23/n22/jenny-turner/reasons-for-liking-tolkien |journal=[[London Review of Books]] |volume=23 |issue=22}}</ref> and for its arrangement as a single narrative thread focused on its [[protagonist]], [[Frodo Baggins|Frodo]], interrupted by two long but critically important [[Flashback (narrative)|flashback narrative]] chapters, "[[The Shadow of the Past]]" and "[[The Council of Elrond]]".{{sfn|Flieger|2001|p=21}}<ref name="Nepveu 2009">{{cite web |last=Nepveu |first=Kate |title=LotR re-read: Fellowship II.2, 'The Council of Elrond' |url=https://www.tor.com/2009/03/27/lotr-re-read-fellowship-ii2-the-council-of-elrond/ |website=[[Tor.com]] |access-date=3 December 2021 |date=27 March 2009}}</ref>

[[File:Frodo's Five Homely Houses.svg|thumb|upright=2|center|Tolkien's descriptions of [[Frodo Baggins|Frodo]]'s five "Homely Houses",{{sfn|Shippey|2001|p=65}} (house icons) alternating with places of danger (swords icons), form a repetitive structure for the first part of ''[[The Fellowship of the Ring]]''.]]

=== The work as a whole ===

Scholars have described the narrative structure of ''The Lord of the Rings'' in a variety of ways, including as a balanced pair of outer and inner [[Quests in Middle-earth|quests]];{{sfn|Birns|2012}} a linear sequence of scenes or [[Tableau vivant|tableaux]];{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=1–3, 12–13, 25–34, 41, 57}} a [[fractal]] arrangement of separate episodes;{{sfn|Risden|2011|pp=70–83}} a [[Gothic architecture|Gothic cathedral-like edifice]] of many different elements;{{sfn|Risden|2011|pp=70–83}}  multiple cycles or spirals;{{sfn|Miller|1975|pp=95–106}} or an elaborate [[medieval]]-style [[Interlacing in The Lord of the Rings|interlacing]] of intersecting threads of story.{{sfn|West|1975|pp=77–94}} Also present is an elaborate symmetry between pairs of characters.{{sfn|Shippey|2001|pp=50–52, 96}} 

[[File:Rosebury's analysis of Lord of the Rings.svg|thumb|upright=1.5|center|Diagram of [[Brian Rosebury]]'s analysis of ''The Lord of the Rings'' as a combined quest (to destroy [[One Ring|the Ring]]) and journey (as a series of Tableaux of places in [[Middle-earth]]); the two support each other, and must interlock tightly to do so.{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=1–3, 12–13, 25–34, 41, 57}}]]

=== Interlacing ===

{{main|Interlacing in The Lord of the Rings}}

The narrative interlacing in ''The Lord of the Rings'', also called by the French term ''entrelacement'', is an unusual and complex narrative structure, known from medieval literature, that enabled Tolkien to achieve a variety of literary effects. These include maintaining suspense, keeping the reader uncertain of what will happen and even of what is happening to other characters at the same time in the story; creating surprise and an ongoing feeling of bewilderment and disorientation. More subtly, the leapfrogging of the timeline in ''The Lord of the Rings'' by the different story threads allows Tolkien to make hidden connections that can only be grasped retrospectively, as the reader realises on reflection that certain events happened at the same time, and that these connections imply a contest of good and evil powers.{{sfn|West|1975|pp=77–94}}<ref name="Thomson 1967">{{cite journal |last=Thomson |first=George H. |title=''The Lord of the Rings'': The Novel as Traditional Romance |journal=[[Wisconsin Studies in Contemporary Literature]] |volume=8 |issue=1 |year=1967 |pages=43–59 |doi=10.2307/1207129 |jstor=1207129 }}</ref> 

[[File:Orlando Furioso 20.jpg|thumb|center|Tolkien disliked the French and Italian interlaced romances, like ''[[Orlando Furioso]]'', but used their technique anyway.{{sfn|Shippey|2005|pp=181–183}} Illustration of  	
[[Ruggiero (character)|Ruggiero]] rescuing [[Angelica (character)|Angelica]] for ''Orlando Furioso'' by [[Gustave Doré]], 19th century]]

The Tolkien scholar [[Tom Shippey]] comments that it is curious that Tolkien used this literary device, as he favoured "northern" literature over French or later Italian epics like [[Ariosto]]'s ''[[Orlando Furioso]]. ''All the same, Shippey writes, Tolkien's use of the technique is far more tightly structured than that of the Medieval romances.{{sfn|Shippey|2005|pp=181–183}}

== Character pairing ==

{{main|Character pairing in The Lord of the Rings}}

Tolkien used [[Character pairing in The Lord of the Rings|character pairing]] to express some of the [[Morality|moral]] complexity of his major characters in ''The Lord of the Rings''.{{sfn|Attebery|2012|p=87}}{{sfn|Burns|2005|pp=91–127}}{{sfn|Caughey|2020|pp=404–417}} Commentators have noted that the format of a [[fantasy]] does not lend itself to subtlety of characterisation, but that pairing allows inner tensions to be expressed as linked opposites, including, in a [[psychoanalytic]] interpretation, those of Jungian archetypes. Major pairings include those between the Hobbits [[Frodo]], [[Sam Gamgee|Sam]], and [[Gollum]], the three of them linked by the Ring, by friendship, and by bonds of loyalty and of oath. This enables Tolkien to portray the good and evil sides of Frodo's character.{{sfn|Burns|2005|pp=91–127}}{{sfn|Bettridge|1990|loc=Article 5}}<ref name="Hall 2020">{{cite web |last=Hall |first=Alaric |author-link=Alaric Hall |title=The One Ring |url=https://www.alarichall.org.uk/lotr/lecture7.htm |publisher=Alaric Hall<!--expert, director of the Institute for Medieval Studies at University of Leeds--> |access-date=5 August 2020}}</ref> The [[Heroism in The Lord of the Rings|unheroic Frodo is further contrasted]] with the plainly heroic [[Aragorn]]. Among the kingly figures, the unhappy [[Steward of Gondor]], [[Denethor]], is paired both with the future king Aragorn{{sfn|Chance|1980|pp=119-122}} and with the bold king of [[Rohan (Middle-earth)|Rohan]], [[Théoden]].{{sfn|Solopova|2009|pp=28–29}}{{sfn|Shippey|2005|pp=136–137, 175–181, 187}} Pairings operate, too, among supporting characters, such as that between the elf-queen [[Galadriel]] and the giant spider [[Shelob]], light opposing darkness.{{sfn|Burns|2005|pp=91–127}} Patrick Grant, a scholar of [[Renaissance]] literature, interpreted the interactions of the characters as fitting the oppositions and other pairwise relationships of [[Jungian archetypes]], recurring psychological symbols proposed by [[Carl Jung]].{{sfn|Grant|1973|pp=365-380}}

[[File:Patrick Grant's Jungian View of LOTR.svg|thumb|upright=1.5|center|Diagram of Patrick Grant's [[Carl Jung|Jungian]] view of [[Heroism in The Lord of the Rings|''The Lord of the Rings'' with hero]], anima and other [[Jungian archetypes|archetypes]]{{sfn|Grant|1973|pp=365-380}}]]

== Impression of depth ==

{{main|Impression of depth in The Lord of the Rings}}

Tolkien deliberately sought to create the aesthetic effect of impression of depth in ''The Lord of the Rings''. It was intended to give the reader the feeling that the work had "deep roots in the past",<ref name="Monsters Sir Gawain"/> and hence that it was attractively authentic.{{sfn|Shippey|2005|pp=259–261}} The effect was constructed on at least four factors, namely the enormous scale of ''The Lord of the Rings'' and the amount of background detail, including [[Tolkien's maps|maps]] and [[Tolkien's Middle-earth family trees|genealogies]]; the apparently casual and incomplete mentions of this background; multiple inconsistent accounts, as in real history; and writing different texts in varying styles.{{sfn|Drout|Hitotsubashi|Scavera|2014|pp=167–211}} Scholars have noted some of [[Tolkien and the medieval|Tolkien's medieval antecedents]] in the effect, such as ''[[Beowulf]]'' and ''[[Sir Gawain and the Green Knight]]'', books which he had studied<!-- intensively--> and translated.{{sfn|Shippey|2005|pp=181–183, 259–261, 351–352}} Fantasy authors such as [[Ursula K. Le Guin]] and [[J. K. Rowling]] have to an extent [[Tolkien's impact on fantasy|followed Tolkien]] in using the technique.{{sfn|Drout|Hitotsubashi|Scavera|2014|pp=167–211}}{{sfn|Sas|2019|loc=Article 9}}

[[File:Gawain and the Green Knight.jpg|thumb|upright|center|Tolkien admired the impression of depth in ''[[Sir Gawain and the Green Knight]]''.<ref name="Monsters Sir Gawain">{{harvnb|Tolkien|1983|p=72, "Sir Gawain and the Green Knight"}}</ref> Illustration from the medieval manuscript]]

== Prose style ==

{{main|Tolkien's prose style}}

Tolkien's prose style in ''The Lord of the Rings'' is remarkably varied and "invitational" to the reader.{{sfn|Walker|2009|pp=7–11, 171}} Commentators have noted that Tolkien selected [[linguistic register]]s to suit different peoples, such as simple and modern for Hobbits and more archaic for [[Dwarf (Middle-earth)|Dwarves]], [[Elf (Middle-earth)|Elves]], and [[Riders of Rohan]].{{sfn|Shippey|2005|pp=134–138}} This allowed him to use the Hobbits to mediate between the modern reader and the heroic and archaic realm of fantasy.{{sfn|Shippey|2005|p=259}} The [[Orc]]s, too, are depicted in different voices: the Orc-leader [[Grishnákh]] speaks in [[bullying]] tones, while the minor functionary [[Gorbag]] uses grumbling modern speech. {{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=81–83}} 

[[Brian Rosebury]] considers Tolkien's portrayal of Gollum his "most memorable success", sharply defining him with an "extraordinary idiolect", a unique way of speaking.{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=81–83}} This encompasses "obsessive repetition", "undeveloped syntax and unstable sense of grammatical person, sufficient to imply [[Mental illness in Middle-earth|dissociative mental illness]].{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=81–83}} The language is, Rosebury suggests, partly modelled on "the playful, wheedling, sentimental argot of the nursery" with its frequent use of "nice" and "nasty" and phrases like "little hobbitses".{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=81–83}} He comments that the effect is to depict Gollum as morally deformed "like ... an unregenerate child grown old, in whom the unattractive infant qualities of selfishness, cruelty and self-pitying are monstrously preserved and isolated."{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=81–83}}

Tolkien's prose style was attacked by scholars of literature such as [[Catharine R. Stimpson]]{{sfn|Stimpson|1969|p=29}} and [[Burton Raffel]] in the 20th century.{{sfn|Raffel|1968|pp=218–246}} Raffel criticised the work's style, the embedded poetry, the characterisation, what he called the "manipulatory" use of incident, and the implied Christian morality.{{sfn|Raffel|1968|pp=218–246}}  It has more recently been analysed more favourably, both by other novelists such as [[Ursula Le Guin]],{{sfn|Le Guin|2001}} and by scholars such as Rosebury{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=71–88}} and Shippey. Where Stimpson called Tolkien's diction needlessly complex, Rosebury argues that even in the example she chose, Tolkien was as plain and simple as [[Ernest Hemingway]].{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=71–88}} He analyses a passage where Merry has just helped to kill the [[Witch-King of Angmar|Witch-King]].{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=81–83}} Tolkien begins this in plain language, modulating into a higher register to deal with the echoes of ancient and magical history.{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|pp=71–88}} More recently, scholars have applied [[corpus linguistics]] to analyse his text quantitatively.{{sfn|Kullmann|Siepmann|2021|pp=39–70}}

== Pseudotranslation and editorial framing ==

{{main|Editorial framing of The Lord of the Rings}}

A [[pseudotranslation]] is a text written as if it had been translated from a foreign language. Tolkien used it in ''The Lord of the Rings'' for two reasons: to help resolve the linguistic puzzle he had accidentally created by using real-world languages within his legendarium,{{sfn|Turner|2007|p=330}}{{sfn|Shippey|2005|pp=131-133}} and to lend realism by supporting a [[found manuscript]] conceit [[Tolkien's frame stories|to frame his story]].{{sfn|Turner|2011a|p=18}}

Effectively, Tolkien pretends to be an editor who has received an ancient manuscript, the ''[[Red Book of Westmarch]]'', written in [[Westron]], the Common Speech of [[Middle-earth]], annotated and edited by many hands, which he [[Pseudotranslation in The Lord of the Rings|undertakes to translate]] into English.<ref name="Flieger 2005 Bilbo's book">{{harvnb|Flieger|2005|pp=67–73 "A great big book with red and black letters"}}</ref> The manuscript supposedly contains names and words from [[Languages constructed by J. R. R. Tolkien|other languages]], some of them related to Westron; he pretends he has translated those into languages related to English, namely [[Old English]] and [[Old Norse]].{{sfn|Shippey|2005|pp=131-133}}{{sfn|Carpenter|2023|loc=#144 to [[Naomi Mitchison]], 25 April 1954}}{{sfn|Brljak|2010|pp=1–34}} 

Tolkien then goes much further, constructing an elaborate [[Editorial framing of The Lord of the Rings|editorial framing of the entire book]], constructing a prologue and multiple appendices that all collude to support the found manuscript conceit, and indeed to construct a secondary world that appears real and solid because of the interlocking evidence from many different technical points of view. These include genealogies, ancient annals complete with scribal footnotes and editorial comments, even a discussion "On Translation" about how it has proven best to handle the complexities of the translation into modern English.{{sfn|Turner|2011a|pp=15–17}}{{sfn|Brooke-Rose|1981|p=247}}{{sfn|Carpenter|2023|loc=#281 to [[Rayner Unwin]], 15 December 1965}}
