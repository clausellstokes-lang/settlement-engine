The Shadowed Old Gods of Game Design: Remembering Holmes, Moldvay and Mentzer

Posted on November 11, 2025 by Serban Ionita

History tends to flatten itself. Nuance becomes nostalgia, multiplicity turns into myth. The history of tabletop role-playing games is no exception. And the history of tabletop role-playing games is no different, for its creation myth usually has has two names engraved in gold: Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson. Rightful twin creators of Dungeons & Dragons, the legendary duo whose collaboration (and later discord) gave rise to everything else which followed.

Creation, though, is not the same as cultivation. In addition to those two giants, there are others – perhaps not as imposing, but quieter, and now less visible – but still as crucial to the process of transitioning the clay that Gygax and Arneson molded, into something players could hold and love and take back to their communities and their world. The ones I am going to talk about today are not the only such quiet figures, but the ones I taught were most important to open this discussion. And these lucky few are: J. Eric Holmes, Tom Moldvay, and Frank Mentzer – three non-giants, whose work paved the way for how generations of players learned, played, and felt in the world of Dungeons & Dragons.

Unfortunately now viewed by the general public more as footnotes than famous names, and even more tragic, often lost in the larger narrative of the hobby’s history, Holmes, Moldvay, and Mentzer had a substantial and lasting impact on the game, nevertheless. Holmes provided clarity and tone, Moldvay furnished elegant structure and warmth, and Mentzel expanded access and gave it heart and soul. Together, they, along with Gygax and Arneson gave context and connectivity to an amateur experiment and transformed it into a living tradition, a cultural artifact that could endure long after its founders had passed.

So yeah, without further ado, let’s take a closer look at these three individuals and why they should still be remembered!

The Translotar: J. Eric Holmes and the Bridge Between Worlds

Becoming a famous game designer was somewhat of a surprising turn of events for Holmes. He was a neurologist and a professor, born in 1930, and had an enduring interest in pulp fiction, spending as much time crafting stories of science fiction as he spent dissecting the human brain. He published short stories in The Blue Book Magazine, and he wrote analyses of Burroughs and Lovecraft long before anyone had heard of D&D. Both the scientist and the fantasist were at the core of who he was. Holmes operated under the premise that creativity and logic were not opposites, but collaborators. His ability to bridge these two worlds would be his greatest contribution to the hobby.

J. Eric Holmes appeared at a time when D&D was in danger of succumbing to the weight of its own possibilities. The original boxed set was groundbreaking in its own right, but was equally messy. The text in the set was dense and peculiar, and presumed that the audience already lived and breathed wargaming. For the inexperienced reader, it would be akin to a maze without a string. Holmes approached the context with the focus of a scientist and the excitement of a fan. In his 1977 Basic Dungeons & Dragons, he translated the swirling complexity of early editions into reader-friendly text.

Holmes didn’t alter the substance of the game. Instead he tried to enrich its essence. His prose is dispassionate, methodical, and encouraging. The experience of reading the Holmes Basic Set conjures an image of sitting next to a patient instructor rather than being lectured to by an arcane master. Holmes focused on clarity of explanation and accessibility of imagination. For many players of the late 1970s, Holmes’ set was their first contact with the hobby. It is the edition that made the odd comprehensible and the complex approachable.

But Holmes’ contribution was not simply pedagogical, but he subtly altered tone. His D&D reads like a pulp novel that has been vetted by a scholar, full of charm, wonder and a little bit of the absurd. The implied setting he provides, one echoing Vance, Lovecraft and Burroughs, is almost dreamlike in its distance from medievalism. Though tone is often unmeasured, it was important. It allowed D&D to embrace science-fantasy. It allowed magic swords to share the same kingdom with ray guns. It allowed the value of irony and an expansion of genre beyond pseudo-historical settings to flourish.

The work of Holmes tied these two worlds together: the inaccessible genius of early design penned by Gygax and Arneson and the simple, sleek adventure game that D&D would become. All of the readable, friendly rulebooks that have been published since owe something to his restraint and clarity. He taught us, in a sense, how to play, and how to teach play.

The poet: Tom Moldvay and the Art of Elegance

Tom Moldvay, by all accounts, was a quiet kind of genius. He was born in 1948, and he was deeply smitten with science fiction and anthropology, with a particularly strong interest in myth, folklore, and linguistics. He was part of TSR during its youth, where he would write not just rules, but whole worlds. His friends described him as introspective and intensely creative, possessing a dry wit, and a habit of seeing patterns in everything. He collected old myths and pulp magazines like people collect postcards, always searching for connective tissue in the stories. That scholar’s inquisitiveness, that ravenous desire to synthesize, was his design philosophy.

If Holmes was the translator and Mentzer the educator, then Moldvay was the poet. Moldvay’s Basic D&D set from 1981 would endure, for many, as the defining version of the game. Not for its completeness, however, but for its perfect balance between wonder and structure. Moldvay’s text seems both efficient and lyrical. In only a couple dozen pages he would pare the experience of fantasy adventure down to a text that read like a myth and played like a machine.

What makes Moldvay unique is his design philosophy. He recognized that rules are more than mechanics, that rules are language. His D&D was meant to be played rather than simply read and every rule serves that intent. Character creation is fast, treasure tables are meant to piques curiosity, and encounters reward creativity rather than strength. The outcome is a game that engenders exploration, consequence, and imagination. The pedagogy is by rhythm rather than by lecture and offers traffic to the players through action toward discovery.

However, Moldvay’s real legacy is tone. His rulebook overflows with warmth. It welcomes you in, without claims of glorious adventure, but rather, the promise of mystery. It suggests that the dungeon is not an unbearable death-trap, but a dialogue between the player, the referee, and the world. This spirit would, decades later, come back into trend with the OSR movement, particularly embodied in games like Old-School Essentials and Cairn that are purposefully returning to Moldvay-like principles: simplicity, elegance, and curiosity as design values.

His subsequent works on Isle of Dread and The Lost City demonstrated his skill for merging mechanics and myth. These adventures communicated worldviews, rather than merely offering maps. Each adventure implied a cosmology, a history, and a mood built-in by its placement of statues, monsters, and ruins. Moldvay showed that RPG design can mirror minimalism and still create infinite depth. He turned minimalism into poetry.

The Teacher: Frank Mentzer and the Human Face of D&D

Frank Mentzer was not only a designer. He was, in many ways, a missionary of play. After joining TSR in the early 1980s, Mentzer swiftly became one of the most active authors in the Basic and Expert lines of D&D. Unlike Gygax, whose prose resembled a baroque sermon, or Arneson, whose games prospered on organic chaos, Mentzer possessed a gentleness born of patience when dealing with the game, because no matter what, he was a teacher. His day job before TSR was in education and training, and that sensibility seeps through every part of every word he ever wrote for our hobby.

He wrote the incomparable BECMI boxed sets (Basic, Expert, Companion, Master, and Immortal), which stands as perhaps the most approachable iteration of D&D in a boxed set. Each of the 5 boxes was more akin to a lesson plan, than your usual rule-set. The Basic Set explained what a dungeon was and how to survive it, the Expert Set opened the world into wilderness and domain play, the Companion and Master Sets scaled that into governance, and finally the Immortal set scaled everything to literal godhood. It was a slow climb through layers of myth, but every ascent was paired with a gentle hand.

Mentzer’s brilliance was found in empathy. He never wrote for him, he wrote for you. His rulebooks were cozy, chatty, and super personable. The complex ideas of the game were made simple by breaking them down in an engaging conversation of player to instructor, even better, often anticipating confusion before it even arose. Reading his D&D literally feels like leaning over to hear your older sibling explain how the dice work. Mentzer, in almost all facets, was D&D’s humanizing agent. He stripped away the intimidation, and restored the magic, the sense of umbilical mastery, the discovery through play.

And beyond simple structure, was vision. More than a hobby, Mentzer, in a similar way to Arneson, saw the game as an engine for imagination, community, and creativity. He infamously created The Known World, which was later branded Mystara, and embodied optimism, exploration, and joy at the prospect of possibility. It was not as grim as Greyhawk, and perhaps less high-fantasy than Forgotten Realms, but in some ways, more human than both. It was Mentzer: curious, kind, quietly brilliant.

If Gygax and Arneson were the fathers of D&D, then Mentzer was the teacher raising it to maturity. In addition to rules, he gave us the lesson (and the meta-rule) of operating within them.

Forgotten Pantheon and the Living Legacy

Speaking of these three feels for me as if speaking of a forgotten pantheon. In our cultural memory, Gygax and Arneson are twin gods of creation, but a pantheon needs balance. Holmes provided vocabulary and clarity. Moldvay provided elegance and coherence. Mentzer provided empathy and structure. Collectively, they articulate the holy trinity of D&D’s early creative spirit.

It is easy to forget them-given that their virtues are in a way more quiet. They are not exalted through branding or personality cults. They are offered through design decisions, through the shape of the dungeon map, through the cadence of the GM’s voice.

I’d argue that, every modern game-designer who values minimalism, accessibility, and warmth owes them at least a modicum of debt. The indie movement that cherishes elegant text and fluidity of play, lives on their shoulders. The OSR revitalization of engagement with early rule sets for philosophical meaning is their legacy in motion. Every time we strip back the fat from the system and invite ourselves back to the pure enjoyment of exploration and choice, we are in communion with their design spirit.

Even if history obscured their names somewhat, their ideas have survived. They are the gods of quiet creation, muses to the handmade map, those who built a frame for the temple long before anyone thought to worship at it. And every time the dice land on the table, I like to believe that they are present, somehow, in the resonance of Chaos. Thank you for reading!
