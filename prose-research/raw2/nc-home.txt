 I am very pleased to announce that Wyrm Publishing is currently taking US-based* preorders for The Best Science Fiction of the Year: 2025 . It will be published in hardcover, trade paperback, and epub editions in November. We’re ahead of the distributors on this one, but it will also be available for preorders from bookstores in a few weeks. (If you are a bookseller who wants to order direct from us, drop me a line.)  AI and Short Fiction #2 – Some recommendations for editors 

 By Neil Clarke 

 On 07/16/2026 

 In editing , generative ai 

 I wanted to get today’s installment out there quickly because I’ve been seeing a lot of publishers/editors putting themselves at risk.

 I will preface this with the fact that I am not a lawyer. 

 Recently, a number of editors and publishers have gone out of their way to announce how they’ve been deceived by someone who likely used generative “AI” to write some or all of a work they published. I’m going to address some of the things that could have prevented that from happening in future posts, but today I want to address the unnecessary risk these publishers are putting themselves at by naming names.

 The use of generative “AI” in submissions is not the same as plagiarism. As a field, we’ve historically shared information about plagiarists to prevent them from successfully landing those works with one of our colleagues. I consider generative “AI” to be a theft-based technology, but it has one important difference from plagiarism: proving it to a legal standard is significantly more complex. Very few will leave a prompt in or admit what they’ve done. They are often willing to sign legal documents that say otherwise. It’s all part of the scam.

 It’s ok to reject a submission or withdraw a published work if you think it was created with generative “AI.” It’s even ok to privately tell someone you suspect their work was partially- or fully-generated. The trouble begins when you share that suspicion with third parties. Calling them out in public or colluding privately with other editors or publishers puts you (and possibly those you spoke to privately) at risk.

 A determined individual called out in this manner could accuse you of defamation and state that you are actively undermining their ability to work in the field. I’ve heard from universities that are already dealing with cases where accused students are showing up with lawyers threatening legal action over such claims. In publishing, the recent controversy over Mia Ballard’s canceled novel Shy Girl , has resulted in the author suing Hachette, her former publisher, over their “AI” claims. It won’t stop there.

 For your own sake, say nothing. I didn’t say do nothing. Remove the works. Reject them. Ban them. It’s your right to do so. Just do so silently. Maintain your confidentiality policies and protect yourself. A work vanishing from your site is not an admission or proof of guilt. There are many reasons that can happen. I’ve had to remove stories from Clarkesworld to protect an author’s mental health, to satisfy an author’s agent who (incorrectly) thought online stories would prevent a collection from selling, to respect an author’s attempt to prevent it from being used for targeted LLM training, to honor author-specific contract clauses, and a host of other non-incriminating reasons.

 If the author wants to explain what happened, that’s up to them. Heck, if they want to say that you accused them of using “AI” that’s fine too. Responding to that publicly, however, could be a problem. I’m a big supporter of transparency, but some things need to be kept behind closed doors for your own safety.

 And finally, one last reason to stay silent…

 You might be wrong. 

 If you are a lawyer and would like to chime in, please do so.

 AI and Short Fiction #1 – Some recommendations for readers 

 By Neil Clarke 

 On 07/14/2026 

 In generative ai 

 I’ve been working on a series of posts that have to do with issues related to “AI” and short fiction (particularly in light of some events of the last month) that I plan to post as I finish them. I’m a slow writer, so this could take some time. First up, one aimed at the general public. This is the easy one.

 So something you read feels like it was written with “AI”… What should you do to be helpful?

 YOU DON’T HAVE TO DO ANYTHING. But if you want to let them know, contact them directly by email, letter, DMs, or any other method that isn’t public (perhaps not letter tied to a rock and thrown through their window). Contact information is often on their website. Tell them why you think it was generated. 

 DON’T POST ABOUT IT ON SOCIAL MEDIA. Social media is the opposite of helpful. Consider for a second that you might actually be wrong and could be doing reputational damage to an innocent person. Instead of being able to focus on identifying the truth of what happened, the publisher will also have to deal with the pressure of managing an upset and impatient online community. The added attention can get in the way of a fair investigation, place some parties on the defensive prematurely, or even cause harm. 

 BE PATIENT. A proper investigation will take time to gather and analyze materials, talk to all parties involved, come to a conclusion, and take action (if necessary). Some might even want to seek legal counsel before acting or making a public statement. Also keep in mind that the parties involved may be living in disparate time zones, dealing with illnesses, or using different languages. This will slow communication. 

 ACCEPT THAT YOU MIGHT NOT BE TOLD THE FINDINGS. If the work is pulled (assuming it can be), it doesn’t necessarily mean that the accused has admitted wrongdoing or been judged to have used “AI.” It’s entirely possible for these situations to end with the accused withdrawing their work to avoid the sideshow that often erupts around these events or just out of the sheer embarrassment of being accused. Sometimes people act to preserve their own mental health rather than be part of a spectacle. If the publisher doesn’t want to disclose their conclusions, that’s entirely fair. The legal standard for proof and the standard for what is good enough for the editor to take action can very different. There could be potential legal consequences for calling someone out (something you could be at risk of too, if you go public) or the publisher may simply be following their own confidentiality policies. 

 ACCEPT THAT YOU MIGHT BE WRONG. It happens. Don’t try to force that you’re right, even if you think they came to the wrong conclusion. You’ve done your part. Disagree and move on or move away. Life’s too short. 

 And in some rare cases… 

 YOU MAY DISCOVER THEY DO NOT CARE. There are a handful of publishers that are ok with generated slop. Horrifying, I know. At the bare minimum, they should be transparent about what they are doing and own it, but that’s not always the case. I won’t advise you on what to do next.

 2025 Clarkesworld Submission Stats 

 By Neil Clarke 

 On 01/27/2026 

 In clarkesworld magazine , slush , stats 

 In 2025, we received 14,805 submissions and averaged 1,233 per month. That’s an increase of roughly 130 per month over the prior year.

 This data does not include the “AI”-generated submissions that resulted in bans, but does include works we consider suspicious. These are stories that we believe may have been “assisted” or translated by generative models, but weren’t certain enough to issue a ban. Instead, the author is flagged for increased scrutiny on future submissions. We don’t want to waste too much time researching each of these, so when uncertain we’re happy to just reject them and move on. The number of these submissions increased by… roughly 130 per month this year. None of those works stood a chance of progressing into the second round of evaluation.

 While reviewing country of origin, I noticed that the “suspicious” submissions were more likely to come from the US and a couple of other top ten regulars. While the initial waves of “AI” submissions were fully-generated and from outside the US, that trend is quickly changing to partially-generated and the US is now the primary producer of slop submissions. Congratulations.

 179 stories (1.21%) made it to the second round, down 20 from the previous year. The difference can be attributed to an overall reduction in the percentage of first round submissions read by our slush readers. Editors tend to be pickier, so they send a lower percentage through to the next round. Despite the decrease, we saw a modest increase in acceptances during 2025. At present that count is three higher than 2024, but it will increase further as we clear the remaining 2025 submissions. (Stories in the second round are read multiple times and take longer to come to a final decision on.) We don’t regard these changes as a problem. It’s likely to rebalance as we rebuild our slush team this year.

 Authors: When I have final numbers, I’ll add the acceptance rate, but I do want to point out that this kind of data can be extremely misleading. There is a significant difference between quantitative data (acceptance rate) and qualitative data (your story’s chances). For example, no matter what our acceptance rate is, I can promise you that a story about zombies has a 0% chance of acceptance. The quality (as determined by us–also a bit of a moving target) is the only determining factor in our decision. We never close submissions (short of a disaster) and don’t tend to have more than two months of inventory in stock at any given time. Nothing is ever rejected because we don’t have space. If it meets the guidelines, send it in. Worst that happens is that we say “no.” It simply means it isn’t right for us.

 In 2025, we received submissions from 133 different countries, the majority (60.5%, typical for the last few years) originated in the United States. The top ten countries (in order: United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, India, Nigeria, Germany, Brazil, Netherlands, Japan) represent 86.3% of all submissions. The number of countries does fluctuate and is monitored on a regular basis. While far from our best year, it was solidly average.

 The language barrier remains the largest obstacle to increasing submissions from other parts of the world. While the amount of time we have to spend dealing with “AI” has temporarily derailed our efforts to hold short submission windows in other languages, it is something we’d like to get back to as it yielded very positive results.

 These final two graphs shouldn’t require any explanation. They are pretty typical for us, but may be of interest to readers/writers.
