# SettlementForge, as it is meant to be

*Written by the chair (Fable) on 2026-09-20. Sources: your vision document, which I read myself, plus three Opus survey seats that read the design corpus, the ontology, your recorded laws and the code at today's tip. It describes the finished product, after every program is built and the last tuning signature is given, not the product as it stands. Where the destination itself is still undecided I say so at the end. I kept it inside your voice rules (no em dashes, no exclamation points) in case you reuse any of it.*

## The short version

SettlementForge is a world engine that sits behind a game master's table. You choose a few things, or eventually just describe what you want, and it builds a settlement that holds together the way a real place does: the economy follows from the land, the institutions follow from the economy, the factions follow from the institutions, the people follow from the factions, and their secrets follow from the pressures on them. Every fact keeps the reason it exists, so any "why" has a real answer. Then you start a clock and the place lives. Weeks pass, news travels and gets garbled, neighbours trade and quarrel and go to war and make peace, and when you come back the world tells you what happened and why. You remain the author throughout. The machine never decides the fate of a named person, never decides what a god does, never rewrites history you have lived, and never takes the pen from your hand. Your own vision document says it in one contrast that I cannot improve on: other tools write what happens next; SettlementForge runs what happens next, and can tell you why.

Everything below expands on that, first as a person experiences it, then as it is engineered.

## Part one: what a person experiences

### Arriving

A visitor arrives at a painted door: your hand-painted war arrow across the top of the page, with the wooden plaque that carries the name and the wax seal. That header is striking on purpose because it is the door, seen once. Everything below it is the book, calm and ruled, meant to be read for an hour. The landing page is a road with five stops (the forge, the voice, the realm, the commons, setting out), and each stop shows a real town rather than describing a product. The sample dossier on that page is the real dossier component, so the demonstration is the product itself. The navigation along the arrow's shaft is an argument in order: Create, then Library, then Realm, then Compendium, then Gallery. Make a place, keep it, set it among neighbours, learn the vocabulary the world is written in, see what others have made. That order is meaning, and it is never rearranged.

### Forging a settlement

Making a town needs one choice and one click: pick a size (Thorpe, Hamlet, Village, Town, City or Metropolis) and forge. Behind an Advanced panel sit about twenty dials: terrain, culture, age, wealth, trade access, the troubles pressing on the place, magic, which institutions must or must not exist, and your own custom content. The engine finishes in milliseconds. The few seconds of forging you watch are theatre, chosen on purpose, and anything over ten seconds would be a defect. At the end state you can also build by speaking. You describe "a fortified river town with three rival claimant families and no internal security," an interpreter turns that into settings for the same causal generator, compares the result with what you asked for, admits where it deviated, and lets you revise. The AI configures the forge. It never fakes the forging.

What comes out is an argument, not a roll on a table. Dramatic town types arrive dramatic: a town in the middle of a slave revolt opens on shut gates, not on market day. Eleven naming cultures speak in researched registers. Histories run deep. And the same seed always gives birth to the same starting town, which anyone can prove with a button.

### The dossier

What you receive is a document, and your law for it is that it should feel like a tome or a set of scrolls, never a dashboard. It is organized by subject, the way a gazetteer is: the land, the trade, the walls, who holds power, what people believe, who lives there, what is rumoured, what has happened. It is never organized around "the game master's question," because that breaks the feeling of holding an in-world document, and because you trust the reader. Nothing hides behind "show more." Volume is handled the way reference books have always handled it: every family of facts lives in the same place in every dossier so the layout is learned once, each section opens with a set-apart lead sentence, there is an index of names, there are marginal cross-references, and typography does the rest. On a phone the dossier is the scroll: one continuous read with nothing behind a tap. In print it is the tome proper, with folios, running heads, red-letter section heads, a table of matters, and a colophon carrying the seal, the seed and the date.

Its contents span the whole life of a place: an overview and a game master's summary, plot hooks, services, economics, power and factions, defense, resources, outlook and causes, magic, the named people and the web of relationships between them, rumours, war, faith, daily life, traditions, history, neighbours, your own notes, the chronicle, and saved versions. Sections a town has nothing to say about do not appear at all. The prose reads like fiction with receipts under every claim. The house style is causality ("the captain is corrupt because the wall fund is short"), and a visible contradiction is treated as the cardinal sin. Plot hooks obey "one story, once": two differently worded hooks that tell the same beat cannot both survive. The voice throughout is one calm campaign archivist: literate, unhurried, one idea per sentence, fond of concrete civic nouns like the wall fund, the garrison and the salt road. It never sells, never hedges, never exclaims. The reader should feel handed a dossier by someone who has lived in that world longer than they have.

### Keeping it, and starting the clock

You save the town to your Library. When it is ready for play you mark it canon, which changes what an edit means: before canon, changes are an author's corrections and apply at once; after canon, changes are events in the world's history. You place the town in the Realm, which is a map of many settlements sharing one clock, tie it to its neighbours by trade and grudge, and start the world clock. From then on the whole loop of the product is: forge, save, mark canon, connect, advance, understand, prepare, record what your players did, repeat. Every remaining piece of work exists to make that loop shorter, clearer or more trustworthy.

### The living world

One step of the clock is one week, and a year is fifty-two real weeks of simulation, never a shortcut. What lives under that clock is the deepest part of the ambition.

Information is physical. News travels the roads at the speed of travel, degrades on the way, counts as confirmed only when it arrives by independent routes, and settles into what each settlement and each faction believes. Beliefs decay, contradict each other, and drift away from the truth in both directions. Courts act on what they believe, so wars can begin in misjudgment, and a war can drag on because both sides think they are winning.

Goods are finite stocks moving on real routes through tolls, bandits and smugglers. Supply can starve. Trading hubs earn their importance. There is deliberately no coin-counting price simulator; scarcity is expressed in bands and in the words of the market ("four silver the bushel, dear, for the roads are cut"). People migrate, with nobody created or lost in the arithmetic, and migrants chase rumours rather than facts. Plague walks the trade roads. Disasters strike, and recovery is as real as ruin.

Every settlement wants things, and it wants them understandably. It has interests (what it prizes in its neighbours, and a pressure for resources that climbs a ladder from trade, to treaty, to coercion, to conquest, at the pace its character allows). It has a character, shaped by faith, alignment and form of government: what a farming council fears is not what a merchant league fears. It has a memory of grievances, debts, refusals and betrayals, each fading on its own clock, each held either by the throne or by the people, so a new ruler inherits the treaties but never the people's hatred. Decisions consume typed, recorded reasons. A war has a list of causes, a peace has its grounds, and a trade route knows why it opened. Causes for war and causes for peace are kept equally strong by law, so the world never slides into endless war or endless calm.

Kindness is simulated as seriously as war. A gift is a real decision under a real ledger: bond and history argue for it, the granary's margin and the army's appetite argue against, and the answer can be full, partial, a loan, or a refusal, with the reasoning written down. Gifts bind in proportion to the giver's sacrifice. Refusals wound according to what the refused party believes about the refuser's means. Generosity can be strategic, even predatory, and is priced as such.

War is more than assault. The informed strangle supply before they strike, the bloodless wage embargo and toll war, and the covert fund sedition and stay deniable until the rumour trail surfaces. Peace is priced: the winner's believed advantage buys a budget of terms (tribute, shares of resources, forced alliances, disarmament, puppet seats, missionary access). Every term has an end date, because a treaty is a season of history and never a law of physics. Every term is watched through fallible belief, so a distant victor can be cheated. Every term strains the loser until it produces grievance, default, or genuine conversion. Harsh peace breeds the next war. Generous peace can turn enemies into allies. Coalitions negotiate together or break apart, and neighbours with ties to both sides become the natural mediators.

Faith is simulated as sociology and never as theology. It is an extension of culture and tradition: congregations, institutions, schisms, prophecy, pilgrimage, suppression, and the backfire of martyrdom. The followers act. The god never does. No premade gods ship at all. Deities enter only through your own content, and whether a divine act ever happens is the table's decision.

Courts have interiors: a ruler and a rival each keeping their own books, legitimacy, coups, succession, and reconciliation at a price. Spies travel in secret to confirm what is only rumoured, and can be caught and ransomed. Allies lend troops into shared wars. Actors learn from repetition, and being predictable becomes a cost. Roads are inhabited, with armies and caravans visibly on the move.

Empires are never created as objects. A hegemony is a pattern the map begins to show: one centre holding vassals, tributaries and puppets. It strains with distance and compliance, frays at treaty expiries, fractures on succession, and unites everyone else against it simply by growing. The engine builds the bones, and the game master names the empire. Over all of it sits a pacing governor that keeps history breathing like a story. It limits how many great spontaneous dramas begin in a decade, so you get one great war and one golden age rather than four of each, but it never suppresses a consequence already in motion. Drama is delayed, never denied.

Named people age into states, never fates. A person can be shelved (on a journey, imprisoned, missing), jailed, banished, turned, or scattered as a story seed when their town falls. The engine raises the stakes and the game master spends them. No named character is ever killed or resolved by the machine, and no generated prose is allowed to imply otherwise.

### Understanding what happened

Depth without legibility is noise, so explanation is treated as an organ of the product. When you return after an advance, the world greets you with what it did, at the altitude you choose: a one-line headline, chapters by season, threads (a story that began, turned, and now stands somewhere), and the individual events with their receipts. A year's advance is never an archaeology dig, and the report leads with what changed. A town crier speaks each settlement's news in period voice. Treaties are readable documents with parties, terms, years remaining and the strain on each clause, so a fraying peace is visible at the seam that will tear. A dramatic-irony brief shows reasons accumulating ("three of five reasons for peace are now present; this war is dying") and beliefs diverging ("both courts think they are winning"). The realm map carries live instruments: fronts, plague, and where belief has drifted from truth. There is a campaign-scale brief, weekly digests, session prep sheets, faction dossiers, and a player-safe brief composed only from what the players are allowed to know, so it cannot leak.

### Putting your own hand in

Edit Mode is your design for making authorship feel like play. A saved settlement enters a mode where every editable card shows a pencil and every section that can grow shows a plus. Clicking opens a dialog over a darkened page with every field that card owns, and nearly every value is chosen from a typed pool drawn from the product's own vocabulary rather than typed freely: institutions legal for that size, names from that culture with a "roll another" button, roles, stances, causes, commodities. Names and notes are free text. Each saved change drops into an ordered decree registry at the foot of the dossier. On a draft, changes apply immediately. On a canon town, each pooled change becomes an event that happens at the next advance, in the order you arranged, chronicled as a cause with the table's hand behind it. Everything is reversible until time moves. After that it is history, and history is permanent.

Guards advise and never refuse. If you ask for something that normally needs something else first, the guard says so and offers three doors: fulfil the prerequisite for me, let me do it myself, or proceed anyway. This is a world with magic and a game master who may play god, so "proceed" is always available, and the override is recorded honestly without an invented excuse.

You edit at the source, never at the result. Root facts (a person's name and role, an institution, a faction's nature, who holds a seat) and world facts (terrain, culture, trade access, resources, stressors) are editable. Derived facts (the economy, supply chains, defense, food security, history) are not; they show where they came from instead of a pencil. When you change a source, the town is re-derived without being re-rolled. Everything else you already know about the place is held fixed, so the port you moved inland keeps its harbour master, and the strangeness that results is a consequence to play with rather than a bug. You can also declare wars, open trade and send forces against places that do not exist on your map. The engine mints such a "phantom" counterparty from a seed; the consequences at home are real, and the far side is record only. A phantom can later be forged into a full settlement from the same seed, carrying its written history with it. Your edits and decrees are yours alone: copies, imports and gallery versions carry only the seed's world.

### Talking to it

The Surveyor is the second way to operate everything, equal to the manual controls and sharing one history with them. Ask anything, and an analyst answers from the receipts, citing the chain of causes. Command anything ("make the king's grip slip without a civil war"), and you get a cautious, labelled, reviewable proposal that separates what is required, inferred, optional, uncertain and protected. After a session you tell the world in plain sentences what your players did, approve what it distils, advance, and harvest next week's material. Play, tell, approve, advance, harvest is meant to be the heartbeat of a campaign. The surface is one prompt box that also accepts files and recordings.

The rule underneath never bends: something proposes, the simulator validates, the game master authorizes. The AI is an interface, interpreter, planner, analyst and editor, and never the engine. It cannot silently change canon, it must admit when the simulator lacks a mechanic it would need, it cannot see the game master's secrets when writing for players, and everything proposed and decided leaves a permanent record. Different models are trusted to different depths according to a measured probe, never according to their name, and because every AI output must fit a typed bucket the engine validates, a weak model fails safely. Models are rented and interchangeable, the world model is yours, and you can bring your own key.

A related, separately sold capability is the Scribe. When a dossier is opened, a model reads a card of that town's exact facts and writes the prose fresh; the result is checked against the facts and against a list of things it must never say; anything that fails falls back to the hand-written corpus. That prose is decoration that no mechanic ever reads. It is held to non-contradiction rather than non-invention, and it is designed as its own paid tier.

### Making the world yours, and sharing it

The Compendium is both the reference for the world's vocabulary and the workshop for your own: institutions, resources, goods, stressors, deities, and eventually tunables and whole setting packs. Custom content is a first-class citizen because the engine reasons about machine-readable traits rather than names, so your "Smugglers' Warren" receives the mechanics of an underground institution by right. At the far end the AI can help draft entire settings, even non-medieval ones, but only content that compiles into a registered, validated type can land, and only in your account. Your documents summarize that ambition as: infinite worlds, one set of laws, kept.

Existing campaigns can be imported through a staged reconciliation in which nothing changes until you have confirmed each match and previewed the result, and imported prose never silently becomes mechanical truth. The Gallery shares sanitized worlds that others can admire freely and fork with the paid tier. Exports are shelf-worthy: a draft brief, a canon dossier, a timeline packet, and a campaign war room with a State of the Realm chapter and a treaty table. A whole realm also exports as one portable, versioned portrait that a Foundry VTT module can read, and that a small local read-only "truth server" can serve to any other AI tool, with citations back to the exact week and settlement. Virtual tabletops are complemented, never fought: finish the session in Foundry, tell SettlementForge what happened, walk into next week prepared.

### Maps

Three map ideas belong to the end state. The realm map, built on an imported Azgaar world, is where settlements, routes, relationships and live conditions are drawn. The settlement map is a town plan drawn deterministically from the town's own dossier, shown through a Dossier and Map toggle on every saved settlement, where hovering a building tells you which institution it is and districts carry their wealth and their dangers. Because it is a pure projection of the dossier, anything that changes one changes the other, always. You have ruled that settlement mapmaking leaves the launch path and is built as its own module whose output the product consumes. A lazily loaded, illustrated 3D portrait of the town exists as an option and stays unpromoted until it earns certification. The very last thing on the road, parked on purpose, is the deep living map: the burned quarter still scarred years later, the boom district visibly risen, the temple grown since the plague made it beloved, and sieges that breach real wall segments whose rebuilt stone is still there at the next siege.

### Accounts, money and the Founders

The ladder mirrors the depth. Anyone can forge for free, and an anonymous visitor can make the middle sizes. A free account (Wanderer) unlocks every size, full customization, three saves and gallery sharing. A single dossier can be bought as a permanent artifact for $2.99. Cartographer (configured today at $5.99 a month) buys the living world: the Realm, the clock, custom content, gallery import, unlimited saves and exports, and the editor. Surveyor (configured today at $14.99 a month, the floor of a band you set up to $19.99) buys the interpreter, priced against the hours of preparation it saves rather than against other subscriptions, with credit packs and bring-your-own-key for heavy use. The simulation itself is never tier-gated: every tier runs the same engine. Thirty numbered Founder chairs exist, all by invitation and none ever sold, used as recognition for prominent game masters rather than as a quick sale. Purchases stay locked until launch. Your own measure of the value is that a single static settlement represents roughly $700 to $1,200 of freelance-equivalent work for a Thorpe and $3,600 to $6,600 for a Metropolis (a model, give or take forty percent), and that the living version has no freelance equivalent at all.

### What it should feel like, and what it is not

The feeling the whole product is built for is that the place was already there, that it will be the same place next week, and that you are its author while it is a co-author that never breaks character and never forgets. Guidance follows the rule "the world teaches; the software whispers": hints sit in the margins in the world's own cloth, never float over content, never interrupt, and commerce never wears the costume. The whole site should feel bound like one book, with a small grammar of quiet motions (a page laid down, a rule drawing itself, a seal pressed once) instead of generic fades, and none of it may ever cost legibility or accessibility. Marketing leads with the experience rather than the mechanism, never concedes speed, and says the true thing: as instant as any generator, orders of magnitude deeper, and every fact agrees with every other.

It is not a virtual tabletop, not an AI storyteller, not a wiki that remembers without understanding, not a map painter whose maps mean nothing, not a strategy game you play to win, and not a replacement for the game master.

## Part two: how it is engineered to keep those promises

### One record, three layers

Every settlement is stored as three things kept strictly apart: the frozen configuration it was born from, the frozen dossier that generation produced, and the live state that the passing weeks have written. Anything that can be computed from those is never stored, only recomputed, so it cannot drift out of agreement. The world can move for a century without the record of its birth ever being altered.

### The forge

Generation is a pure, headless program with no clock, no network and no screen. It runs about two dozen steps in a fixed causal order: resolve the configuration, resources, stresses and neighbours; assemble institutions and prune them for plausibility; build the economy; build power and factions; reconcile the economy and the power structure against each other until a fingerprint proves they agree; validate the structure; generate the people, then pass over them for corruption; then write the narratives and assemble the record. All randomness comes from one seeded source, and each step draws from its own named branch of it, so adding or reordering steps never changes anyone else's dice. A draw from anywhere else stops the program, in production too. Loops run in an order no computer's language settings can bend. Generation is structurally blind to the living world, which is what makes a town regenerable in the middle of a campaign. Your edits are an overlay applied after the dice and consume no randomness, so an untouched town regenerates byte for byte and an edited town keeps its edits. A library of 525 pinned configurations is stored as "golden" output, and any unintended change to any of them fails the build. The whole simulation runs on the user's own device, which is why it is instant and why protection of the asset is legal rather than technical.

### The clock

The living world is a second pure engine. Each tick is one simulated week, run through nine declared stages: wake the world, update memories, age conditions, move each settlement's clock, run the movers, choose what happens, check permissions and apply, fold in the consequences, write the receipt. About twenty independent movers run inside it in a documented order: pressures, calamity, generosity, intervention, recovery, settlements founded and abandoned, resources found and exhausted, migration, trade, smuggling, fleets, armies and sieges, the reasons for war and peace, treaty strain, alliances, contests of faith, the pricing of lies, rumours hardening into beliefs, commitments, the pacing governor, and finally the world writing its own news. Each mover sits behind its own switch and contributes exactly nothing when it is off, which is how new capabilities ship without disturbing old worlds.

The idea that makes it produce stories rather than state changes is that reality is modelled three times: the truth, what each observer believes (late, foggy, wrong in both directions), and what each audience is permitted to see. The seam between them is sealed on every surface, including everything handed to an AI. One architectural law was learned the hard way and is now permanent: a filtered view used for reading must never become the thing that is written back, because that is exactly how shelved people were once erased from a save.

### The depth program

War is already built to a high grade. The remaining ambition is organized as a foreign policy program with a simple thesis: foreign policy is four substances (war, trade, faith and populations) perceived through one medium (information), negotiated in one grammar (diplomacy), and driven by an interior (each court's books and memory). Once the other layers reach war's grade, foreign policy emerges on its own and no further module is ever needed. It is compiled as thirteen programs of roughly a hundred waves: a shared spine, the grammar of pacts, information, trade, faith, populations, the interior, the cross-couplings with an explainer that can walk any chain of causes, espionage, inhabited roads, learned habit, shared wars, and the "advance epoch" that governs living futures and immutable pasts. Later drafts add logistics (fleets, journeys, interception, evacuation), coherent pestilence, a game master's console, and the fabric of the settlement map. Beside it sit the architected volumes of the paid program: state treasuries and taxation by form of government, foreign and occupied seats, lived experience and defining characters, the temperament of faiths and their influence, missions and envoys, a durable memory of concluded wars, and generative heraldry that inherits through kinship.

Every mechanism must carry a law, a force and its counterforce, a belief posture, receipts, tests and tuning bands. A force without a counterforce only ever ratchets one way, and one-way ratchets are this project's proven killer. Your rule for this program is that every family's architecture is written, one at a time and each with the earlier ones in view, before any of it is built. The yardstick is not an economist's simulation. It is ten out of ten on cohesion for a game master, reached by curing the one root cause of every weak subsystem: several parallel models of the same truth drifting apart. The cure is one canonical substrate, derived once, that everything reads and writes.

### One way to change anything

In the finished system there is one narrow path for every change, whether it comes from a hand, a decree, an import or the AI. The change is compiled into a typed command. An executor admits it, checks who is allowed, checks that the preview the user saw is still fresh, claims its identity so it can never apply twice, runs the pure operation, saves with a compare-and-set against your own rows in the database, and writes a receipt. Because everything passes through that one door, everything is previewable, auditable, and reversible where reversal is possible: undo, checkpoints, branches, and reproducible what-ifs. This is also your finite semantics law made into machinery. Players have unlimited freedom, but the world's memory of that freedom is finite: every effect that touches the engine is a typed record from a closed vocabulary with a bounded size. Free text has exactly three jobs: your own words preserved on a receipt, fuel for flavour, and input that the AI sorts into proposed typed records a human must confirm.

The editor rests on a second piece of hard engineering. Re-deriving a town after an edit cannot be a re-roll, so every choice the generator ever made is registered, and re-derivation re-runs the causal steps with every registered choice pinned to what the record already says, releasing only what the edit touches. Changes are merged by consistency group (the smallest set of facts that must agree with each other), checked against the town's own invariants, and applied one at a time like decrees. People, institutions and factions carry permanent identities, so nothing is ever joined by name or by position in a list. When an edit moves a derived figure, the delta card shows three numbers honestly: what it was, what it is now, and how much of the move the edit alone explains.

### Words

The prose is a hand-authored corpus of thousands of short variants organized into pools, each pool keyed to one typed combination of facts, each variant taking a different angle. A small pure kernel picks among them by a stable hash and renders a sentence only when every blank in it has a real fill, so the product prints nothing rather than a stub. A joining layer weaves sentences into paragraphs and handles repeated names without adding a single authored word. The voice is machine-enforced: no em dashes, no exclamation points, no interface-speak, no raw engine numbers in reader text.

The AI layer is a clerk by construction. The text that teaches the model and the schema that constrains its output are generated from the same source, so a category the engine does not know cannot even be emitted. Every request is grounded in simulator facts, with an explicit list of what is forbidden and which entities are locked, and a verifier types any violation of canon before it can land. Keys you bring are encrypted, used per request and never logged, and a failure fails closed rather than quietly spending the house key. Anything the AI learns about how people build arrives as an anonymous, aggregate picture injected per request, and is never remembered.

### Platform

Saves run through one service with two interchangeable backends: a cloud database with row-level security as the spine of its safety, or the browser's own storage, with an outbox that replays queued writes when you come back online. Every stored payload passes an admission check that classifies it as current, migrated or rejected, and old saves are upgraded in place. Around the database sit a long train of versioned migrations, a few dozen server functions for AI, payments and operations, consent-gated analytics with a field observatory that watches how worlds behave in the wild, and a deployment path that fails closed: nothing ships unless every check is green on the main branch. What the public sees of a shared world is a strict allowlist, mirrored on the server so the client cannot be tricked. Every paid promise on the pricing page names the code that enforces it, and a test walks that list so an advertised paywall and an enforced one can never drift apart.

Screen and print are derived from the same selectors, so the PDF cannot say something the screen does not, and a test fails if a living-world fact reaches one without the other. Performance is a promise with budgets: the first paint must stay under a byte ceiling that may only go down, the engine loads only when you first forge, and "fast on day one and fast in year ten" is proven by endurance runs of one, thirty and a hundred simulated years.

### The quality estate

The product's whole claim is that the world explains itself because the system that built it refuses to do anything without a reason you can read. So the test estate is nearly the size of the product (about 700,000 lines of product guarded by about 640,000 lines of tests, some 33,000 tests), and it is built less around "does this feature work" than around "can this class of failure still happen." Ratchets freeze a debt at a ceiling that can only shrink, so a backlog never blinds the gate. Walkers enumerate the source tree against a frozen inventory, so nothing new joins a family without the wiring that family requires. One instrument runs the real generators across many seeds to find code that reads a field nothing ever writes, and its twin finds generated facts that are never shown to anyone. A mutation register demands that every important test be proven able to fail. Golden outputs can only move through a single door that demands a signed record of exactly what moved and why. Work is handed to building agents only as sealed packets with a verified base, an exact list of files and a budget. Every judgment call is recorded in an owner decision queue where you can veto it afterward. Every hazard that ever bit becomes machinery rather than a note. And the final certification is deliberately left empty until a long soak actually proves each subsystem alive, because a passing world can hide a dead organ.

### Tuning, and the order of the end

Tuning is the last act, and it is yours. What gets tuned are the dials and weights, never the structure of the dice. Targets are bands on observed outcomes over long simulated runs (for example, how many court successions a faction sees in a decade), not values for constants. Every tuned value is inventoried in a register with its unit and the one person allowed to sign it, which is you. Auto-tuning was considered and rejected. The known first job is a carrying-capacity ceiling for population, because a three-hundred-year run showed the realm splitting into runaway growth and collapse, which a thirty-year run cannot see.

The endgame has one authoritative order: build every wave; run a diagnostic soak with everything lit together for the first time, findings only; repair how the systems compose; take your own walk through the product and repair what you find; light the flags and perform the one regeneration; run the terminal soak on the exact tree that ships; give the tuning signature; then push and deploy. Nothing that can move an output may land after that signature. Launch readiness is a bundle of evidence rather than a green test count: a migration rehearsal on a production-shaped copy, backup and restore receipts, an enforced content security policy, post-deploy probes, a rollback rehearsal, live payment canaries, and an uncoached study of real game masters going through the whole loop, because no internal test can prove ease or willingness to pay. The metric that matters afterward is consecutive weeks of use of a canon world, not the number of towns generated.

After launch the Promise governs tuning absolutely. An observatory may watch the field and draft tuning releases, you sign each one, new worlds are born with the newest physics, and existing worlds keep the physics they were born with unless their owner asks them to grow.

## The promises, in one place

A seed is a starting world, forever: the same seed always gives the same beginning. A lived history, once lived, is forever: the future is not written in advance, and the same beginning can tell more than one plausible future, but whatever future you actually live becomes permanent.
