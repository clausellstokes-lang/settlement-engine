## THE ANSWER IN ONE PARAGRAPH (for the DM)

Every town big enough to keep a watch puts a few riders on its own roads. How many, and how good, is set by four things the world already knows: the town's size (a hamlet keeps one thin patrol at most, a city many), its people (the same men the levy would call), its wealth (what it can pay them), and how seriously it keeps order. War and fear move the number inside those limits: more riders out when an enemy is believed near or the town is at war, fewer in quiet years; when the town's army goes on campaign its riders keep the army's road out first, and the back roads go to the brigands unless the town keeps riders there too — men it then does not send with the army. Each week the riders walk a round of the town's roads, the most dangerous and the richest first; where they walk, bandits strike less often, smugglers are caught more often, spies and enemy captains risk being stopped, and the town hears what passes on its land. When the town believes something is out there, its riders go and look; when the thing is bigger than one patrol, several ride together; when it is gone, or was never there, they go back to their rounds — so a false report costs a town its watch for a while. When war comes, the riders are the first to see it and the first to die buying the town a week. Allied towns' riders pass each other at the shared gates and trade what they saw; a kingdom whose hold is strong sends the crown's riders down every member's roads. Nothing the riders do is a second law: each effect is a bounded lean inside a number the world already keeps. The DM sees the deeds ("Kell's riders came in from the north road with word of Merewick's host at the ford"); the reasons wait for a look on purpose; the DM can direct the riders, hold their pay down (or raise the town's purse to raise it), and decide every clash.
