[WITHHELD] The village has no wall around it and no soldiers of its own. A drover says the village is no safer to sleep in than the road, and he pays for a bed all the same. The older heads here hold that a place with nothing worth an army's march needs no wall, and they say so to whoever raises it. A woman who sells at the market says nothing between the road and her stall would slow a man coming down it. The drinkers at the alehouse can name every house that would come out for trouble, and they say the naming is as far as it goes. One of the watch says the round is walked by people who keep a bench by day. Those who sit to hear a matter hold that the place has no line of its own, and the boundaries before them run between one plot and the next. A local priest says the church door is the only one in the village barred at night, and that it is barred against the weather. A guild member says the trades have priced their goods to a place that stands open. The hall has never had anybody come in asking for a wall, a clerk there says.

[WITHHELD] No court sits here, and a man taken up has nowhere to be kept. A pedlar says a man cheated here is pointed at the door of the house that did it and left to knock. The potboy reckons a quarrel over money here is settled at the table or not at all, and the table is his to clear. One of the elders holds that a wrong here is heard by whoever is standing near it, and that the hearing is the whole of the answer. The chapel's warden says a man with nowhere else to be kept is kept at the church, and the church has nothing to keep him with. At the gate they say a stranger is looked over on the way in, and nothing is kept of the looking. The muster tells a stranger who complains that there is nowhere to lock up the man he names. There is nowhere for the stranger either. The market clerk says a dispute over a sale is heard at the stall where it happened and nowhere after that.

[WITHHELD] Little coin is put by here against trouble, and what is wanted beyond it is asked for at the doors. A carter says what is put into his hand here is grain and not coin, and he sells it somewhere else to have coin at all. A woman old enough to remember the custom holds that an ask here is made out loud in front of the neighbours, and that a household refusing is heard by the same ears. A woman at the long table says the same houses are asked for money every time, and hers is one of them. The people who keep the register say a household gives toward a burying whatever it has, and that what is given is remembered by the neighbours longer than any debt. Those who hold the way through say the bar coming down is heard at the far doors. Not one of the doors opens. The muster reckons what comes up from a door is a body or a tool, and the tool is remembered as well as the body. A fish seller reckons the village asks the stalls for money before it asks the farms, the coin being in one place and not the other. One of the watch says the same corners get stood in whether the purse is settled or not. The round ends where it starts, at the water. Nobody here asks the soldiers their business, the soldiers say, and everybody here asks them for a hand.

[WITHHELD] No grain is kept back here against a bad year, and the nearest thing to a house for the sick is the church. A pilgrim says a man who falls sick on this road is carried to the church, there being no other door to carry him to. A carter at the alehouse reckons a man who asks here for a house for the sick is served at the door and no further in. The households that keep the custom hold that a bad year is met out of each house's own, and that a common store would only be argued over. A woman who sings in the choir says the sick are laid at the back of the church, and that the singing goes on over them. A buyer off the road says grain can be had here on a market day and kept nowhere once the day is over.
