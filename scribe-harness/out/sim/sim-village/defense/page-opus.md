[WITHHELD] The arrangements Hochhausen has made against trouble are thin. Something is in place against most of what arrives and nothing is in place behind it.

[WITHHELD] No soldiers of the town's own are kept here, and nothing is built for them to stand behind. A traveller says the road runs in past the last house and nobody stands on it to ask anybody's business. The older heads here say the place keeps no line around itself and nobody asks it to. The stallholders say the market is open on every side and that is what brings the buyers off the road. A woman at the long table says the houses that would come out if anything happened can all be named, and the naming is as far as it goes. One of the watch says the round is walked by people who keep a bench by day. Those who sit to hear a matter hold that the place has no line of its own, and the boundaries before them run between one plot and the next. The chapel's warden says the church door is the only one here barred at night, and it is barred against the weather. A guild member says the trades have priced their goods to a place that stands open. The hall has never had anybody come in asking for a wall, a clerk there says.

[WITHHELD] The town has no court and no place to hold a man. A rider who stopped a night says a man wronged here takes it to the door of the house that did it and no further. The potboy says a quarrel here is settled at the table or carried out of the door, and the house would rather it went out. A woman old enough to remember the custom says a wrong here is put to the households, and the households are the whole of it. The people who keep the register say a man with nowhere else to go sleeps in the church porch, and the church is no place to hold anyone. At the gate they say a stranger is looked over on the way in, and nothing is kept of the looking. The muster tells a stranger who complains that there is nowhere to lock up the man he names. There is nowhere for the stranger either. The market clerk says a thief taken at a stall gives the goods back, and the stallholders count that the whole of the remedy.

[WITHHELD] What the town could put behind itself in a hard season is thin. A carter says what is handed over here is goods and not coin, and no apology comes with the sack. The households that keep the custom say an ask is made out loud and in front of the neighbours, and a refusal is heard by the same ears. The drinkers at the alehouse say the loudest household at the table is the one that would be asked last. A woman who sings in the choir says a household gives what it has toward a burying, and what it gives is remembered by the neighbours. Those who hold the way through say the bar coming down is heard at the far doors. Not one of the doors opens. The muster reckons what comes up from a door is a body or a tool, and the tool is remembered as well as the body. The women who gut the catch say the place takes from whoever can be found, and a stall is the easiest thing here to find. One of the watch says the same corners get stood in whether the purse is settled or not. The round ends where it starts, at the water. Nobody here asks the soldiers their business, the soldiers say, and everybody here asks them for a hand.

[PASS] No store is kept against a bad year, and no house here takes the sick. A pilgrim says a man who asks here for the store is shown a field. A carter at the alehouse says a man who asks after a house for the sick is taken for sick himself and served standing at the door. One of the elders says what a stranger calls a want here is only the way the place is arranged. A local priest says the sick are nursed in their own beds, and the church takes the ones with nobody to nurse them. A fish seller says a buyer can have grain here on a market day and nowhere to keep it after.
