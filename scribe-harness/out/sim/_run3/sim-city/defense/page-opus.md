[PASS] Nothing presses Rundgate, and the quiet is not luck. The town is kept ready, and nobody has found it worth the trying.

[PATCHED] What is out in the country is known to everyone here and named by nobody. The town keeps a garrison and a citadel, and counts the silence as their doing. A drover says the men at the gate ask what he carries and never what he passed on the road. The men who hold the ways in say the bar goes down at dusk and nobody has ever asked them to leave it up. A parish warden holds that the prayers name the garrison and never name what is out in the country. One of the aldermen says the hall hears no complaint about the country and counts that as the garrison paying for itself. A serjeant of the watch holds that what is brought to the watch at night is brought at the gate and goes no further. An innkeeper says the drinkers argue about who stands the nights and never about what the nights are stood against. The garrison say nobody from the town comes out to watch the drills. The guild master reckons the toll at the gates comes off the trades and the quiet it buys is nobody's bill but theirs. The stallholders say the stalls go up whatever is loose in the country, and the rent falls due whether anybody comes or not. A bailiff holds that the courts hear every dispute the town brings and nothing out of the country ever reaches them. The elders say the young stand the nights and the old say how they are stood.

[WITHHELD] The town is walled and gated, and the soldiers who hold the walls are its own and paid for the work. A citadel stands behind them. A gatekeeper says a stranger pays at the gate and is counted there before he is anybody's business but the gate's. A woman who keeps the hall books says the money taken at the gates belongs to the town and not to the gates. A clerk of one of the courts holds that a stranger who paid at the gate may argue the sum in the courts like anybody else. A pilgrim says a pack is opened at the gate here and nothing is taken out of it but the toll. The drinkers of the district say the town is never asked whether it wants soldiers of its own, only what they come to.

[WITHHELD] The courts of Rundgate sit and there is nowhere to hold a man. What the law takes from him is his money or his place in the town. The watch say a man taken up after dark is dealt with before morning, there being nowhere to leave him. The officers of the court hold that they hear by daylight what the watch settled in the dark and are asked about none of it first. A clerk in the hall says the purse has never run to a room for holding men, and the courts do the work without one. A market clerk says a short weight goes to the court and comes back as a sum. The man who sold it keeps his stall, there being nowhere to put him. A canon holds that the parish keeps a place for a man once he is dead and the town keeps none for him while he lives. A journeyman says a fine comes out of a man's takings and is forgotten, and the mark against his work is not. A potboy of the district reckons a man with a house here pays and stays, and a man with none is put on the road. A carter says a man who owns nothing here has nothing the court can take, and the road is what is left. A man on one of the gates says he is handed a man and a direction and no reason for either. A serjeant of the garrison holds that what comes at the walls is the garrison's business and the men walked out of the gates are not.

[WITHHELD] The stores are kept full and the money that fills them is the town's own. The people of the town count that as a crisis already answered. A secretary of the city's offices says the town asks the trades for no more than the trades can carry. The guilds of the town say the carrying is theirs and the asking is somebody else's. The guilds' account is the likelier, the hall having a purse to defend and the guilds only a bill. A night patrolman says the rounds are kept by men who are owed for keeping them. A serjeant on the wall reckons a cart bound for the grain store is never kept waiting at the gates, whatever else is. A traveller says the grain store is praised here and the wage complained of by the same people. A woman at the long table holds that the watch drinks on what it is owed and pays when it is paid. A man who draws the wage says a town able to feed itself through a crisis and still short on his wage has decided something, and has not said what. A porter in the square says the hall's share comes off the day's take before anybody's own does. One of the vergers holds that the stores are watched closely and the people at the almshouse door are not.

[WITHHELD] The town keeps a grain store and no sick house. Whoever tends the sick here comes out of the church. A priest of one of the parishes says a stranger asks for the sick house and is sent to a person instead. A trader come in for the fair holds that the grain store is the first thing a stranger is shown and the last thing he can buy out of. A carter at one of the taverns says a man who falls ill on the road is carried to the church here, there being nowhere else to carry him. One of the watch says the grain store is what strangers ask after, and a sick man on the street is what the watch carries to the church. A warden of one of the guilds reckons the store a stranger admires was filled by the trades and cannot be opened by them. A clerk of the court says a dispute over the store comes to the court and a dispute over the sick has never come at all.
