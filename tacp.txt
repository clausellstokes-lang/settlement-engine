'''''Tolkien: A Cultural Phenomenon''''' is a 2003 book of [[literary criticism]] by '''Brian Rosebury'''<!--redirect target--> about the English author and [[philologist]] [[J. R. R. Tolkien]] and his writings on his fictional world of [[Middle-earth]], especially ''[[The Lord of the Rings]]''. A shorter version of the book, '''''Tolkien: A Critical Assessment'''''<!--alternate title and redirect target-->, appeared in 1992. Rosebury examines how Tolkien imagined Middle-earth, how he achieved the [[Sound and language in Middle-earth|aesthetic effect]] he was seeking, his place among twentieth century writers, and how his work has been retold and imitated by other authors and in other media, most notably for film by [[Peter Jackson]].

Other [[Tolkien scholar]]s have praised the book, noting that it raised the standard of Tolkien criticism, and that it made the point that readers have to be delighted with Middle-earth so as to care that it is not destroyed by the [[Dark lord|Dark Lord]] [[Sauron]]. In particular, [[Jane Chance]] comments on Rosebury's demonstration of the high quality of Tolkien's work, including his comparison of Tolkien's writing [[Tolkien and the modernists|with that of twentieth-century modernists]]. [[Tom Shippey]] finds the book a compelling analysis.

== Context ==

[[J. R. R. Tolkien]]'s fantasy writings about [[Middle-earth]], especially ''[[The Hobbit]]'' and ''[[The Lord of the Rings]]'', have become extremely popular,<ref>{{cite web |last=Seiler |first=Andy |date=16 December 2003 |url=https://www.usatoday.com/life/movies/news/2003-12-12-lotr-main_x.htm | title='Rings' comes full circle |work=[[USA Today]] |access-date=5 August 2020}}</ref> and have exerted considerable influence since their publication,<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.veritas.org/Talks.aspx#!/v/323 |title=J. R. R. Tolkien: Father of Modern Fantasy Literature |access-date=22 August 2013 |last=Mitchell |first=Christopher |format=streaming video |work=The Veritas Forum |date=12 April 2003 |archive-date=12 October 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131012040819/http://www.veritas.org/Talks.aspx#!/v/323 |url-status=dead }}</ref> but acceptance by the [[literary criticism|literary establishment]] has been slower. Nevertheless, [[Tolkien studies|academic studies]] on Tolkien's works have been appearing at an increasing pace since the mid-1980s, prompting a measure of [[Reception of J. R. R. Tolkien|literary re-evaluation of his work]].{{sfn|Lobdell|2013}}

Brian Rosebury is a lecturer in the humanities at the [[University of Central Lancashire]]. He has specialised in Tolkien, in [[literary aesthetics]], and later in moral and political philosophy.<ref name="UCLAN">{{cite web |title=Dr. Brian Rosebury, Senior Lecturer |url=https://www.uclan.ac.uk/academics/dr-brian-rosebury |publisher=University of Central Lancashire |access-date=14 August 2020}}</ref>

== Book ==

=== Publication history ===

A short version of the book (167 pages, four chapters, paperback) was first published by [[Macmillan Publishers|Macmillan]] in 1992 under the title ''Tolkien: A Critical Assessment''.{{sfn|Chance|2005}}
 
The full version of the book (246 pages, six chapters, paperback) was published by [[Palgrave Macmillan]] in 2003 under the title ''Tolkien: A Cultural Phenomenon''.{{sfn|Chance|2005}}{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|p=246}} It has been translated into Italian.<ref>{{cite book | last=Rosebury | first=Brian | title=Tolkien: un fenomeno culturale | publisher=Marietti | publication-place=Genoa and Milan | year=2009 | isbn=978-88-211-8576-2 | oclc=800027095 | language=it |ref=none}}</ref>

=== Synopsis ===
{{further|Reception of J. R. R. Tolkien}}

[[File:Rosebury's analysis of Lord of the Rings.svg|thumb|upright=1.75|Diagram of Brian Rosebury's analysis of ''[[The Lord of the Rings]]'' as a combined Quest (to destroy [[One Ring|the Ring]]) and Journey (as a series of [[Tableau vivant|Tableaux]] of places in [[Middle-earth]]); the two support each other|alt=Diagram showing The Lord of the Rings' structure as both a heroic quest and a descriptive journey]]

The book begins by examining how Tolkien imagined Middle-earth in ''The Lord of the Rings'', and how he achieved the aesthetic effect he was seeking. Rosebury shows that Tolkien does two things simultaneously: he builds up a detailed picture of Middle-earth, on a journey through a series of tableaux; and goes on a [[quest]] to destroy [[One Ring|the Ring]]. The tableaux create places realistic enough for readers to love and wish to save; the quest saves the Middle-earth that is being created as the reader moves through it.{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|loc=chapters 1, 2}}

Rosebury then explores Tolkien's long career writing both prose and [[Poetry in The Lord of the Rings|poetry]], from the start of war in 1914 to his death in 1973.{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|loc=chapter 3}} The fourth chapter briefly situates Tolkien in the twentieth century literary scene, contrasting his work with [[Modernism]] and describing it as not ignorant of that movement but actually antagonistic to it.{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|loc=chapter 4}}

The later edition added two new chapters to the book. The first looks at Tolkien as a thinker within the history of ideas: it examines in turn how his writing relates to the times in which he lived, how his work has been used to support various ideologies, and the underlying coherence of his thinking.{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|loc=chapter 5}} The other chapter, which gives its title to the book, looks at the "afterlife" of his work, and how it has variously been retold in film and other media, assimilated to various genres, imitated by "thousands" of other authors, and, despite Tolkien's stated opinion that ''The Lord of the Rings'' was "quite unsuitable for 'dramatization'", adapted, most notably for film by [[Peter Jackson]]; Rosebury considers [[The Lord of the Rings: film versus book|how well this succeeds in conveying the message of the book]].{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|loc=chapter 6}}

== Reception ==
{{further|Tolkien's style}}

[[Jane Chance]], a Tolkien scholar, writes that the refusal by some critics to accept that Tolkien is a major writer has "consistently annoyed Tolkien readers ...over the past twenty-five years", but that [[Tom Shippey]] and Rosebury have attempted "to persuade these nay-sayers". She notes that Rosebury strategically uses Shippey to begin his book, praising him but saying that he doesn't clinch the argument that Tolkien's works are "of high quality". Rosebury then, she writes, applies his expertise as seen in his 1988 book ''Art and Desire: A Study in the Aesthetics of Fiction'', to demonstrate Tolkien's aesthetic skill. She contrasts Shippey's comparison of Tolkien with fantasy authors from [[George Orwell]] and [[William Golding]] to [[T. H. White]] and [[C. S. Lewis]], with Rosebury's search for parallels among the Modernists such as [[Marcel Proust]], [[James Joyce]], and [[T. S. Eliot]].{{sfn|Chance|2005}} Claire Buck however comments in the ''[[J. R. R. Tolkien Encyclopedia]]'' that this brings up the problematic definition of what "modern" is according to the same critics who thought Tolkien "a peripheral figure".{{sfn|Buck|2013}}

[[Nancy-Lou Patterson]], reviewing the first version of the book in ''[[Mythlore]]'', notes that Tolkien criticism had been distinctly "uneven" at best, but British critics such as Rosebury were improving the standard. She liked his characterisation of "Tolkien's descriptive gifts as possessing 'a certain sensuous precision, distinctive of Tolkien'". She agreed with Rosebury's assertion that ''The Lord of the Rings'' works not because of its [[Christianity in Middle-earth|basis in Christianity]] but for its emotional appeal of a powerfully imagined but essentially good world that treats [[Boethius|evil as the absence of good]]. In her view, Rosebury successfully defends ''The Lord of the Rings'', even if she wouldn't call ''The Hobbit'' a minor work.{{sfn|Patterson|1997}}

[[File:Peter Jackson01.jpg|thumb|upright|left|The 2003 version of the book adds a substantial discussion of how well [[Peter Jackson]] (pictured) [[Peter Jackson's interpretation of The Lord of the Rings|captured the subtlety of Tolkien's text]] with his [[The Lord of the Rings (film series)|film version of ''The Lord of the Rings'']].|alt=portly white man wearing a black shirt and orange garland]]

The Tolkien scholar [[Douglas A. Anderson]], reviewing the work for the literary magazine ''VII'', notes that this was a retitled edition of Rosebury's 1992 ''Tolkien: A Critical Assessment'', with two "significant" chapters added. He finds especially valuable Rosebury's analysis of [[Tolkien's style]] in the first three chapters; and he admires the two chapters on Tolkien's relationship with the modern world, including his views on religion, politics, and the environment. He finds disappointing Tolkien's treatment of ''The Hobbit'' as merely "a dry run for ''The Lord of the Rings''". Anderson comments, too, that Rosebury is "less rigorously critical" when discussing [[Peter Jackson's interpretation of The Lord of the Rings|Peter Jackson's film interpretation of Tolkien]] than when analysing the literature. Overall, however, Anderson finds the book "a shrewd and engaging academic study"; he notes that its traditional literary approach usefully complements Tom Shippey's "excellent" [[Philology|philological]] approach. He calls the book's prose "clear and direct..., without jargon", stating that Rosebury builds a cogent case for Tolkien "as a significant—though flawed—twentieth century writer".{{sfn|Anderson|2005}}
<!--
Liz Milner, in ''The Green Man Review'', notes that the second version of the book makes use of two major developments: [[Christopher Tolkien]]'s publication of his father's many Middle-earth manuscripts, and Peter Jackson's film version. She comments that the "refutation of [[Germaine Greer]], [[Edmund Wilson]] and their cronies may have been needed in 1992",{{sfn|Milner|2003}} but not any more. She found Rosebury at his best on [[Tolkien's style|Tolkien's literary techniques]], especially his creation of Middle-earth in such convincing form, and his argument that ''The Lord of the Rings'' succeeds as a work of art because it connects readers' desire for Middle-earth with their desire for Frodo to fulfil his errand. She is fascinated, too, by Rosebury's discussion of the divergences of Tolkien's mythology from Christian doctrine, comparing [[Eru Iluvatar]] with [[John Milton|Milton]]'s God in ''[[Paradise Lost]]''. She suggests that "general readers" will be most interested in the last chapter on the book's "afterlife" in film, games, and other artefacts. She notes that Rosebury calls Jackson's film trilogy a "qualified success" (based on the first two films); he admires the film realisation of Middle-earth, but mourns the loss of "some of the book's greatest virtues" including [[English understatement]], emotional tact, and spaciousness, and the film version's choice of physical conflict over rhetorical power, "dignity of presence[,] or force of intellect", and the book's emphasis on free will and individual responsibility. She finds Rosebury less readable than Shippey, but it comes a close second with "some wonderful insights".{{sfn|Milner|2003}}-->

Tom Shippey calls the book a compelling analysis, and finds Rosebury's explanation of how Tolkien wove free will, moral choice, and creativity into Middle-earth "especially convincing". He admired the account of Tolkien's narrative and descriptive skill, and thought Rosebury's chapter on Peter Jackson's film adaptation the "best available" at that time.<ref name="Palgrave 2020">{{cite web |title=Tolkien {{!}} A Cultural Phenomenon: Reviews |url=https://www.palgrave.com/gp/book/9781403915979#reviews |publisher=[[Palgrave Macmillan]] |date=2003 |access-date=13 August 2020}}</ref>

Christopher Garbowski, in the ''J. R. R. Tolkien Encyclopedia'', writes that Rosebury looks at the humanistic implications of [[eucatastrophe]], quoting him as saying that "the reader must be delighted in Middle-earth in order to care that [[Sauron]] does not lay it desolate".{{sfn|Garbowski|2013}}{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|p=41}} The eucatastrophe is convincing because "its optimism is emotionally consonant with the work's pervasive sense of a universe hospitable to the humane."{{sfn|Garbowski|2013}}{{sfn|Rosebury|2003|p=71}} [[Allan Turner (scholar)|Allan Turner]] comments in the same work that Rosebury rejects the unsupported assertions of archaising and "wrenched syntax" by critics like [[Catharine Stimpson]], and that Rosebury pointed out that Tolkien used a plain descriptive style, demonstrably favouring the "familiar phrasal verbs 'have on' and 'get off' .. to the slightly more literary 'wear' and 'dismount'".{{sfn|Turner|2013}}
