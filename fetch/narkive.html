I'm afraid that the pompous title doesn't hide a thorough attempt on
my side to provide, by myself, the analysis and criticism -- rather I
wish to initiate a discussion about the /Ainulindalë/ along the lines
sketched in some in the current 'Tolkien's Creation vs Biblical
Creation' thread (and I'll quote from posts in that thread -- but
please beware that all quotations will be indented only one level
regardless of whom I quote and from what post). I've looked around
for
comments and criticism of the tale from other sources, and I'll quote
also from such sources whenever I deem these learned Tolkien scholars
have expressed matters better than I could ever hope to do (not
entirely though -- I have to stay within the bounds of fair use <G>).


The /Ainulindalë/ or 'Music of the Ainur' has a special place in the
tales that make up the 'Silmarillion' (as more than the great tale,
the /Quenta Silmarillion/, both as published and as imagined by
Tolkien), in that the /Ainulindalë/ underwent less change than any
other story. In /The Book of Lost Tales: Part 1/ Christopher Tolkien
writes about 'The Music of the Ainur':

In later years the Creation myth was revised and
rewritten over and over again; but it is notable that in
this case only and in contrast to the development of the
rest of the mythology there is a direct tradition,
manuscript to manuscript, from the earliest draft to the
final version: each text is directly based on the one
preceding. Moreover, and most remarkably, the earliest
version, written when my father was 27 or 28 and embedded
still in the context of the Cottage of Lost Play, was so
evolved in its conception that it underwent little change
of an essential kind. There were indeed very many changes,
which can be followed stage by stage through the
successive texts, and much new matter came in; but the
fall of the original sentences can continually be
recognized in the last version of the Ainulindale, written
more than thirty years later, and even many phrases
survived. [BoLT1 p. 60]

These are the observable facts that set the /Ainulindalë/ apart, but
noting that the tale is special is, IMO, only half the work -- it
The Ainulindalë takes an interesting position, IMO, in the
development of Tolkien's mythology. It was -- I nearly said 'of
course' not the first thing to be written; i.e. Tolkien didn't
start
with the beginning, but at some point backtracked to make a
beginning that would fit the middle and the end that he had created
(or had in mind). There is, however, nothing particular about that
-- when I do a paper, I usually go back and write the introduction
last, but the Ainulindalë, once written, remained incredibly
constant -- more so, IMO, than any other text/myth/tale/story/lay
in
the whole mythology. This suggests to me that the central elements
of this myth were probably very essential to Tolkien -- as much as
the dancing Lúthien -- and that again makes it interesting to ask
'What?' and 'Why?'
This discussion, in the present incarnation, takes its outset in the
thread asking why Tolkien's cosmogonic myth seems to different from
the one in the Bible. As has been pointed out, the Genesis is not the
only account in the Bible, and even considering the Genesis, the
differences are generally within the scope of the interpretation by
St
Augustine's /The Literal Meaning of Geneses/ which is a highly
allegorical interpretation (e.g. 'The words, "Let there be light,"
refer to the illumination and formation of intellectual creatures.').
Augustine posits the role of the angels in creation, and it is
"biblical" (Daniel and Revelation in particular) that suggest that
angelic beings "rule" or have authority or responsibility for
specified regions of the earth. And at least since Justin Martyr
there has been a strain in the Christian tradition (that at least
in
practice won out, if not in the rhetoric) that has taken the pagan
religions as holding some part of the truth, that rather than being
demonic, the pagans instead held a part, however small, of the
original, true revelation of God (see Paul in Romans 1 and 2) and
that they simply got it wrong or misunderstand what they've
received, and so have worshipped the "creation", for example an
angel over country X, rather than the Creator, i. e. in their
simplicity and lack of direct, sustained revelation, they got
confused.
Tolkien and Lewis enlarged and adapted these ideas to fit their
own subcreations, but their roots are firmly in the patristic,
Christian tradition.
This inspiration from St Augustine is also the focus of much of the
Criticism referred to or quoted in the /Reader's Guide/ (RG):

Other commentators have suggested that Tolkien's
Creation myth reflects ideas found in the writings of St
Augustine. In 'Augustine and the /Ainulindalë/',
/Mythlore/ 21, no. 1, whole no. 79 (Summer 1995), John
Houghton compares the /Ainulindalë/ with Augustine's
interpretations of Genesis:

This is, I submit, an Augustinian account of
creation. . . . In both cases, God first creates
the angels, and then reveals to them the further
elements of creation; the angels' own knowledge
reflects ideas in the divine mind. In both cases,
as well, after the revelation, God gives real
existence to what the angels have perceived,
upholding that existence in the void; yet that real
existence has only the undeveloped potential of
what it will become in the unfolding of time, and
God reserves to God's self the introduction of
elements unanticipated in the basic design.
Granted these similarities, however, the two
schemata do contrast in two ways. First is the fact
that the predominant musical images function in the
/Ainulindalë/ in the way that speech and light,
taken together as intellectual illumination do in
Augustine's reading of Genesis. Second is the way
the Ainur act as sub-creators, developing the
themes proposed to them by Eru Ilúvatar, whereas
Augustine focuses on God as the sole creator.
[p. 7]
[RG p. 31f]

Hammond and Scull also describe how Brian Rosebury, in /Tolkien: A
Cultural Phenomenon/ (2003), points out the similarities to Christian
myth, 'with Ilúvatar, the Ainur, and Melkor in place of God, the
angels, and Lucifer' (is this, I wonder, an example of the use of
allegorical language?) And again there is a reference to Augustine,
in
the quotation of Rosebury's note that 'the basic Augustinian
apparatus
in which nothing is created evil, but evil arises from the free will
of created beings, is in place'.

Focusing momentarily upon the similarities, Garth has a comment to
Ilúvatar's assertion (in the Lost Tale, 'The Music of the Ainur'):

One thing only have I added, the fire that giveth Life and
Reality' -- and behold, the Secret Fire burnt at the heart
of the world. [BoLT1 p. 53]

Garth, in his epilogue, 'A New Light', points out an explanation for
the 'Secret Fire', by referring to the Quenya lexicon that Tolkien
was
composing at the same time as the Lost Tales:

The early lexicon of Quenya may shed some light on the
last statement, explaining that /Sa/[*], 'fire, especially
in temples, etc.', is also 'A mystic name identified with
the Holy Ghost'. Ilúvatar's further solo contribution is
the creation of Elves and Men, together with their
distinguishing talent -- language.
[GW p. 255]
[*] In the original it's an 'a macron' (with a bar over it).

It is, IMO, a compelling argument, and one that places unequivocally
Eru as the one with the Holy Spirit . . . (is the Holy Spirit
particular to Christian thought, or is it shared by Judaism and/or
Islam?)

One of the oft-seen complaints about /The Silmarillion/ is that it
feels too 'biblical' when reading -- not surprising given the above,
and it doesn't stop there. Anne C. Petty in /Tolkien in the Land of
Heroes: Discovering the Human Spirit/ (2003) points also the the
narrative style of the tale: 'Anyone looking for biblical parallels
within /The Silmarillion/ should make note of the highly formal, King
James-style diction.' (as quoted in RG p. 32). Personally it is one
of
the things that I envy about English: that it is possible to produce
a
text of this kind and nevertheless expect to find an audience for it.


A major difference of Tolkien's /Ainulindalë/ from the Biblical/
Augustinian interpretation, also noted by Rosebury and emphasized by
being quoted by Hammond and Scull, is, then, the level of
participation by the angelic 'helpers' -- Tolkien's Ainur. That
Tolkien, who is often named a 'medievalist', was intimately familiar
with St Augustine, and took inspiration from the saint's work to his
own cosmogonic myth is hardly surprising to anyone, but this only
emphasizes the differences between the two, one of which is the
level of contribution by the Ainur.

The contribution of the Ainur is sub-creative in nature as e.g.
expressed in letter #153:

So in this myth, it is 'feigned' (legitimately whether
that is a feature of the real world or not) that He gave
special 'sub-creative' powers to certain of His highest
created beings: that is a guarantee that what they devised
and made should be given the reality of Creation.
[Letters p. 195]

This invokes Tolkien's comments and believes about sub-creation, as
expressed in many places, and raises, to my mind, the question of
'why'?
To get back to the initial point- I think Tolkien postulated a
demiurgic creation to justify his belief that Man is a subcreator
in his own right (cf. Mythopoeia). The post-LR shift wherein the
Music formed the blueprint for Ea, not Ea itself, probably was
his way of reinforcing the idea that its Reality- its ontological
status equal to that of the Ainur- could only originate with God,
lest Tolkien be accused of a modified polytheism (or even
Manichaeanism, given Melkor's prominent role).
I'm not sure that the contribution by the Ainur can be called
demiurgic, if that is what is meant here -- they help shaping
creation, but I don't think they actually Create in their own right,
but leaving that aside, I agree that the unique position of the Ainur
in Tolkien's cosmogonic myth is directly related to his views on
sub-creation, the sub-creative urges of Man, and the potential of
Man's sub-creative effort for both redemption and condemnation.

As William points out, Tolkien had 'Views' regarding Man's role as
sub-creator, expressed, among other places, in letter #153

To conclude: having mentioned Free Will, I might say
that in my myth I have used 'subcreation' in a special way
[...] to make visible and physical the effects of Sin or
misused Free Will by men.
[Letters p. 195]

There is, in other words, a parallel between the role of sub-creation
in Tolkien's writings, and the Free Will of the actors (not only Men,
I'd say) -- through sub-creation the effects of 'Sin or misused Free
Will' become incorporated into Creation. But conversely the Free Will
can be used correctly, and the sub-creative effort can then come to
express some deeper truth about Creation -- something Tolkien saw in
myth and fairy-stories:

The peculiar quality of the 'joy' in successful Fantasy
can thus be explained as a sudden glimpse of the
underlying reality or truth. It is not only a
'consolation' for the sorrow of this world, but a
satisfaction, and an answer to that question, 'Is it
true?' The answer to this question that I gave at first
was (quite rightly): 'If you have built your little world
well, yes: it is true in that world.'
[T&L p. 71]

This brings me to what I think is an important point. Tolkien saw
sub-creation in art (perhaps in particular the literary sub-creation
as expressed in myth and fairy-stories) as capable of revealing a
glimpse of a greater Truth -- an attribute he also ascribed to pagan
myths, as Shippey points out in /J.R.R. Tolkien: Author of the
Century/:

Tolkien was presenting his Valar as benevolent
archangels subject to the One, the Creator, but he
showed a certain tendency towards identifying them
with the pagan deities of his ancestors [...].
Tolkien was by no means the first Christian
philologist to veer in this direction, and a case
could be made in his defence. C.S. Lewis's /Mere
Christianity/ suggestion about the 'good dreams' of
heathen religions has already been mentioned; and
Tolkien seems to accept something like this through
the 'splintered light' thesis embedded in his poem
'Mythopoeia' [...] [AC p. 292f]

John Garth points out that Eriol hadn't heard about Eru (the Creator,
the One, the Heavenly Father), but he did know about the 'Anglo-Saxon
gods /Wóden/ (Odin) and Þunor (Thor), whom the Elves identify as
Manwë, chief of the Valar, and Tulkas, their champion.' (GW p. 256?.)
Again the suggestion is a justification of pagan beliefs as being the
best they could do prior to the advent of Christianity.

But Garth also identifies what he sees as another of Tolkien's
purposes with the /Ainulindalë/, one related to the main theme of his
book:

Elevated subject and style should not obscure the tale's
pertinence to the terrible times Tolkien had known. It is
nothing less than an attempt to justify God's creation of
an imperfect world filled with suffering, loss, and grief.
The primal rebel Melko covets Ilúvatar's creativity where
the Satan of Milton's /Paradise Lost/ coveted God's
authority, a distinction reflecting Tolkien's aestheticist
anti-industrialism and Milton's puritan anti-monarchism.
Melko enters the void to search for the Secret Fire, yet
having failed to find it he nevertheless introduces his
own discordant music, brash but marked by 'unity and a
system of its own'. But in his collaborative Genesis, he
distorts Creation itself, as Ilúvatar reveals: Through him
has pain and misery been made in the clash of overwhelming
musics; and with confusion of sound have cruelty, and
ravening, and darkness, loathly mire and all putrescence
of thought or thing, foul mists and violent flame, cold
without mercy, been born, and death without hope.' These
ills (universal, though strikingly evocative of the Somme)
do not arise exclusively from Melko's repetitive music;
rather, they spring from its 'clash' with Ilúvatar's
themes. [AC p.255]

Once more I find that the underlying theme is that of Free Will
walking hand in hand with sub-creation, the power and the urge to
contribute to Creation by one's own sub-creative shaping of Creation.
Founded in Free Will, this may go either way -- for Good or Evil.
When
abused it leads to Sin and ultimately is the cause of falling, 'as
the
Diabolus Morgoth did' when he 'started making things "for himself, to
be their Lord"' (Letters, #153 p. 195). When used rather than abused,
it lets even the heathen sub-creator find some glimpse of God's
deeper
truth in his sub-creative Art.

To Men, in the aftermath of the Music, is given a sub-creative power
that is, in some ways, greater than that of both Elves and Ainur. To
Men is given a 'free virtue' to shape their own fate /outside/ the
bounds of the Music, and as Garth points out, 'without the agency of
human beings, Ilúvatar's universal drama would not reach
completion.'
(GW p.) Thus it is through the sub-creative powers of Men that
Creation will reach completion, so with this addition the
/Ainulindalë/ not only serves as 'an attempt to justify God's
creation
of an imperfect world', it also embues Men with the power to correct
this imperfection, and shows us how the sub-creative powers work,
both
for good and for bad, by the example of the Ainur (and particularly
Melkor, Manwë and Ulmo).

Having mentioned sub-creation in the /Ainulindalë/, one must also
acknowledge the manner of the sub-creative effort of the Ainur within
the Music -- i.e. by music. The role of music is of course central to
the tale, and one that is the first Hammond and Scull focuses on in
the RG in their summary of criticism of the /Ainulindalë/:

Several critics have remarked on the important role of
music in the /Ainulindalë/ as in other creation myths. In
'The "Music of the Spheres": Relationships between
Tolkien's /The Silmarillion/ and Medieval Cosmological and
Religious Theory' in /Tolkien the Medievalist/ (2003),
Bradford Lee Eden writes:

As a medievalist, Tolkien knew and recognized the
importance of music as an anthropomorphic reality
and creational material in many mythologies. The
medieval concept of the 'music of the spheres' was
grounded in ancient and classical philosophy,
discussed and theorized by Plato and Aristotle,
through the early Christian writers and the
third-century pagan philosopher Plotinus, up to the
eventual standardization by Boethius in the early
sixth century . . . as a classicist and
medievalist, the 'music of the spheres' concept
would have been deeply ingrained in his educational
training, and his Catholic background would also
have influenced his thought and creative processes.
[p. 181]

Eden also finds recalled in the /Ainulindalë/ 'the
medieval depiction of the various hierarchies of angels
singing continuously around the throne of God' (p. 185).
[RG p. 31]

I like this reference to 'the music of the spheres' -- that is, I
suppose, basically what this Music of the Ainur really is.

But Tolkien's love for music goes beyond that -- it is a love for
music (and for the sea) that goes through all his writings, not just
the /Ainulindalë/, and which seems also to permeate his own life: did
Tolkien love Edith in part because of her musicality, or did Tolkien
love the music because of Edith's musicality? His reminiscence in
letter #340 (written shortly after Edith's death) is, to me, deeply
moving:

In those days her hair was raven, her skin clear, her eyes
brighter than you have seen them, and she could sing - and
dance.
[Letters p. 420]

I suppose we don't need to go further into the why Tolkien loved
music
or the sea; he did love them, and that love is, IMO, ultimately
expressed in the prominence to which he raise them in the
/Ainulindalë/.


In their summary of criticism of the /Ainulindalë/ in the /Reader's
Guide/, Hammond and Scull notes how Anne C. Petty in /Tolkien in the
Land of Heroes: Discovering the Human Spirit/

also points out that this Creation myth 'is where the
seeds of discord are first sown and where all the
difficulties, conflicts, sorrows, and heroic efforts that
infuse Tolkien's fantasy originate. Here's the starting
point for the main theme of The Fall, as well as the first
use of the signature imagery of music and water, light and
shadow, that Tolkien used to carry this theme through all
three of his major works' (p 39). [RG p. 32]

It is here important to remember that the /Ainulindalë/, while
describing the beginning, wasn't itself the starting point of
Tolkien's writings. Therefore, phrases such as 'first', 'originate'
and 'starting point' in the above quotation must be read in their
story-internal sense only. One of John Garth's points is that all of
this originate, in the story-external sense, in two important facts
about Tolkien's young years -- his friendships in the TCBS and his
participation in the Great War. In that respect, story-externally, I
would rather say that the /Ainulindalë/ is where Tolkien justifies
the
insertion of all later difficulties, sorrows and heroic efforts that
infusesd not only his fantastic writings, but also the world he saw
around him. The /Ainulindalë/ is the point where Tolkien, by the
mediation of Melkor's sub-creative fall, explains the appearance of
the Fall in Creation, and were he makes the promise of salvation by
the mediation of the sub-creative powers of Man. The tale also
explains the allure of both music and the sea to Men -- justifying,
it
almost seems, Tolkien's own overwhelming attraction for music and for
the sea.



To round off all the quotations from the various scholarly criticism,
I
have chosen to turn again to Hammond and Scull's excerpts in the RG,
in
which they tell how 'Brian Rosebury in /Tolkien: A Cultural
Phenomenon/
(2003) finds the /Ainulindalë/':
a success both in literary and philosophical terms. Its
fundamental mythical conception, the world as a Great
Music made visible, its history a fulfilment of creative
purposes which proceed both directly from God and
mediately from him, through the sub-creativity of created
beings, dates from as early as 1918-20 . . . it is the key
to much else in Tolkien's religious, moral and aesthetic
vision. And its prose is at once appropriately
'scriptural' and distinctive of Tolkien. [p. 107]
[as quoted in RG p. 32]


For my own part, having learned to appreciate the lyric quality of
Tolkien's prose, I always find myself drawn into the tale, balancing
between being completely absorbed by it and keeping enough of a
distance to enjoy the language. I find that the attraction of both
music and the sea contained in the tale accentuates my own love of
both, but only in recent debates here, and after reading Shippey and
Garth, have I come to appreciate also the underlying themes of the
story -- in particular it's relation to sub-creation, Free Will as
well as the way it relates to Tolkien's more general views on myth.

I will not be so presumptuous as to claim that I fully understand the
position of these themes in the /Ainulindalë/, and I hope that this
introduction (perhaps the one I ought to have posted little more than
a year ago when I had the honour of introducing this chapter in the
CotW series) will encourage further comments and insights (as well as
pointing out where I have misundstood the writings of the scholars
from whom I have quoted so freely).
