Tolkien and the Bible

  • Douglas Estes

At first glance, the works of J. R. R. Tolkien’s seem devoid of explicit biblical references, but they are, in fact, heavily influenced by the Bible.

      J. R. R. Tolkien (1892–1973), often considered the “father of fantasy,” had a deep respect for the Bible. He infused his great works of fantasy with biblical themes and allusions and served briefly as an editor and translator of the Jerusalem Bible.

      How did the Bible influence Tolkien’s writings?

      At first glance, Tolkien’s epic fantasy works The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings seem devoid of biblical allusions or of religion in general, portraying a natural world of good and evil set amidst a struggle for life and death. Since their publication, however, readers have recognized the presence of significant theological undertones. Tolkien confirmed this: “The Lord of the Rings is of course a fundamentally religious and Catholic work; unconsciously so at first, but consciously in the revision” (Letters, 142). This influence is most explicit in Tolkien’s personal letters. However, even in his popular stories, Tolkien cleverly hid biblical ideas and theological expressions. He eschewed allegory and created a fairy story instead—a narrative mode that privileges immersion and absorption over the direct representation of theological themes by symbols and abstract personifications. The Bible heavily influenced Tolkien, but as a storyteller, not as a theologian.

      What allusions to the Bible exist in Tolkien’s fictional world?

      There are clear allusions to the Bible in Tolkien’s works. One occurs in The Two Towers, when Frodo, Sam, Faramir, and select men of Gondor sit down to eat. Before eating, Faramir instructs his soldiers and guests to face west in silence, an act that Tolkien himself describes as “grace before dinner” (Letters, 156). Faramir then explains their expression to Frodo and Sam: “we look towards Númenor that was, and beyond to Elvenhome that is, and to that which is beyond Elvenhome and will ever be.” This is a fantasized version of the Gloria Patri, a Christian prayer that declares the temporality of the Trinity: “Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, as it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be, world without end. Amen.” This prayer is based on a number of biblical statements, especially Rev 1:4: “Grace to you and peace from him who is and who was and who is to come” (see also Rev 1:8). As a Roman Catholic, Tolkien was quite fond of these types of liturgical formulas and occasionally used them to frame his thinking.

      Clear allusions also occur in Tolkien’s The Silmarillion, a slightly lesser-known work published posthumously by Christopher Tolkien from his father’s notes. The first two sections of The Silmarillion, the Ainulindalë and the Valaquenta, represent dual creation stories not unlike Gen 1 and 2. They also contain extensive allusions to the creation stories in Genesis. The Valaquenta opens with “in the beginning” (compare Gen 1:1) and uses symbolism such as light and darkness in a nondualistic sense (compare John 1:5). The Ainulindalë also unambiguously proclaims that Eru Ilúvatar, the name of God in Tolkien’s works, is “one” (compare Deut 6:4). Like the God of the Hebrew Bible, Eru Ilúvatar is sole creator and supreme ruler of the cosmos. In a famous letter, Tolkien, annoyed at critics who argued his works had “no religion,” explained that Middle-earth “is a monotheistic world of ‘natural theology’” (Letters, 165). Finally, the Ainulindalë contains a fall as in Genesis. It is not the fall of humanity here; rather The Silmarillion recasts the fall as a fall of the Ainur, a group of angelic beings.

      Tolkien’s works also contain more implicit allusions to biblical themes. Perhaps the most famous are scattered throughout The Fellowship of the Ring, wherein Gandalf repeatedly suggests that the other characters have been called together for a purpose and that there is a divine power at work beyond what these characters can perceive. These utterances echo biblical passages on divine providence (Gen 50:20; Rom 8:28).

      Beyond these, some scholars suggest that that Tolkien echoes biblical literature extensively. For example, Jonathan McIntosh argues The Silmarillion is a retelling of the Exodus story (except, with Elves). Other scholars have tried to push the envelope of Tolkien’s ideas, looking for more explicit allegorical allusions—such as seeing Eärendil as John the Baptist or Gandalf (or other characters) as Christ-figures—but Tolkien explicitly rejected these attempts (e.g., Letters, 181, 297).

      Tolkien made it quite clear that his works are fairy stories, meant merely for the enjoyment of his readers. But in their enjoyment, these tales whisper of biblical and theological concepts throughout, such that the reader is unwittingly immersed in biblical concepts—an unexpected journey.
