## §1 · FIELD SYSTEMS & THE HINTERLAND MOSAIC (the payload — 8 register rows)

### (a) The open-field core, measured

The open-field arable of a nucleated township was organized as 2–3 great fields, "usually several hundred acres each," divided into furlong blocks, each sliced into strips — CONFIRMED-digest (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-field_system search digest). The strip (selion) was "typically one furlong (660 ft) long and one chain (66 ft) wide, so one acre in area" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selion digest); the furlong length is "roughly the distance an ox team could plough without needing to rest: approximately 220 yards" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.fieldworthy.co.uk/guides/ridge-and-furrow-explained digest). The word furlong names BOTH the strip length and the block of same-direction strips — CONFIRMED-digest (https://ruralhistoria.com/2023/10/15/medieval-ridge-and-furrow/ digest). In practice strips ran smaller: on the Isle of Axholme they "averaged half an acre" — CONFIRMED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-field_system, fetched 2026-08-24).

A measured manor, Elton (Huntingdonshire, historic; the fetched page says Cambridgeshire per modern boundaries): 1,872 acres of arable; the abbot's demesne three hides "plus 16 acres of meadow and 3 acres of pasture"; a hide 144 acres, a virgate 24; the rest worked by 113 tenants; "a typical family might possess about 70 selions totalling about 20 acres scattered around the fields" — all CONFIRMED (same fetch). Rotation, three-field form: spring crops in one field, winter wheat/rye in the second, the third fallow, rotating annually — CONFIRMED (same fetch).

Holdings were SCATTERED — the 70 selions are not a block. This is the load-bearing fact for the drawn look: an open-field countryside has no per-family consolidated farms, no hedges between strips, and its visible grain is the furlong patchwork with strips inside it (ridge-and-furrow: "parallel raised ridges separated by furrows… usually of a furlong in length" — CONFIRMED-digest, fieldworthy).

### (b) The four-part partition and what governs proportions

The township's land classes are arable, meadow, pasture/common, and waste — with wood where present. "Adjacent parishes had entirely different proportions of arable, meadow, pasture, commons, woodlands and waste" — CONFIRMED-digest (search digest over https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/wilts/vol4/pp7-42 and https://miltonabbashistorygroup.com/medieval-peasant-life-in-milton-abbas-landholding/). The classes are functionally distinct: "Common meadow was land liable to flooding… permanent grassland in which individuals owned their own plots for growing grass to make hay. After the hay harvest, those with common rights could graze" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://acraew.org.uk/history-common-land-and-village-greens digest); "common waste land was uncultivated… grassland for sheep and cattle pasture" — CONFIRMED-digest (Milton Abbas digest).

Meadow was the SCARCEST and most valuable class per acre: at Domesday, floodplain meadows were "the most valuable of lands due to the natural fertilisation provided by silts deposited through river flooding" — CONFIRMED (https://floodplainmeadows.org.uk/discover/learn/history, fetched 2026-08-24). Elton's proportions show the shape: 1,872 acres of arable against a demesne meadow of 16 acres and pasture of 3 (the tenants' meadow is not in the fetched figure, but the order-of-magnitude asymmetry — arable in the thousands, meadow in the tens — is the confirmed pattern at that manor).

Even the most arable township kept grazing: the plough itself ran on oxen that had to be fed. This coupling (arable ⇒ meadow + pasture, mechanically) is PLAUSIBLE as stated — no source this lane phrased it as a law — but the corpus already carries its converse pieces: R-INST-2 §14's manorial sheep economy (Dyer's sheepcotes, the ditched barton) and §12's turbary over bog, both CONFIRMED there, are land classes a 100%-ploughed countryside cannot host (contest-B's own note on register row 1).

**Counterexample discipline.** Proportions are seed material, not a fixed ratio: the confirmed statement is precisely that proportions VARIED parish to parish. What does not vary: meadow pinned to flood-liable ground (see (c)), waste at the margins, and no class at literal zero in a tillage economy.

### (c) Floodplain meadow (register row 2)

The floodplain was the parish's hay ground, managed as Lammas meadow: mown first (private doles), then thrown open to common grazing after Lammas Day (1 August) — CONFIRMED (floodplainmeadows.org.uk fetch: "grazing animals would not be allowed on the meadows until after Lammas Day"; doles "often marked out using stones"; on many meadows "the strips were alternated in different years to ensure that everyone got equal shares"). Dole boundaries were "wooden posts or upright stones termed 'dole stones' or 'mere stones'" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://englandarchive.org/stewards/ancient-meadows-of-england digest).

Measured example: North Meadow, Cricklade — 24.6 ha between the Thames and the Churn; grazed in common 12 August–12 February; hay cut no earlier than 1 July; Hay Lot stones surviving; the system preserved by the court leet and documented to the medieval period — CONFIRMED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Meadow,_Cricklade, fetched 2026-08-24; the Lammas cycle there called "the oldest documented version of this cycle in England" — CONFIRMED-digest, Countryfile digest).

The engine-relevant inversion: the audited code makes damp riverside ground the BEST arable and clips fields to the bank; the historical regime made damp riverside ground the meadow — mown, never ploughed, and the settlement's fields began behind it. A riverside settlement's most distinctive countryside band is the meadow ribbon along the water. (Register row 2's convention is CONFIRMED as stated, by the two fetches above.)

### (d) Tofts, crofts, orchards and the settlement-edge belt (register rows 4, 8)

The nucleated village's own edge is a built land-use gradient, not a hard line: toft (the homestead plot — "house, yard, and outbuildings… typically spanning 0.25 to 1 acre within a hedged or walled plot"), then croft (the enclosed strip behind — "a larger area… of about one-half acre, enclosed for a garden and grazing"), then the open fields. Toft roster CONFIRMED-digest (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toft_village digest + https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/wharram-percy-deserted-medieval-village/history/description/ digest: "the house plots (tofts) and their adjacent strips of land (crofts) are grouped in planned rows"); the croft figure CONFIRMED (Open-field system fetch). The croft belt IS the enclosed close belt at village scale — every nucleated open-field village carries a ring of small enclosures by construction, even where the fields beyond are open. This directly serves register row 8: the strips must NOT abut the houses; the toft-croft belt intervenes.

In towns, the same gradient runs inside the plot: "shop/workshop at the street, living behind/above, garden/yard/service rear" — corpus-cite (DWR1A §6, CONFIRMED there via burgageplots.info). Orchards as a NAMED town-edge feature: one digest places them "in certain areas within the town and to the south of it" for a specific Wiltshire town — CONFIRMED-digest (https://apps.wiltshire.gov.uk/communityhistory digest, weak); London's extramural fringe was "essential… for the production of food" with market-garden demand documented from a 1345 gardeners' petition and dedicated plots outside the walls by the early 16th century — CONFIRMED-digest (https://read.uolpress.co.uk/read/the-margins-of-late-medieval-london-1430-1540 digest; https://www.encyclopedia.com/plants-and-animals/agriculture-and-horticulture/agriculture-general/market-gardening digest). **The universal orchard/close BELT at every settlement's edge, as a rule, remains PLAUSIBLE** — the confirmed pieces are the croft ring (villages), rear gardens (town plots), and extramural horticulture (large towns). The engine should draw the belt from those three confirmed forms rather than from a generic "orchard ring."

### (e) Field lanes and access (register rows 3, 4, 6)

Access machinery inside the open fields is confirmed at earthwork grade: HER inventories of ridge-and-furrow list "associated plough headlands, trackways, furlong boundaries and drainage ditches" as one earthwork package — CONFIRMED-digest (https://heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk/Monument/MBC47196 digest); the headland is the strip at the furlong end "on which the oxen were turned" — CONFIRMED-digest (fieldworthy digest); Warwickshire's HER records the compound monument class "Ridge and Furrow with Headland or Hollow Way" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.ourwarwickshire.org.uk/content/catalogue_her/ridge-and-furrow-with-headland-or-hollow-way, title and digest; fetch 403'd). Packhorse-era lanes descending to fields and between settlements wore into holloways — CONFIRMED-digest (https://museum.chobham.org/village-history/by-subject/roads/ digest: tracks "descended hillsides and have eroded into deep narrow holloways"). Droving access is implied by the partition itself: stock had to reach common pasture and the meadow aftermath without crossing standing crops — PLAUSIBLE as a stated rule (no source phrased it), but the trackway earthworks above are its physical residue.

For dispersed steadings: the settlement forms of Rackham's Ancient Countryside are "hamlets, medieval farms in hollows of the hills… cavernous holloways and many footpaths" — CONFIRMED-digest (Rackham 1986 quoted at https://www.alisonmorgan.co.uk/Books/Rackham%201986.pdf digest). The isolated farm and its lane arrive as a PAIR in every description this lane found; no source shows a trackless steading. (Register row 4's convention holds; its strongest statement remains the engine's own habitation.js doctrine, per the register.)

### (f) The intensity gradient (register row 7)

The theoretical statement is von Thünen (1826): concentric intensity rings — market gardening/dairy innermost ("bulky to transport, can potentially spoil quickly… high productivity per acre"), then wood for fuel, then grains, then stock ranching outermost; "farming intensity decreases with distance" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.e-education.psu.edu/geog597i_02/node/7 and https://www.geographyrealm.com/von-thunen-model-of-agricultural-land-use/ digests). It is a MODEL, post-medieval by date; label it the formal skeleton, not medieval evidence.

The medieval/early-modern empirical leg: London gardeners petitioned in 1345 to sell their own produce; dedicated market-garden plots outside the walls are evidenced from the early 16th century; "the vicinity of London pioneered commercial market gardening in Britain during the 16th century, when Flemish immigrants introduced intensive vegetable cultivation" — all CONFIRMED-digest (Margins of Late Medieval London + Encyclopedia.com digests, above). The corpus's intramural analogue is already CONFIRMED (contest-B's note on this row): Pantin's frontage-scarcity plan types and Hull's 1347 frontage-rent premium — grain fines where market value is highest. Applying that gradient outside the walls is the same mechanism; the DIRECTION (a metropolis's near-hinterland is the finest-grained and most intensively worked, not the coarsest) is safe; specific ring radii are NOT sourced and stay unminted.

### (g) Regional regimes and the no-mixing rule (register row 5)

Two independent, converging regionalizations:

1. **Roberts & Wrathmell's three settlement provinces** (mapped from 19th-c settlement data — that dating caveat is the atlas's own): a Central Province of predominantly NUCLEATED settlement "stretching from Dorset in the south-west to Northumberland in the north-east," with predominantly DISPERSED settlement in the Northern & Western and South-eastern provinces on either side; the Central Province "equates to the area identified by earlier studies as 'champion'" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://historicengland.org.uk/research/current/heritage-science/atlas-of-rural-settlement-in-england/ and https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/archives/view/atlasrural_he_2015/overview.cfm digests; the ADS page itself 403'd on fetch).
2. **Rackham's Ancient vs Planned Countryside** (Rackham 1986, extending Maitland 1897): Planned Countryside "stretched from the East Riding and Vale of York… sweeping southwards across the East Midlands to the south coast," with "a strong tradition of open field agriculture… then transformed by the coming of enclosure"; Ancient Countryside (Essex, Kent, the west and south-west) has "hamlets and small towns, ancient isolated farms," winding sunken lanes, thick mixed ancient hedges, and "did not include open fields or, if it did, they were abolished before around 1700" — CONFIRMED (https://ruralhistoria.com/2023/07/17/what-is-ancient-planned-countryside/, fetched 2026-08-24 — a secondary summary of Rackham 1986; primary not opened this lane). The south-east's small square enclosed fields are "a pre-Roman system" — CONFIRMED (Open-field system fetch).

The two maps coincide: nucleated village + open-field strips + late enclosure travel together; dispersed farms/hamlets + anciently enclosed hedged closes + holloways travel together. **The mixing law, bounded honestly:** what the regimes forbid is not any co-presence of forms — an open-field township always contains closes (the toft-croft belt, demesne closes), and the atlas's own caveat warns the Central Province's open-field uniformity is "not as clear-cut as has been suggested" (CONFIRMED-digest, ADS overview digest). What has no historical analogue is the audited combination: a countryside whose FIELD system is communal strip furlongs while its HABITATION is fully dispersed severalty steadings scattered over those same furlongs. Strip tenure presupposes the nucleated village whose families hold the scattered strips (the Elton structure, (a) above); a dispersed-farm countryside encloses. The engine's own header states this ("a field-barn-dotted open-field render is a pattern mix-up" — register row 5's evidence); this dossier confirms the two package-regimes it should draw from instead.

**What the engine may consume (§1).** Closed enums, owner-gated (tillage:1.00 is tuning-signed): `fieldRegime ∈ {OPEN_FIELD_NUCLEATED, ANCIENT_ENCLOSED_DISPERSED, UPLAND_STOCK}` — one DOMINANT regime per township, selected with the settlement form (§2), never independently of it. `landUseClass ∈ {ARABLE, MEADOW, PASTURE_COMMON, WASTE, WOOD, TOFT_CROFT_BELT, GARDEN_ORCHARD_CLOSE}` with weights per regime and seed; MEADOW is pinned to flood-liable ground along watercourses (never ploughed to the bank); WASTE claims the margin; ARABLE never 100% of dry ground. ## §2 · SETTLEMENT-FORM TYPOLOGY (register rows 10, 11, 13, 14)

### (a) The form roster, as the field knows it

Village morphology in the English literature is a closed family: settlements are **nucleated or dispersed** (Roberts & Wrathmell, §1(g) above), and nucleated plans divide into **row plans** (single or double rows of tofts "arranged either in a linear form as a single or double row along a central street, or as a cluster, usually gridded around a crossroads") — CONFIRMED-digest (https://hlamap.org.uk/types/8/rural-settlement/medieval-village digest); **green villages** (rows fronting a green rather than a street); **street villages** (the row along a through-road); **agglomerated/cluster plans** at crossroads; and **polyfocal** settlements — "settlements that do not develop with one single core but… have a number of separate concentrations" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://dmv.wordpress.hull.ac.uk/index.php/villages/terminology/ digest; the page itself timed out on fetch). Dispersion's own vocabulary is hamlets and isolated farms (Rackham, §1(g)).

Measured regional depth on the row/green forms: in County Durham and the wider north-east "up to 80% of settlements contain evidence of planning in their structure and layout," many showing "the regular two-row format, with the rows facing each other, often on either side of a street or a green," commonly attributed to post-Conquest (late 11th–12th c) reorganization, possibly after the harrying of the north — CONFIRMED-digest (https://dmv.wordpress.hull.ac.uk/index.php/durham-2/ digest); lowland Yorkshire's regular toft rows likewise "established in the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/030574887690150X digest). Greens could be PLANNED features: "in the later Middle Ages, some greens were planned and incorporated when new villages were laid out… serving as locations for markets and fairs or providing security" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://heritagecalling.com/2024/09/26/the-history-of-englands-village-greens/ digest; fetch 403'd).

Two consequences for the engine, both directly against register rows 10/11: (i) the LINEAR/row village is not an exotic variant — it is among the *commonest* nucleated forms, and in whole provinces the regular two-row plan is the default; (ii) regularity is NOT the town's signature and irregularity the village's — a 12th-century planned village row is more regular than most organic towns. Form and regularity are independent axes.

### (b) Planted towns, measured

- **Bastides** (SW France, 13th–14th c): "built to a strict grid layout with equal space allocated to each house, centered on a square which often contained a market hall and sheltered arcades" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.francethisway.com/tourism/bastide-towns.php digest). Monpazier (founded 1284 by Edward I): "a quadrilateral of 400 metres by 220 metres," streets parallel to the long sides crossed by four transversal streets; central arcaded place with market hall — CONFIRMED-digest (https://frenchmoments.eu/monpazier-dordogne/ + https://www.travelfranceonline.com/monpazier-bastide-in-perigord/ digests).
- **English planted boroughs** (Beresford, *New Towns of the Middle Ages*): laid out "on a grid pattern, with a central marketplace, spaces for religious buildings, and symmetrical building-plots of a standard size" — CONFIRMED-digest (http://users.trytel.com/tristan/towns/townint5.html digest). New Winchelsea (1280s): "a rental of 1292 shows that the grid pattern still evident on the ground was already occupied" — CONFIRMED-digest (same digest). New Salisbury: Old Sarum's hilltop abandoned for "a new grid pattern alongside Salisbury Cathedral" — CONFIRMED-digest (same round).
- **Zähringer foundations** (12th c, SW Germany/Switzerland): Bern (1191) "divided by three longitudinal streets, which stretched from the Castle to the city wall" — CONFIRMED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C3%A4hringerstadt, fetched 2026-08-24; the fetched article confirms only Bern and the spine-street parti; the broad-market-street generalization to Freiburg/Villingen/Rottweil stays PLAUSIBLE this lane).

The planted town's plan signature is therefore: whole-plan straight streets, standard plot module, the market as a DESIGNED void (square or spine street), one dated founding event. Organic accretion's signature is the opposite: the plan is a QUILT.

### (c) Recognition in plan — the Conzen apparatus

M.R.G. Conzen's town-plan analysis (Alnwick, 1960, IBG Publication 27) reads any town plan through three element complexes — streets, plots, buildings — and partitions it into **plan-units**: "areas of morphological homogeneity… each indicating a phase of urban morphogenesis" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240739085 and https://www.surf.com.cy/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/EPUM_BP1_Historical-Geographical.pdf digests). Its working vocabulary, definitions fetched this lane (https://www.burgageplots.info/glossary-of-terms, fetched 2026-08-24 — the same source DWR1A confirmed for the perch module):
- **burgage**: "the urban strip-plot held by a burgess in a medieval borough… charged with a fixed annual rent" (Conzen 1969), "typically 28–32 feet wide and arranged in series along streets" — CONFIRMED;
- **plot series**: "a row of adjacent plots that share similar building line and development characteristics" — CONFIRMED;
- **back lane**: "a road, lane, alley or footpath providing subsidiary, commonly back, access to adjoining plots" (Conzen 1969) — CONFIRMED;
- **perch** 16'6" (5.029 m), "used in metrological analysis of burgage layouts to identify regular planning patterns" — CONFIRMED; the corpus's measured case is Tewkesbury's primary plots of 4 × 40 perches subdividing by perch/part-perch widths — corpus-cite (DWR1A §6, CONFIRMED there);
- **market colonisation**: permanent structures gradually replacing open market ground — CONFIRMED; and the **burgage cycle** (plot infill to clearance) plus **fringe belts** as Conzen's dynamic concepts — CONFIRMED-digest (EPUM briefing digest).

An "organic" town under this apparatus is not formless: it is a composite of small plan-units — each street-and-plot-series package internally coherent (often metrologically regular), the UNITS differing in orientation, module and date, with seams between them. That is what the engine should mean by organic: a quilt of locally-regular pieces, not global jitter (register rows 11/12's cure at the research level).

### (d) What regular plot series do and do not imply; the extent curve

**Do imply:** one planning EVENT for that series — a lord or borough laying out a row at one date (the Durham two-row plans, the burgage series). **Do not imply:** a planned TOWN. Modular, rank-like plot grain "is described as ordinary English borough practice, not as a bastide signature" — contest-B's own flag on this dossier's scope, grounded in DWR1A §6 (CONFIRMED there); the diagnostic for a planted foundation is whole-plan coherence (one module, one orientation system, one date) plus documentation, never plot regularity alone.

**The extent curve (row 14's assigned half).** Contest-B confirmed growth at plot scale (Salisbury's rows: shambles 1314 → shops by 1455) but found no corpus statement that an earlier town occupied a smaller footprint. ## §3 · STREET-SYSTEM GEOMETRY (register rows 11, 12, 15, 16, 17, 18)

### (a) The junction signature

Network-analysis literature defines the grid by its junctions: "to be a grid, a street network must have an internally-consistent orientation, be relatively straight, and primarily comprise four-way intersections instead of three-way 'T' junctions or dead-ends"; regular patterns carry "a large proportion of four-way intersections (X-junctions)" relative to T — CONFIRMED-digest (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.00600 "Urban Spatial Order" + https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.09962 digests). The converse — organic self-organized fabric is T-dominant with dead ends — is carried at the same digest grade. A cross-era quantitative anchor, fetched: Altaweel, Hanson & Squitieri, PLOS ONE 2021 (89 pre-industrial sites, Bronze Age–18th c): orthogonal sites show "somewhat even or minimal centrality variation" while organic/hybrid networks are markedly more unequal — degree-centrality Gini "orthogonal sites averaged 0.47, while for hybrid/organic it is 0.71"; node density declines sub-linearly with urban area; organic/hybrid centrality "diminishing more rapidly than orthogonal settings" — all CONFIRMED (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0259680, fetched 2026-08-24). ⚠ PARTIAL clause: a medieval-towns-specific T/X percentage table was not found this lane; the engine gets the SIGNATURE (T-dominance, unequal degree distribution, short irregular blocks) at digest grade, not a numeric target.

### (b) The market as street event: widening, colonisation, islands

The organic market is a WIDENED STREET or a triangle at a junction, later colonized: Saffron Walden's excavated market shows 12th/13th-c gravel surfaces "then occupied by small and relatively impermanent buildings, consistent with a pattern of market infill," the infilling halting in the 14th c and "resumed and completed around 1525–1620" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://saffronwaldenhistory.org.uk/recent-archaeology-in-saffron-walden-town-centre/ digest). Atherstone's "block of housing between Church Street and Market Street represents an area of later market infilling"; "islands of buildings within the market place may represent the site of medieval shops which lay on the market" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.ourwarwickshire.org.uk/content/catalogue_her/market-place-atherstone digest). The corpus already holds the street-market and encroachment laws (R-INST-2 §2, CONFIRMED there: Oxford's market "centred on Carfax and extended into the surrounding streets"; Salisbury's rows) — cited, not restated. Plan consequence: the market void's edges are ragged with island blocks and row buildings; a clean rectangular plaza is the PLANTED signature ((§2(b)); Monpazier's arcaded place).

### (c) Back lanes and the burgage-series unit

The back lane is a TYPED member of the plan-unit, not an accident: Conzen's definition ((§2(c) — subsidiary rear access to adjoining plots, CONFIRMED). The canonical high-street unit is: street — plot series (frontages at the perch module) — plot tails — back lane. Where the series backs onto a wall or water, the back lane runs along the obstacle (the pomerium-lane reading in register row "minor streets penetrate the curtain" belongs to this family — the lane TURNS along the wall rather than crossing it; that specific wall behavior is the register's convention, supported here only by the unit's geometry, PLAUSIBLE as a stated street rule).

### (d) Dead-end structure (row 15's assigned half)

The corpus's rookery law (R-INST-6 §1.4/§5, CONFIRMED there from Beames 1852) already gives the deep structure: the densest organic fabric is "perforated by… courts and blind alleys, culs de sac, without any outlet other than the entrance" — every blind court has exactly ONE mouth on the public web; nothing is stranded. This lane's extension from the network literature ((a) above): dead ends are a normal ORGANIC feature (the grid definition explicitly treats dead-ends as anti-grid), so dead-end COUNT is not a defect signal; ZERO-mouth components are. Bounded engine rule: the street graph is one connected component per landmass-piece of the settlement; cul-de-sacs hang off it with one mouth each; stranded fragments and 20–31-component webs (the register's measured diagnostics) have no analogue in any source this lane or the corpus.

### (e) Gates, desire lines, and barriers

- **Main streets thread gates:** the road-to-gate correspondence half of register row 16 is carried by mechanism in the corpus (R-INST-1's murage funnel and the Statute of Winchester's guard at "every gate," both CONFIRMED there; contest-B's stamp). This lane adds the plan-level pattern from planted cases: Bern's three spine streets run castle→wall (CONFIRMED, §2(b)); Staines's "High Street oriented to the site of the medieval bridge" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staines-upon-Thames digest). The desire line gate→market→bridge as a NAMED generative rule is PLAUSIBLE synthesis over these confirmed pieces — flagged as such.
- **Frame composition** (row 17): a cartographic-craft rule, out of documentary reach this lane; noted as owned by a cartographic-convention tranche (contest-B's same assignment for the water-stroke row). PLAUSIBLE only: a sheet that shows a walled city shows its principal gates.
- **Barriers** (row 18): the corpus confirms fabric ENGAGING a defunct wall (Kloveniersdoelen absorbing the tower — contest-B stamp on that row); the street half (streets deflect along a surviving fragment; buildings back onto it) follows from the same mechanism — a standing masonry line is free wall and boundary, so plots colonize it as their rear — PLAUSIBLE as a stated street rule, CONFIRMED only at the building-scale precedent.

## §4 · TERRAIN RESPONSE AT STREET/DISTRICT SCALE (register rows 19, 20, 21)

### (a) Stepped and climbing streets

Steep sites carry their main streets as stepped/cobbled climbs: Steep Hill, Lincoln — "a steep, cobbled street," reported average incline 16.12°, "the main route into uphill Lincoln since medieval times," Roman in origin and "originally had steps" connecting the lower settlement to the plateau — CONFIRMED-digest (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steep_Hill + visitlincoln digests). Gold Hill, Shaftesbury: steep cobbled street, average incline 16.09° — CONFIRMED-digest (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Hill,_Shaftesbury digest). ⚠ Flag: one digest renders Steep Hill as both "16.12°" and "one-in-seven (14%)" — those disagree (16.12° ≈ 29%); consume the qualitative form (a main street may CLIMB at stair-worthy grade) and leave the number unminted until the primary is read.

### (b) Terracing: the ground itself steps

Where medieval communities worked steep slopes they TERRACED them: strip lynchets — "small unwalled contour ridges created by the ploughing of strip fields," following slope contours, with 'treads' and 'risers', formed by plough soil-creep; measured at Worth Matravers: "from between 70m to 360m in length, 3m to 28m in width and about 2m to 4.5m in height"; built "to bring marginal hilly ground into cultivation where the supply of good quality lower level arable land was in short supply"; concentrated in the steep-sided valleys of the SW, Cotswolds and North Yorkshire — all CONFIRMED-digest (https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1019951 digest, fetch 403'd; https://ruralhistoria.com/2023/06/06/strip-lynchets-medieval-features-in-our-landscape/ digest). This is the register's missing middle scale: between building (split-level, already CONFIRMED in DWR1A) and nothing, the GROUND steps in contour-following terraces — and a relief-1.00 site with untouched planar fabric has no analogue.

### (c) The waterfront/upper-town split

On steep coastal sites the shore quarter is a DISTINCT settlement element: Scottish burghs record "Seatown of…" / "Fishertown of…" as named appendages of later origin than the main town (Cullen, Boddam, Pennan, Petty); Cullen is "split into two parts — the Seatown… and the upper town"; Lossiemouth's Seatown is a discrete pocket of 52 houses; Gardenstown "is built on cliff ledges" — all CONFIRMED-digest (https://open.journals.ed.ac.uk/ScottishStudies/article/download/397/423/452 + wander/chaoticscot digests). District differentiation on a steep waterfront is therefore a FORM fact (two fabrics, two dates, two grains), not just a dress fact.

### (d) Switchbacked and oblique approaches

Steep ground breaks straight approach: medieval upland goods movement was by packhorse because of "steep gradients and treacherous going" (Yorkshire Dales) — CONFIRMED-digest (https://outofoblivion.org.uk/themes/transport/routes-trackways/ digest); worn tracks on steep hillsides survive as "deeply-incised earthworks of single zig-zag ditches, probably worn trackways in steep locations" — CONFIRMED-digest (Oxford Brookes trackway-typology PDF digest, https://radar.brookes.ac.uk/radar/file/5ae161bf-5495-43b5-ad2c-046b854af852/1/Typology%20of%20ancient%20trackways%20latest%20version.pdf); descending tracks wore into holloways — CONFIRMED-digest (Chobham museum digest, §1(e)). ⚠ PARTIAL clause: this is trackway-scale evidence; a measured medieval STREET switchback was not found this lane. The register's own §176/§5.-1c law (switchbacks as the strain idiom) already covers intent; this dossier grounds the zigzag as a real worn-path form on steep ground.

**What the engine may consume (§4).** A slope-response ladder, applied at street/district scale by slope band (weights, not walls): gentle → streets oblique to contour; moderate → contour-following streets + terraced plots (lynchet geometry: tread widths in the single-digit-to-low-tens of meters, risers 2–4.5 m — the one measured band this lane); steep → stepped street segments on the desire line, switchbacked vehicular approach; coastal-steep → split districts (shore row + upper town) with distinct grain and a stepped/zigzag link. Crag/outcrop marks are obstacles routes deviate around (the trackway evidence deviates; nothing this lane shows a road through an outcrop). The open-water stroke row (21) stays with a cartographic tranche — no drawing-convention source was in scope this lane.

## §5 · DRAINAGE & RIVER CONVENTION (register rows 2, 22, 23, 24)

### (a) Terrace-edge siting

The repeated siting fact: settlements sit at crossing points JUST ABOVE the floodplain — Devon's Axe valley: "historic settlements were sited at old river crossing points just above the floodplain… Axminster, Seaton, and Colyton" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.devon.gov.uk/planning/planning-policies/landscape/devon-character-areas/east-devon-area/axe-valley/ digest); Staines "built on gravel 'islands' that rise above the low-lying floodplains, with High Street oriented to the site of the medieval bridge" — CONFIRMED-digest (Wikipedia digest); at Sturry "the orientation of development was governed directly by the pre-existing orientation of a river terrace" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.kentarchaeology.org.uk/journal/137/… digest). River terraces are "benches or steps… represent[ing] a former level of the valley floor" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.britannica.com/science/river-terrace digest). The floodplain itself is the meadow (§1(c)) — which is WHY the water's edge reads as an unbuilt, unploughed ribbon on a real plan: the river bounds the buildable plan not as ink but as land use.

### (b) The far-bank rule (row 23) and the sea rule (row 24)

A built far-bank quarter exists only as a BRIDGEHEAD: Bedford's Town Bridge "connects the town centre… with St Johns, historically a separate settlement" across the Great Ouse; Londinium's Southwark was "the bridgehead in the southern suburb"; Huntingdon grew as a bridgehead where Ermine Street crossed the Great Ouse; Maidenhead materialized AROUND its 1280 bridge when the Great West Road was diverted over it — all CONFIRMED-digest (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_Bridge,_Bedford, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londinium, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Bridge,_Huntingdon, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maidenhead_Bridge digests). The direction of causation is uniform in every case found: crossing first, far-bank quarter second. A walled far-bank market quarter with zero crossings (the register's Baigou) has no analogue in any source this lane. For the sea: the corpus already carries the positive convention at PLAUSIBLE grade (a road meets water at a hard/ferry with a landing — contest-B's stamp on row 24's sibling); this lane's bridgehead evidence supplies the land-side half — the road TERMINATES at the crossing work; its continuation is a vessel or a bridge deck, never carriageway ink over water. (Negative statements — "no source shows X" — are this lane's search absence, not a fetched negation; labeled accordingly.)

### (c) Monotone descent (row 22's assigned basin half)

The corpus carries the local-gradient premise quantitatively (head, leats, mills-in-series — contest-B's stamp citing R-INST-2 §11); the basin half is geomorphic: a terrace sequence records a valley floor that only ever LOWERS toward the trunk stream (Britannica, (a) above), and a stream that entered and left a closed bowl over its rim would contradict the drainage that built the valley. Formalized for the engine as: along a drawn watercourse from entry to exit, ground height is non-increasing within noise, and the exit crosses the frame at the lowest edge crossing of that watercourse's valley — **PLAUSIBLE as a formalization** (the geomorphology is textbook; the specific rule text is this lane's synthesis; no source states it as a drawing law).

**What the engine may consume (§5).** Siting weights: nucleus and fabric prefer the terrace/gravel edge at a crossing point; floodplain cells take MEADOW land use, not parcels and not arable (ties §1(c)); the drawn plan's water edge is meadow-then-fields, not fields-to-bank. Hard-ish rule (proposed as a heavy weight + census, per §640.2): far-bank built area requires ≥1 crossing, and the far-bank quarter's size scales with the crossing's grade (ferry → hamlet-scale bridgehead; bridge → street-plus-series); corridors terminate at crossing works. Drainage: monotone-descent along watercourses with exit at the valley's lowest frame edge; tributaries join, never leave.

## §6 · SIEGE PRACTICE (register row 25)

### (a) The weapon envelopes, measured

- **Longbow:** Henry VIII's 1542 statute set a MINIMUM practice range of 220 yd (200 m) for adults with flight arrows; the longest 16th-c Finsbury Fields practice mark was 345 yd (315 m); a 150 lbf Mary Rose replica shot a 53.6 g arrow 328 m; a suggestion for Edward III-era professionals reaches 400 yd flight — all CONFIRMED (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_longbow, fetched 2026-08-24). A 12th-c Welsh-war account gives a "direct killing range of 200 yards" — CONFIRMED-digest (medievalchronicles digest).
- **Crossbow:** effective combat range 50–150 m, best under 100 m — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.tienda-medieval.com/en/faq/faqs/effective-range-medieval-crossbow-in-combat digest).
- **Trebuchet:** "most military trebuchets achieved 100–300 meters," large machines 300–400 m with heavy stones — CONFIRMED-digest (historycalc/scopu digests; enthusiast-grade sources, flagged).

The defended wall's danger envelope is therefore ~200 m for aimed archery, ~300 m+ for harassing flight shot and engines.

### (b) Siege-work siting, measured

The one measured English case this lane: **The Rings, Corfe** — the earthwork siege castle attributed to King Stephen's 1139 siege stands "300m south-south-west of the Butavant Tower of Corfe Castle" (National Trust HBSMR — CONFIRMED-digest, https://heritagerecords.nationaltrust.org.uk/HBSMR/MonRecord.aspx?uid=MNA136769) — the Historic England scheduling styles it "a ringwork and bailey and a Civil War battery 400m south west of Corfe Castle" (CONFIRMED-digest, https://heritage.dorsetcouncil.gov.uk/Designation/DDO17221) — sited "commanding the town, the castle and a main approach route," and re-used as an artillery battery in the 1640s. So a purpose-built siege WORK — the closest fixed element of a siege, closer than any tented camp needs to be — sat at 300–400 m: at the outer edge of aimed bowshot, inside flight-shot harassment, commanding the approach. Blockade lines sat farther and were vast undertakings: a typical fort-to-circumvallation distance "around 1,800 meters" — CONFIRMED-digest (https://www.syler.com/SiegeWarfare/onset/surround.html digest — hobbyist-grade source, low confidence, flagged); line LENGTHS for comparison, not distances: Leucate 1637, 2,400 m of works; Metz 1552, 12,200 m — CONFIRMED-digest (same digest round). ⚠ PARTIAL clause: no second measured camp-to-wall distance was found this lane; the band below leans on The Rings plus the weapon envelopes.

### (c) The band the audit row needs

Proposed, as weights: **tented camp** ≥ ~400 m from an intact defended circuit (beyond flight-shot harassment; The Rings' 300–400 m is a fortified WORK, not soft canvas); **siege castles/batteries** 250–400 m, dug in and commanding a gate or approach; **approach saps/assault positions** under 200 m only as trench/mantlet works, never standing tents. The audited ~55 m tent cluster sits at less than a third of the closest measured fortified siege work's distance, inside every weapon envelope in (a) — point-blank for a wall archer. Counterexample honesty: assault CAMPS could stand nearer where ground masked them (dead ground, the corpus's Lille-on-marsh caveat about sitings being terrain-driven), and a starving or shot-out garrison changed the calculus — the band is a default weight, not a wall (§640.2).

**What the engine may consume (§6).** `siegeStandoff` bands keyed to the defender's armament grade: camp ring ≥400 m-equivalent at bow grade (scaled by the map's own pace bar); works ring 250–400 m at a gate/approach with command of it; sap features only below 200 m. At artillery grades, push the camp ring outward (the Civil War reuse of the same 300–400 m work as a BATTERY, not a camp, is the era's own testimony).
