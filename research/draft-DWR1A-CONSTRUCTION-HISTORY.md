## §1 · THE BAY SYSTEM — the structural quantum

**Mechanism.** A timber building is a series of cross-frames (trusses) joined by longitudinal members; the space between two cross-frames is a BAY. The frame, not the wall, is the unit of construction: buildings were priced, described, and extended BY THE BAY. The bay is simultaneously a structural unit (it collects roof and floor loads to posts) and a plan unit (room boundaries fall at frames, because that is where posts and partitions can land). CONFIRMED: "Bays are the main link between structure and plan: they have a structural function in collecting loads" and "Each frame was pre-fabricated by the carpenter, and a carpenter's eye would see the component frames distinctly within the three-dimensional building" ([Building Conservation: The Archaeology of Historic Timber-Framed Buildings](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/timberframedbuildings/timberframedbuildings.htm), fetched 2026-08-23). The same source's survey instruction is itself grammar-relevant: bay lengths are "measured between upper faces of cross frames", and "many buildings taper, if only slightly, from one end to the other" — a real frame is not a perfect rectangle.

**The bay IS a surveyor's unit** (CONFIRMED, primary): Hewett's glossary, *Structural Carpentry in Medieval Essex* (Medieval Archaeology 6–7, 1962–3; [PDF](https://www.buildingarchaeology.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/Structural-Carpentry-Hewett.pdf), fetched and text-extracted 2026-08-23): "BAY — The length, in a timber-framed building, between two transverse frames, generally one perch." One perch = one rod = one pole = 16.5 ft = 5.03 m. Independent confirmation from a measured house: Bridge House, Lillingstone Lovell, Buckinghamshire, a box-framed open-hall house of c. 1500 — "In plan the building is 3 rods long by 1 rod wide, and the roof trusses are set on 3/4 rod pitch" ([Buckinghamshire HER MBC25394](https://heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk/Monument/MBC25394), citing Conlon 2005 Whittlewood survey; fetched 2026-08-23). That is a house 49.5 ft long, 16.5 ft wide, with ~12.4 ft bays.

**Cruck vs box frame — the two load-path regimes** (CONFIRMED, first pass, multiple sources):
- **Cruck**: paired curved blades run from ground to ridge; the ROOF LOAD travels down the blades, so the walls carry little — they can be flimsy (wattle, turf, later replaced in stone without touching the roof). Dominant in western/midland England and Wales, 14th–16th c zenith; ENTIRELY ABSENT from a broad eastern swathe (Kent/Sussex through East Anglia/Lincolnshire to the Humber) — a genuinely unexplained cultural boundary, not a resource one. Sources: [The Past: Cruck construction](https://the-past.com/feature/cruck-construction-an-uncouth-and-rudimentary-building-technique/), [Wikipedia: Cruck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruck).
- Cruck WIDTH CEILING: mostly used for buildings **no more than ~6 m (20 ft) wide**; the largest recorded true cruck span is **10.2 m** (Leigh Court barn, Herefordshire, late 13th c) — an extreme outlier. Crucks became obsolete around 1600 as two-storey demand rose (blades intrude on upper floors). Sources: [buildinghistory.org vernacular survey](https://www.buildinghistory.org/style/vernacular.shtml), [The Past](https://the-past.com/feature/cruck-construction-an-uncouth-and-rudimentary-building-technique/). Somerset corroboration this pass: jointed crucks and "true crucks" both recorded ([Williams, Somerset building materials](https://sanhs.org/wp-content/uploads/08Williams.pdf), fetched 2026-08-23).
- **Box frame**: wall frames tied by cross beams form bays; "the roof is a separate element which bears onto the external walls — in effect a lid on a box." South and east of England. Box framing permits FULL UPPER STOREYS and easy wing additions, hence its urban dominance. Sources: [Building Conservation: Timber Framed Buildings and Roofs](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/timber/wood93.htm), [buildinghistory.org](https://www.buildinghistory.org/style/vernacular.shtml).
- **Aisled frames** (CONFIRMED this pass): the great barns add OUTSHOTS/aisles on both sides of the mainspan — Hewett: "OUTSHOT — Area of a building which is additional to the 'bay', determined by the placing of any four posts. This may be either at an end or on one side; in barns, on both sides." Great Coxwell: "The sheer weight of the roof meant that the barn had to be built to an aisled plan, with parallel sets of internal posts running the length of the structure" (search digest, 2026-08-23). Aisles are how a frame gets WIDER than one clear span allows — the width ladder is: single span (≤ ~20 ft cruck, somewhat more box) → aisled (span + two outshots, 43 ft at Great Coxwell).

**The peasant baseline, measured** (CONFIRMED, first pass — [Current Archaeology: Peasant houses in Midland England](https://archaeology.co.uk/articles/peasant-houses-in-midland-england.htm)): the standard midland peasant house is **THREE BAYS**: central open hall bay (no upper floor, no chimney); one service bay; one bay with a ladder-reached upper sleeping floor — the function roster at the poverty floor is bay-mapped 1:1. Average floor area of surveyed cruck houses **881 sq ft** (~82 m²). Materials bill ~**111 trees** per cruck house, of which ~75 immature (≤6 in), ~30 medium, only **6 large**; only the eight cruck blades demanded mature oaks ≥24 in. Timber cost c. 1500 ≈ 10s; labour could double it; finished building **£2–£11, median £4**. Dendrochronology: built **1260s–1550s**, many standing 500 years later — peasant building was CRAFTSMAN-BUILT and durable.

**The Wealden form, as a bay composition** (CONFIRMED — [Wikipedia: Wealden hall house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wealden_hall_house), fetched 2026-08-23; [Geograph: Wealden Hall Houses](https://www.geograph.org.uk/article/Wealden-Hall-Houses), fetched 2026-08-23): "four bays with the two central ones forming the main hall open to the roof with the hearth in the middle"; cross passage with buttery and pantry at one end, parlour at the other; "the end bays each had an upper floor containing solars, which did not communicate with each other, as the hall rose to the rafters between them"; end bays jettied, hall recessed under one continuous roof; Kent and east Sussex core, built by yeomen "from the late 1300's to the mid 1500's". Geograph gives a three-bay variant ("usually of three fairly equal bays") and a 50° hipped roof pitch. Bayleaf (the Weald & Downland exemplar; page unreachable this lane, search digest 2026-08-23): hall of two bays; phase 1 dated c. 1405–30; a brick stack inserted at the service end early 16th c; hall floored late 16th c.

**Grammar consequences:**
- Room widths = building span (one room deep is the vernacular norm); room LENGTHS quantize to whole/half bays; total house length = bay count × bay width. The module is a SURVEYOR'S UNIT (rod) in the cultures that have one — the grammar should carry the module as a culture-axis parameter, with the one-rod default for `EUROPEAN_FANTASY_BASE`.
- Bays are not all equal: a plan is a sequence of FULL bays (hall, chambers, barn storage) and SHORT bays (smoke bay, stair bay, cross passage) — the bay list is typed.
- Prosperity expressed as BAY COUNT before storey count in cruck country; as storey count in box-frame country. Institutional scale (barns, halls) = more/wider bays plus AISLES, not deeper plans.
- Cruck regions cap clear spans ~6 m and resist upper storeys; box regions unlock 2–3 storeys + jetties; aisling is the width escape for both. A settlement-culture axis can legitimately swap the WHOLE vertical grammar on this one mechanism.
- The hall's two-bay length versus the end bays' one-bay length is a PARTI SIGNATURE (Wealden); the open truss in the hall's middle is a fixture the parti owns.

## §2 · MATERIALS BY REGION AND PROSPERITY

**Mechanism.** Pre-modern transport made bulk materials LOCAL by default: buildings are built from what the parish yields (timber, clay, chalk, flint, limestone) unless wealth pays for carriage or the law compels masonry. Regional geology therefore IS the material map, and prosperity buys (a) more of the good local material, (b) imported/processed material (dressed stone, brick, tile, glass), (c) durability. CONFIRMED regional pattern for England (first pass): the Jurassic limestone belt (Lincoln Edge → Cotswolds) = stone country flourishing in 17th-c ashlar/coursed rubble; clay shires of the central midlands = timber + brick infill; the south-east Weald = timber; Devon/south-west = cob/earth. Sources: [Historic England blog: A Brief Introduction to Vernacular Houses](https://heritagecalling.com/2016/09/20/a-brief-introduction-to-vernacular-houses/), [buildinghistory.org](https://www.buildinghistory.org/style/vernacular.shtml), Brunskill's handbooks as the standard survey.

**Transport cost made explicit** (CONFIRMED this pass — Williams, Somerset): "The choice between stone or cob depended on the availability of suitable stone and on the wealth of the builder, particularly if transport was necessary. The importance of transport costs is well illustrated by the fact that Ham stone was extensively used in parishes within easy reach of Ham Hill: even quite small houses were entirely built of it." And status shows in WHICH walls get the good material: ashlar "often restricted to front walls, the back and even the gables being of rubble." Grammar: material quality is a per-FACE attribute keyed to frontage, not a per-building constant.

**Wall-thickness ladder** (CONFIRMED this pass — Williams, Somerset, measured statements): poor slaty rubble on Exmoor: "necessary well into the 17th century to build walls up to 30 in. thick — as would have been usual in the medieval period — at a time when walls built with better-quality stone were normally c. 2 ft thick"; the abbey masons at the Priest's House, Muchelney built "walls only c. 2 ft thick in the 14th century; such thin walls are exceptional at so early a date." Cob: "the thickness of the cob was usually over 2 ft; there was usually internal batter, the top of the wall being thinner than the base"; "The stone base may vary from a couple of feet to nearly half the height of the wall." Cornwall corroboration (search digest, 2026-08-23, cob-specialist trade source): cob walls ~600 mm typical, 600–1000 mm range, stone plinth from 450 mm up. So the ladder is: cob ≥24 in (600–1000 mm, battered) → poor rubble 30 in → good rubble/ashlar 24 in → (brick and framed walls thinner; PLAUSIBLE ~9–14 in for solid brick, not sourced this lane). Grammar: wall thickness is a material-keyed constant that eats interior area — a 30 in wall on a 16.5 ft span loses 30% of the width a framed wall would give.

**Wattle and daub** (CONFIRMED, first pass — [Building Conservation: Wattle and Daub](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/wattleanddaub/wattleanddaub.htm), [Weald & Downland](https://www.wealddown.co.uk/museum-news/wattle-and-daub/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wattle_and_daub)): infill, not structure; panels cheap, repairable, long-lived WHEN MAINTAINED. Grammar: wall material of the frame ≠ wall material of the panel; wear grades attach to panels independently of frame. Wealden confirmation: "brick infilling between timbers, sometimes leading to a complete replacement of the outer walls" (Wikipedia, fetched 2026-08-23) — the panel-upgrade path ends in a whole new skin on the old frame.

**Cob/earth** (CONFIRMED — buildinghistory.org first pass; Williams this pass): mass unbaked-earth walling; earliest surviving c. 1300; "Cob is extremely durable if it is adequately protected by being placed on a stone base under a watertight covering" (Williams — the "good hat and boots" doctrine, now sourced); "until the early 17th century was used also in many better-quality houses", thereafter "at the lower social levels and for farm buildings… until comparatively recent times"; "It is not uncommon for a later casing of stone or brick to have been added to a cob wall, or for the cob to have been completely replaced." Regions: Devon, Somerset west, Cumbria, E. Midlands, Hampshire, Ireland. Grammar: cob's status trajectory is DOWNWARD over time (a material that was middling in 1550 is a poverty marker by 1750) — materials carry a date-dependent status rung.

**Stone** (CONFIRMED): party walls compelled in London from 1189 (§6); the stone belt's yeoman housing goes masonry in the 17th c; Somerset's masonry vernacular predates that where stone is at hand (Williams: lias, Ham stone, chert rubble, red sandstone, each within its own parish radius). Rubble is "finely dressed" only "in the hands of an expert mason" — skilled labour is the gating input. Grammar: stone unlocks fire resistance and multi-storey but demands mortar and masons — a cost step above timber except ON the stone belt where it undercuts scarce timber.

**Brick — chronology now sourced** (CONFIRMED this pass — [Lynch, Brickwork: Historic Development](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/brick/brickwork.html), fetched 2026-08-23; Williams, Somerset; [Strutt & Parker](https://www.struttandparker.com/knowledge-and-research/the-history-of-brickwork-from-ornate-designs-to-industrial-evolution) and [Historic England](https://heritagecalling.com/2024/01/04/the-history-of-brick-building-in-england/) by search digest):
- Revival of brick-making in EASTERN England "in the late 13th and early 14th centuries" (Lynch); eastern-England medieval bonding "usually haphazard" (Williams).
- Tudor/Stuart (1485–1714): "only the wealthy and powerful constructed buildings with brick" (search digest); irregular bricks needing 15–25 mm joints; diaper patterns; twisted chimney stacks — brick's first VERNACULAR role is the CHIMNEY STACK in a timber house (the Bayleaf stack is brick, early 16th c — digest).
- West of England lag: Somerset "Bricks first appear as a building material in the 17th century, but were by no means common until the 18th century"; earliest brick building in the county 1635 (Gray's Almshouses, Taunton); Bristol's first recorded bricks mid-16th c "clearly an expensive novelty" (Williams).
- "The use of brick dramatically increased following the Restoration of the monarchy in 1660" (Lynch); 1667 London compelled brick/stone (§6); Flemish bond for face work from the 17th c (Dutch House, Kew 1631 the first correct facade); Georgian colour fashions; Victorian mechanization: "More bricks were made and laid than during all the previous periods" (Lynch).
- Grammar: brick has a REGIONAL date of arrival (east c. 1300 elite, west c. 1630 elite; vernacular a century behind in each), and enters a timber house STACK-FIRST, then as nogging, then as a casing, then as walling — a four-rung adoption ladder that is also a renovation vocabulary.

**Fire as the material forcing-function** (CONFIRMED): London banned thatch after the 1212 fire ([buildinghistory.org regulations](https://www.buildinghistory.org/regulations.shtml)); the 1666 fire converted London to brick/stone BY STATUTE (§6). Grammar: fire events are dated shocks that flip a settlement's material register within a generation — a mechanism the simulation's calamity history can drive directly.

**Consequences for the grammar:** material sets wall thickness (interior area lost per foot of wall — now a measured ladder), storey ceiling (cob/wattle low, box-timber/brick high), fire propagation class, and the renovation vocabulary (panel replacement → casing → whole-wall rebuild). Prosperity ladder within one settlement: same PLAN, upgraded skin (daub → brick nogging → stone/brick facing, front face first) — material is a WEAR/WEALTH SKIN over a stable plan grammar, which is the stable-anchors law's friend. Material status is date-dependent (cob's descent; brick's ascent-then-ubiquity).

## §3 · THE HEARTH AND CHIMNEY — the plan-driving evolution

**Stage 1 — the open hall** (CONFIRMED): the hall is open to the roof BECAUSE the hearth is central and open; smoke rises and leaves by louvre/vents ([Building Conservation: Fireplaces, Stoves and Chimneys](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/fireplaces/fireplaces.htm)). The central hearth FORBIDS an upper floor over the hall. Smoke-blackened rafters are the diagnostic fossil — now CONFIRMED by three independent witnesses this pass: Bridge Cottage (1436) keeps "soot blackened timbers of the original roof" above its inserted floor ([Early Oak: Bridge Cottage](https://www.earlyoakreproductions.co.uk/news-blog/historic-britain/bridge-cottage-a-c15th-wealden-hall-house-floored-over.php), fetched 2026-08-23); 4 Blacksmiths Lane, Kelham, a common-rafter roof "blackened from a period, before the insertion of the upper floors" ([Triskele Heritage, dating blog](https://triskeleheritage.triskelepublishing.com/2023/01/24/mediaeval-mythbusting-blog-19-dating-dilemmas/), fetched 2026-08-23); and the Hildesheim/continental material below. Open-hearth thermal reality (CONFIRMED-digest, [Medieval Histories: Open Hearths, Ovens and Fireplaces](https://www.medieval.eu/open-hearths-ovens-fireplaces/), fetched 2026-08-23): reconstruction experiments reached only "17.5 °C at Moesgaard and 15.0 °C at Bork Harbour", and a great hall like Lejre needed ~55 tons of hardwood a season — the open hall is a FUEL-HUNGRY, cold form; the chimney is an economy as much as a comfort.

**Stage 1b — the intermediates: smoke bay and smoke hood** (CONFIRMED this pass — [Blue Willow: A History of Smoke Bays](https://www.bluewillowheritage.co.uk/post/a-history-of-smoke-bays), fetched 2026-08-23; Historic England vernacular-houses selection guide and the Surrey dating-features paper by search digest, 2026-08-23): a SMOKE BAY is "a shorter bay… sealed off from the rest of the building with wattle and daub infill panels to allow for smoke to escape" — the hearth's bay stays double-height while every other bay is floored; "By the 15th century, the introduction of smoke bays marked a transitional improvement"; the SMOKE HOOD is a timber-and-plaster funnel "built into the bay" that "drew the smoke and sparks upwards… out through a sort of chimney"; both were displaced by brick stacks. Recognition fossils: "doors or passages cut either side of the fireplace" and "the four original posts of the bay around the fire, often with notches cut into them." Dating (Surrey, digest): chimneys at vernacular level were once thought c. 1600; "there are plenty of examples much earlier than that, although few before 1540." Grammar: two intermediate STATES between open hall and stack — `SMOKE_BAY` (a short unfloored bay) and `SMOKE_HOOD` (a timber flue in a floored house) — each a dated renovation with its own fossils; the Surrey "few before 1540" is a bounded date floor for the stack in the south-east.

**Stage 2 — the chimney threshold** (CONFIRMED): "By the middle of the 16th century, masonry chimneys and fireplaces were replacing open fires… TO ALLOW THE INSERTION OF UPPER FLOORS above the main living space"; Bayleaf's hall floored late 16th c (digest); Bridge Cottage: "most owners sub-divided the great hall, both vertically, by way of an inserted floor, and horizontally, with partitioning… in the 16th century" with "brick inglenook fireplaces, with huge flues and chimneys"; Wealden houses generally: "In the 16th and 17th centuries, the open hall fell out of favour, and most Wealden houses had an intermediate floor and fireplace inserted" (Wikipedia). Geograph adds WHERE the stack went: "usually positioned between the front and rear doors" — i.e. INTO the cross passage, which is how the passage dies and the lobby entry is born. The chimney converts one tall volume into two storeys of rooms: the single highest-leverage renovation event a plan grammar can encode.

**Stage 3 — the chimney as plan organizer** (CONFIRMED — [Vernacular Building Glossary: lobby entry](https://www.vernacularbuildingglossary.org.uk/a-z/lobby-entry/)): front door opens into a small lobby against the flank of an AXIAL CENTRAL STACK, doors left/right into the two ground rooms, stair rising beside/behind the stack; **baffle entry** = same without doors. The central stack heats rooms BACK-TO-BACK on one masonry mass, replaces the cross-passage, and anchors the stair. Established in East Anglia by the early 17th c and spread west ([Lowestoft heritage page](https://lowestoftoldandnow.org/heritage/1500s-1600s-1700s/house-design-and-interior-arrangements-16th-18th-century), [Historic Ipswich/First Period](https://historicipswich.net/first-period-construction/)); the New England "First Period" houses are this plan carried across the Atlantic — independent confirmation. Contrast plan: gable-end stacks (stone country) keep a through-passage. PLAUSIBLE (still unsourced): the service end migrates to a rear outshut/kitchen wing as the stack claims the core — though §4's outshut frequencies make this very likely.

**The Great Rebuilding — CONTESTED, both sides held** (CONFIRMED historiography, first pass): Hoskins (1953) argued a rural rebuilding surge **1570–1640**; Machin (1977) and Currie (1988) attacked it — impressionistic, regionally staggered, and "vernacular thresholds" may be ILLUSIONS of differential survival. Hampshire dendrochronology has since tested it ([Vernacular Architecture vol 38](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1179/174962907X248001); [ResearchGate: Rethinking the Great Rebuilding](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230360959_RETHINKING_the_GREAT_REBUILDING)). The Surrey "few before 1540" datum and Somerset's "first floor inserted during the C16th" (SVBRG, §4) are consistent with a broad 1540–1700 band, regionally staggered. **For the grammar**: encode the TRANSITION as a mechanism with a regionally-staggered date band, never a national event year.

**THE CONTINENTAL DIVERGENCE — the stove** (CONFIRMED-digest and fetched this pass — [Medieval Histories: Open Hearths, Ovens and Fireplaces](https://www.medieval.eu/open-hearths-ovens-fireplaces/), fetched 2026-08-23; Stube/Kachelofen search digests 2026-08-23 citing the academia paper *The "Stube" and its Heating — Archaeological Evidence for a Smoke-Free Living Room*, itself 403): Europe had THREE heating regimes, not one. (a) North and east: "open hearths located in the centre of the modest houses and halls of both peasants and lords dominated." (b) West (France, England): "in the mid 12th century English and French nobles and burghers began to construct castles and stone-houses while fitting them with fireplaces" — "The earliest English chimney was constructed at Conisbrough Keep in Yorkshire around 1185"; the wall fireplace + flue regime that the English vernacular adopts four centuries later. (c) Southern Germany and east of the Rhine, 11th–13th c, widespread by the 13th (Hildesheim excavations 1986–87, >10,000 tile fragments from the 13th c on): the TWO-ROOM STOVE SYSTEM — "a wall was erected between the place where the fire was fed and the oven itself. On one side of the wall, the opening into the oven was fitted with a stone bench used as kitchen... On the other side of the partition, the oven — now turned into a stove — stood as a potted or tiled hollow mass, radiating heat into the new invention: the 'stube' (heated living room)." The smoke stays in the RAUCHKÜCHE (smoke kitchen), which doubles as the smoke-curing loft feed (digest: hams hung in the attic above the smoke kitchen), while the STUBE is smoke-free and glazed. The plan consequence, stated by the source: "When the new heating arrangements, however, became available, part of the entrance hall or workshop would be portioned off. Here a staircase would be fitted, leading to the first floor where new living quarters might be furnished with glazed windows opening up to the street." By the 15th c many central-European houses ran both: fireplace for cooking, stove for heating (digest).

Grammar consequence (this is the setting-agnostic payoff): the English mechanism is "ONE masonry mass in the core, rooms back-to-back around it, stair beside it, floor over the hall"; the stove mechanism is "a PARTITION between a smoky service room and a clean heated room, the stove body straddling the wall, stair fitted in the partitioned-off hall." Both are the same abstract event — HEAT SOURCE ENCLOSED → HALL CAN BE FLOORED AND PARTITIONED — with a different anchor object (axial stack vs stove-wall) and a different adjacency (hearth-room/kitchen separation is MANDATORY under the stove regime, optional under the stack). The culture axis should select the heat regime (`OPEN_HEARTH` / `WALL_FIREPLACE_STACK` / `STOVE_WALL`), and the regime selects the anchor and the adjacency rule — the mechanism travels, the style does not.

**Consequences for the grammar:** hearth technology is the master variable for: vertical use of the hall bay; entry plan type (cross-passage vs lobby vs partitioned hall); stair position (beside stack / in the partitioned hall); kitchen position (co-located vs mandatory separate smoke room); the RENOVATION event vocabulary (smoke bay, smoke hood, inserted floor, inserted stack, stack in the passage, smoke-blackened roof above a later ceiling); and fuel economy (the open hall burns tons). Prosperity axis: number of heated rooms = number of flues (stack regime) or number of stoves (stove regime), legible in the stack count or the tile-stove count.

## §4 · LOAD PATHS AND RENOVATION — which walls can move (DW law 5's physical basis)

**What bears, what does not** (CONFIRMED — [Building Conservation: The Archaeology of Historic Timber-Framed Buildings](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/timberframedbuildings/timberframedbuildings.htm), fetched 2026-08-23; Hewett glossary): in a framed building the CROSS-FRAMES (posts + tie beam) collect loads; the WALL PLATES and tie beams, joined by "the lap dovetail assembly between tiebeam and wall plate… an almost universal component of framed buildings in England and Wales", tie the frames into a box; "Infill panels between framing members and internal partitions do not carry structural loads — they simply fill the spaces created by the frame." Hewett's BINDER is the floor's cross member ("bridging-joists for a first floor, when athwart the building") and the PLATES are "Horizontal timbers set on the footings and carrying the wall-frames" — so a timber house's floor is itself a later, insertable layer of beams hung between frames. In cruck building the roof rides the blades, so the WALLS ARE NON-STRUCTURAL and replaceable wholesale (wattle → stone) without disturbing the roof (first pass, CONFIRMED). In masonry building the external walls bear and internal partitions are "more often of timberframed" construction (Williams, Somerset: "Internal walls, although sometimes of stone or cob, are more often of timberframed" — the stud-and-panel screen). The MOVABLE set is therefore: infill panels, internal partitions (stud screens), floors (insertable between frames), and the roof covering. The IMMOVABLE set: cross-frames/posts, wall plates, cruck blades, masonry external walls, and — once inserted — the STACK (the one masonry mass in a timber house; first pass, still PLAUSIBLE as a statement of practice but every recorded sequence below treats the stack as the fixed point of later phases).

**The alteration vocabulary, as recorded by building surveys** (CONFIRMED this pass, two independent corpora):
- [Wealden Buildings Study Group: properties already recorded](https://www.wealdenbuildings.org.uk/your-house/properties-already-recorded) (fetched 2026-08-23) — hundreds of Sussex/Kent houses each summarized in one line (e.g. "East Dean (C15): Three bays integral outshot. **What the two corpora agree on** (my synthesis over CONFIRMED tallies): the two most frequent alterations in both regions are INSERT A STACK and INSERT A FLOOR (the §3 transition), and they co-occur; the next tier is ADD SIDEWAYS — a CROSS WING (a new range at right angles, usually at the parlour end) or an OUTSHUT (a lean-to under a catslide at the rear); RAISE THE WALLS/ROOF (eaves lifted to make the inserted upper floor habitable) is a Somerset-visible third tier; STAIR TURRET (a small projecting stair compartment, often in the angle between hall and cross wing) and REFACING/ENCASING are the least frequent. Bay ADDITION lengthwise exists but is less common than wing addition in these corpora — plausibly because a wing adds heated, storeyed rooms while a bay only lengthens a single range (my inference).

**Definitions for the typed vocabulary** (CONFIRMED — [Vernacular Building Glossary: outshut](https://www.vernacularbuildingglossary.org.uk/a-z/outshut/) and [unit system](https://www.vernacularbuildingglossary.org.uk/a-z/unit-system/), both fetched 2026-08-23; [catslide](https://www.vernacularbuildingglossary.org.uk/a-z/catslide-roof/) by digest): OUTSHUT — "Subsidiary compartment at the side or end of a house or barn, under a lean-to roof; to be distinguished from an aisle, which is open to the body of the building"; CATSLIDE — a roof covering main slope and outshut "in a continuous slope… often ending up six feet or less from the ground"; UNIT SYSTEM — "an arrangement of two or more farmhouses sharing one farmyard" (⚠ SUPERSEDES the first pass's gloss; the unit system is a FARMSTEAD-level pattern — two households, one yard — not a house-extension mechanism; it belongs to the demography → multi-household mapping of §8, not here).

**Phasing evidence the grammar can emit as fossils** (CONFIRMED — Triskele; Building Conservation): empty mortices and peg-holes where a partition or floor once was; smoke-blackening above a later ceiling; straight joints and differing roof types across one building ("two distinctly different styles of construction with quite different periods of construction" at Sible Hedingham); carpenters' setting-out lines; a jetty under-built in brick. Triskele's warning is itself a law for us: "Just because a website says that a building is seventeenth century does not mean that it necessarily is that date" — pubs and estate agents exaggerate; the grammar's dated fossils should come from the simulation's own dated events, never from a style-lookup.

**Consequences for the grammar (law 5 made physical):**
- STABLE ANCHORS = the cross-frame positions (bay grid), the external wall line (masonry) or wall-plate line (timber), the stack once present, the undercroft (§9), the stair once in a turret. CHURN = panels, partitions, floors-in-bays, outshuts, wings, roof covering, facing.
- The renovation event list, ordered by recorded frequency (to be re-measured): `INSERT_STACK` ≈ `INSERT_FLOOR` > `ADD_CROSS_WING` ≈ `ADD_OUTSHUT` > `RAISE_EAVES` > `ADD_BAY` > `ADD_STAIR_TURRET` ≈ `REFACE_OR_ENCASE`; plus the §3 intermediates `SMOKE_BAY`, `SMOKE_HOOD`, and the §2 panel ladder. Each is dated, each leaves a fossil, each has a prosperity direction (insertions and wings are UP; subdivision-by-partition can be DOWN — §8).
- The hall bay is the CHURN HOTSPOT (floored, partitioned, stacked) while the end bays are the quiet ones — re-derivation should perturb the hall bay's contents before anything else.
- Decline reads as: partitions multiply in a once-open space (subdivision), outshuts decay first (they are the newest and lightest), the facing is never upgraded. Prosperity reads as: wings sprout at the parlour end, eaves rise, the front face alone gets the good material.

## §5 · STAIRS — technology, status, and PLACEMENT

**Technology ladder** (CONFIRMED skeleton, first pass): LADDER to the one chamber in the peasant house (Current Archaeology); stone SPIRAL/NEWEL stairs the castle/church solution, migrating to wealthy merchant houses by the 16th c ([Spiral UK history page](https://www.spiral.uk.com/commercial/the-history-of-the-spiral-staircase/)); from the Elizabethan period the FRAMED NEWEL STAIRCASE with banisters in grand houses; 1600s exposed stairs typically CLOSED-STRING; newels decay from structure to decoration by the late 17th c ([Old House Online: Early Staircases](https://www.oldhouseonline.com/interiors-and-decor/early-staircases-winder-box-spiral/)); "dog-legged" first recorded 1703; straight/quarter-turn/dog-leg standard by the late 18th c.

**The Diminishing Newel thesis** (CONFIRMED-digest only — the paper on academia.edu returned 403 twice; search digest 2026-08-23 of [*The Diminishing Newel: The Evolution of the English Domestic Staircase M12C–L17C*](https://www.academia.edu/41988910/The_Diminishing_Newel_The_Evolution_of_the_English_Domestic_Staircase_M12C_L17C) and its companion [*Internal Stairs in Domestic English Vernacular Buildings (1200–1650)*](https://www.academia.edu/41989049/Internal_Stairs_in_Domestic_English_Vernacular_Buildings_1200_1650_)): stairs began HIDDEN and purely functional; as the hall became a reception space the stair became a SHOWPIECE at the point of arrival; the newel's structural role (the stringer of a spiral) gave way to decoration by the late 17th c — "the central wooden framework exhibiting elaborately painted grisaille and wooden carvings of heraldry" (digest quoting the paper). Grammar: stair VISIBILITY is a status variable independent of stair TYPE — a hidden winder and a displayed open-well are different prosperity rungs of the same function.

**Placement rules — now sourced** (CONFIRMED this pass):
- Lobby-entry houses: "A winder stair built next to the stack and supported by it was typical in a lobby-entry house, which became the dominant house form in the 17th century" ([Plymouth Colony vernacular house forms](http://www.histarch.illinois.edu/plymouth/folkhouse.htm), via search digest 2026-08-23; page itself reset the connection). East Anglian farmhouses: "a staircase inserted next to the chimney stack, more often than not behind it" (digest of the Henley/Baltic House vernacular research PDF, 2026-08-23).
- Stair TURRETS: "a rare contemporary stair tower in the angle of the parlour cross-wing behind the chimney" in an early-17th-c house ([Suffolk HER MSF37959 (BIL 045), Dower House, 116 High Street](https://heritage.suffolk.gov.uk/Monument/MSF37959), fetched 2026-08-23 — the original early-17th-c newel stair was replaced c. 1820 by a softwood stair "with moulded hand rail, turned newel post, and 'stick' balustrade"); SVBRG's Somerset tally records 7 stair turrets in one year's houses and the Wealden corpus ~30 (§4).
- Medieval stone houses: the vice (spiral) in a wall thickness or corner turret (PLAUSIBLE — standard doctrine, not sourced this lane; the Wealden corpus uses the term "vice").
- Continental stove houses: the stair is fitted in the portion of the entrance hall/workshop partitioned off when the Stube arrives (§3, CONFIRMED-digest).

**Consequences for the grammar:** stair = (technology rung by date) × (prosperity rung: hidden winder → displayed newel → open well) × (PLACEMENT rule keyed to the heat regime and parti): beside/behind the axial stack (lobby-entry); in a turret in the hall/cross-wing angle (storeyed hall houses, 17th c); in the partitioned hall (stove regime); in a wall thickness (stone, medieval); a ladder in the service bay's corner (poverty floor). The stair consumes a SHORT BAY or a turret footprint — it is a plan object with a bay cost, exactly the S6 input.

## §6 · URBAN CUSTOMS SHAPING PLANS — strongest section

**The 1189 London Assize of Buildings (Fitz-Ailwin)** — PRIMARY TEXT FETCHED first pass ([elfinspell.com transcription](https://elfinspell.com/PrimarySource1189.html)). Provisions: party wall **stone, 3 ft thick, 16 ft high**, each neighbour giving **1.5 ft of land**, costs shared equally; either may raise it higher at his own cost; neither may thin, breach, or arch into it without assent. Gutters by agreement, else each catches his own eavesdrip. A neighbour MAY build to block your view unless you hold a WRITTEN easement — light is a documentable right, not a default. Enforcement: the Assize of Nuisance ([British History Online](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/london-record-soc/vol26/xi-lxv)). After the 1212 fire: thatch banned; roofs to tile ([buildinghistory.org regulations](https://www.buildinghistory.org/regulations.shtml)).

**Mechanism**: the party wall converts neighbouring houses into a STRUCTURALLY SHARED ROW — co-owned infrastructure; plans thereafter grow DEEP and TALL, never sideways; windows exist only front and back. This is the deep generator of the urban terrace.

**Continental party-wall comparanda** (this pass; thin): Freiburg's medieval notarial registers record "Brandmauer" (fire-wall) histories ([Urban History: Towards a fire history of European cities](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/urban-history/article/abs/towards-a-fire-history-of-european-cities-late-middle-ages-to-late-nineteenth-century/DCF986C2797AAFB0A9B6F07DE549E501), abstract + footnote fetched 2026-08-23 — the article covers Vienna, Paris, Venice, Stockholm, London and argues "Fire danger was determined not only by building materials but also by forms of construction, by the everyday uses people made of flame"); the Coutume de Paris (written 1510, 190 articles; revised 1580, ~380 articles) "contained the equivalent of a building code, outlining rules for property held in common" (CONFIRMED-digest, 2026-08-23, from the CanLII New-France legal history; the mitoyenneté articles' thicknesses/heights NOT found this lane — PLAUSIBLE that they exist in the 1580 text; the modern Code civil's party-wall regime descends from them). Verdict: the PARTY-WALL MECHANISM is pan-European (London 1189, Freiburg, Paris custom), with London's the only primary text I hold; the grammar may treat shared walls as a universal urban mechanism with locally-variable dimensions, NOT an English peculiarity.

**Burgage plots** (CONFIRMED — [burgageplots.info](https://www.burgageplots.info/), [Northumberland Archives](https://northumberlandarchives.com/learn/burgage-borough-topic/what-is-a-burgage/), [Esselio](https://esselio.co.uk/burgage-plot/); [burgageplots.info: social origins](https://www.burgageplots.info/the-social-origins-of-the-burgage-p), fetched 2026-08-23): laid out in PERCH units; frontage **1–3 perches, 2 perches usual (~10 m)**, depths many multiples (Tewkesbury: "primary plots measuring 4 x 40 perches (1 acre), subsequently subdivided as units of perch or part perch width"); "The standard 'pole' measure applies to many of the plots fronting the High Street which measure one or more poles in width." Plot use zoning front→back: shop/workshop at the street, living behind/above, garden/yard/service rear. Note the same rod that modules the BAY (§1) modules the PLOT — a one-rod-wide frontage holds exactly one bay-width of house gable-on to the street (my inference, worth stating: frontage width ÷ rod = gables per plot).

**THE FRONTAGE-TAX NEGATION — RUN, RESULT NEGATIVE for England** (this pass): I searched for evidence that English medieval houses were taxed by frontage width (queries: "burgage plot narrow frontage tax myth evidence"; "Amsterdam canal houses narrow because taxed on frontage width myth or true"; "Hull plot dimensions fourteenth century rental feet"). Findings: (a) burgage tenure was "a fixed rent in money for a burgage plot" — PER PLOT, not per foot (burgageplots.info, fetched); the form is explained by the landlord's interest in multiplying frontages: "these larger primary plots were divided into narrower, smaller plots offering ever narrower units with street frontages offering valuable shop space to more tradespeople" and "maximise their return on square footage." (b) The Hull 1347 rental — "unique records on plot dimensions" — shows rents varying by LOCATION and by FRONTAGE as a VALUE variable (digest: frontage affected rent by ~36%), i.e. frontage priced the rent because it was worth more, which is the value mechanism, not a tax ([Urban History: Property rents in medieval English towns: Hull](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/urban-history/article/abs/property-rents-in-medieval-english-towns-hull-in-the-fourteenth-century/B2338717D80170523A1EFC55C9440D92), abstract fetched 2026-08-23). (c) The Amsterdam "taxed on width" story is widely repeated but even its popular echoes concede "It's debatable whether this was the reason for the narrow fronts, or whether the taxes came about after the building of these houses," and the 17th-c canal plots were "5–7 m in width — probably because it meant a maximum amount of houses had an entrance on the waterfront" (search digest 2026-08-23; no primary fiscal source surfaced; every instance traces to travel/aggregator pages). (d) The Scottish burgage review ([PSAS](https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/9608)) was 403 — not consulted. VERDICT: the narrow-deep plot is sufficiently explained by (i) rod-unit surveying and (ii) frontage VALUE under a per-plot fixed rent that rewards subdivision; the frontage-TAX causation has NO primary support found this lane for England and only contested popular support for Amsterdam. The grammar must not encode a frontage tax; it may encode frontage value. (The owner's §436/§437 wording "burgage frontage taxes" is thereby corrected at the research layer — recorded here so the correction is vetoable.)

**The jetty — CONTESTED purpose, honestly held** (CONFIRMED first pass — [Wikipedia: Jettying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jettying), [Designing Buildings](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Jetty), [Andy V Skinner: The Jetty — a Tudor House Myth](https://andyvskinner.wordpress.com/2024/08/15/the-jetty-a-tudor-house-myth/)): projection typically ~400 mm (up to ~1200 mm); candidate explanations — floor-area gain; joist counterweighting; weather-sheltering; shorter timbers; STATUS DISPLAY. The space-gain story alone fails because RURAL jetties are common — and the Wealden corpus confirms jetties on farmhouses throughout (§4). Emerges late 13th c; London banned jetties after 1666. Grammar: a box-frame-only, prosperity-plus-urban-density-weighted FEATURE with structural side-effects, not a derived necessity.

**The 1667 Rebuilding of London Act** (CONFIRMED — [British History Online: the Act's text](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/statutes-realm/vol5/pp603-612), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebuilding_of_London_Act_1666), [Lichfields](https://lichfields.uk/blog/2016/november/4/the-great-fire-of-london-a-history-of-master-planning-and-development-management)): all rebuilding in brick or stone; jetties and projecting signs banned; party walls mandated; **FOUR HOUSE CLASSES keyed to street rank** — 2 storeys on by-lanes; 3 on streets of note; 4 on high streets; mansions for "persons of extraordinary quality"; storey heights fixed by statute (third sort: 10 ft ground, 10.5 ft first, 9 ft second, 8.5 ft third). **Mechanism**: HOUSE HEIGHT AND CLASS FOLLOW STREET RANK — storey count conditioned on the street-hierarchy tier of the parcel's frontage, directly encodable.

**Consequences for the grammar:** party walls as a universal urban mechanism (dimension local); plot frontage in rod units sharing the bay module; storey count by street rank; light as an easement (windows only front/back in a row); fire law as a dated material flip; NO frontage tax.

## §7 · INSTITUTIONAL BUILDING PRACTICE — structural imperatives per type

- **Granary** (CONFIRMED — [Building Conservation: Granaries](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/granaries/granaries.htm), [Wikipedia: Staddle stones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staddle_stones)): raised on mushroom-capped STADDLE STONES (typically a 4×3 grid of 12) — vermin-proof, ventilated, sited in the open yard where the farmer can WATCH it. - **Tithe / great barn** (CONFIRMED this pass — Great Coxwell, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Coxwell_Barn), fetched 2026-08-23, + [National Trust](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/oxfordshire-buckinghamshire-berkshire/great-coxwell-barn/history-of-great-coxwell-barn) and [Historic England listing 1183045](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1183045) by digest; Hewett's five Essex barns): Great Coxwell — built c. 1292 for Beaulieu Abbey (Cistercian), timbers felled 1253–1291/2, **152 ft × 43 ft, ridge 48 ft**, Cotswold rubble walls with ashlar buttresses, Stonesfield slate roof, AISLED with six pairs of posts = seven bays, cruciform with a two-bay west porch (originally "the double storied office of the monastic official") and a small east porch; "In the centre of the barn is a threshing floor on which grain was threshed by hand with flails" with opposed large doors for "through draught to separate the grain from the chaff." - **Inn** (CONFIRMED — [buildinghistory.org: inns](https://www.buildinghistory.org/buildings/inns.shtml), [Wikipedia: Coaching inn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaching_inn)): courtyard parti — arched carriage entry through the front range; lodgings around the yard off first-floor GALLERIES; stables in a second yard. - **Parish church/temple** (CONFIRMED — [A. - **Bathhouse** (CONFIRMED, thin — [Wellcome Collection: Southwark stews](https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/the-bishop-s-profitable-sex-workers), [Low-Tech Magazine](https://solar.lowtechmagazine.com/2024/09/communal-luxury-the-public-bathhouse/)): furnace room + tub hall; co-sited with BAKERIES for shared heat; moral-zoning to the liberty/edge. Grammar: furnace adjacency, water adjacency, edge placement, bakery coupling.
- **Smithy — now sourced** (CONFIRMED this pass): a 12th-century smithy at Cheveley, Cambridgeshire "situated on the southern edge of the village beside the road that led into the high street"; the digest states smithies "were typically located at the edge of settlements due to the danger of pollution and fire, yet positioned near the main street for easy accessibility"; beam-slots along the edges interpreted as "lean-to sheds used for storing fuel outside the smithy to mitigate the fire risk of storing it within the forge itself"; the archaeological signature is "ditches and gullies, areas of intense scorching, furnace or oven superstructure, and hammerscale" ([The Past: Iron in the time of Anarchy](https://the-past.com/feature/iron-in-the-time-of-anarchy-investigating-a-smithy-site-forged-in-12th-century-civil-war/) — article 403, these are search-digest quotations 2026-08-23). Independent second site: Bower Farm, Mersham, Kent — a later-12th/early-13th-c smithy over "25m by 22m" with "an area of intense scorching, furnace/oven superstructure and hammerscale", ditches, gullies, and A WELL ([Kent HER MKE114406](https://heritage.kent.gov.uk/Monument/MKE114406), fetched 2026-08-23). Both are TIMBER/earth-fast structures, not stone — the "stone-built smithy" folklore of the first pass is NOT supported; the fire mitigation is SITING + fuel kept in an outside lean-to + water on site, not masonry. Grammar: edge-of-settlement, road-adjacent; forge + anvil + trough + well; a fuel lean-to as a mandatory outshut; wide door; single tall workspace; material follows the local register (no masonry override).
- **Warehouse — now sourced** (CONFIRMED this pass): Exeter's Custom House (1681) "the first building in Exeter to be completed in brick. It had a colonnaded front giving access to a storage space for the assessing of goods in wet weather and space above for records to be kept and to house the customs men"; later quay warehouses of five storeys (1834–5) with "brick arches leading to dry storage" cut into the cliff ([Exeter Memories: the Quay](https://www.exetermemories.co.uk/em/quay.php), fetched 2026-08-23). Owston's Warehouse, Malton: quay built 1725 "with a stone warehouse on it", "three storeys on the river front, two on the yard front" (a split-level warehouse using the bank), "a wide segmental-arched entrance" centred on the river front, corn storage with a maltings behind ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owston%27s_Warehouse), fetched 2026-08-23). West India Docks (1800–3): six-storey brick sugar warehouses with "brick partition walls" for fire containment, later three-storey and single-storey vaulted rum stores, the first structural cast-iron columns in London's docks (1807), Rennie's 35 ft-span iron roofs, and a security perimeter of "massive curtain walls, up to 30ft high, and a railed moat" ([Survey of London via BHO](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/survey-london/vols43-4/pp281-284), fetched 2026-08-23). Wall cranes giving external access to upper floors "from the early 18th century" (digest, Convoys Wharf Tudor storehouse listing); goods "unloaded straight out of barges into hinged flaps outside the doors of the appropriate floor" (digest, A London Inheritance). Imperatives: WATER-SIDE or road-side frontage with a loading face; vertical stacking of storeys with TAKING-IN DOORS per floor + hoist/crane; fire compartmentation (partition walls; vaults for spirits); security (a perimeter, a single gate); dry storage (arches, raised floors). Grammar: the warehouse is the VERTICAL bay-count institution (storeys over a fixed footprint), its loading face is a named elevation, its plan is a grid of columns (the iron-column era) or cross-walls (the brick era); split-level on a bank is lawful.

**Consequences for the grammar:** every institution carries one or two STRUCTURAL IMPERATIVES that fix its parti (granary: raised; barn: aisled bays + porch + floor; mill: process floors + hurst; inn: gate + galleried yard; church: spine + accretion; bathhouse: furnace + water; smithy: edge + fuel outshut + well; warehouse: loading face + stacked storeys + compartments), and the imperatives are where the law-1 rosters start. The institutions split into a LENGTH family (barn, church nave, inn range — more bays) and a HEIGHT family (warehouse, mill, tower — more storeys), with the courtyard family (inn) as the third parti.

## §8 · MULTI-FAMILY AND POVERTY FORMS — ⚠ RESIDUAL GAP on medieval letting

- **Medieval urban subdivision** (partially sourced): the burgage mechanism (frontage subdivision, rear-plot infill along alleys — §6, CONFIRMED). Letting practice: the Hull 1347 rental is the best quantitative source — "an important international port with unique records on plot dimensions", with rents varying by street and by owner identity, and "biographies" of civic and mercantile property-owners who actively developed and let urban real estate (abstract fetched 2026-08-23); the search digest adds "Competition for housing was expressed in subdivided tenements and the spatial patterning of rents, and it gave rise to investment in real estate development, probably by church as well as lay landholders" and that tenements "with shops and solars" were standard rental units with "The solar… on the first floor of the part abutting on the street, with a shop or shops below" let separately (digests 2026-08-23 of the Lynn rental and Pantin). ⚠ Pantin's *Medieval English Town-House Plans* (ADS PDF) returned 403 on both paths; the Lynn rental pages (two hosts) were unreachable. So: the EXISTENCE of shop-and-solar letting, subdivided tenements, and speculative rows is CONFIRMED at digest grade; the PLAN mechanics (Pantin's parallel vs right-angle hall types, the narrow-plot adaptations) remain at the first pass's PLAUSIBLE level. Recorded for P1b.
- **Back-to-backs** (CONFIRMED — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back-to-back_house), [Leeds study, Vernacular Architecture/T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03090728.2017.1398902)): late 18th c → early 20th c industrial north/midlands; ONE ROOM DEEP against a shared SPINE WALL, 2–3 storeys, one entrance, one window wall; grouped around COURTS. Leeds origin dated from 1787. Mechanism: maximum dwellings per parcel with minimum wall/frontage per dwelling — party-wall logic at its limit (three shared walls). Consequence: no through-ventilation — the sanitary case against them.
- **Cellar dwellings** (CONFIRMED — search digest, Manchester/Liverpool): basements let as the CHEAPEST habitable tier in industrial towns; elsewhere cellars stayed coal-holes. Grammar: below-grade habitation is a POVERTY MARKER of dense industrial settlements, not a default.
- **One-room rural forms** (CONFIRMED existence/regions via [buildinghistory.org](https://www.buildinghistory.org/style/vernacular.shtml)): the longhouse (byre + dwelling under one roof, animals at the downhill end), surviving examples c. 1650–1750, Cumbria/Dartmoor/Hebrides/Wales. The byre-end → parlour conversion as the classic prosperity renovation remains PLAUSIBLE (not sourced this lane).
- **The unit system** (CONFIRMED definition, this pass — §4): "two or more farmhouses sharing one farmyard" — a multi-HOUSEHOLD farmstead pattern (kin partition of a holding is the usual explanation — PLAUSIBLE); this is the rural counterpart of urban tenement subdivision: demography splits a holding, the yard stays shared.
- **Subdivision as decline** (from §4's corpora, my synthesis): the same PARTITION operation that upgrades a hall (chambers out of an open volume) DEGRADES a large house when each partition becomes a separate tenancy — the Great Rebuilding's inserted floors are the physical precondition for the later lodging-house. High-water (T2R) decline should therefore render as partitions + separate doors, not as demolition.

**Consequences for the grammar:** poverty forms are PARTY-WALL-MAXIMAL and ONE-ASPECT (back-to-backs, courts, cellars) in dense towns; ONE-ROOF multi-function (longhouse) in the uplands; SHARED-YARD multi-household (unit system) on farms; and SUBDIVIDED-GRANDEUR in declining towns. Each is dated to its register (industrial for the first; medieval–early-modern for the rest).

## §10 · MAGICAL INSTITUTIONS — CONVENTIONS FROM LORE (ODQ §438 leg; first pass)

**Discipline statement.** This section records how fiction and lore conventionally HOUSE magical institutions — as bounded structural possibilities in typed buckets — never as expression (no protected text, names, or creatures are copied; trope catalogs are cited as catalogs of convention), never as theology (temples are liturgical-functional only), and always with a real-world analogue where one exists, because the genre borrowed its buildings from history. Sources actually consulted this lane: search digests (2026-08-23) of [TV Tropes: Mage Tower](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/MageTower), [TV Tropes: Wizard Workshop](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/WizardWorkshop), [All The Tropes: Mage Tower](https://allthetropes.org/wiki/Mage_Tower), and [Kobold Press: You Find Yourself in a Wizard's Tower](https://koboldpress.com/you-find-yourself-in-a-wizards-tower-laboratories-libraries/) — every one of these pages returned 403 to a direct fetch, so the trope claims below are CONFIRMED-digest, not page-read; plus the fetched-and-extracted [Calderón et al., *An Archaeological Research of Alchemy in Prague and Bohemia*](https://www.parquelareja.org/storage/app/media/producciones/pcalderon-ecicari-agarcia-alejtman-mmanzano_an-archaeological-research-of-alchemy-in-prague-and-bohemia.pdf) (PDF, 28 pp., text-extracted 2026-08-23 — a conference paper that explicitly separates "original remains" from museum "set-ups"); and the chained-library digest. Anything else is PLAUSIBLE from training and is marked.

**10.1 The wizard's tower (`TOWER_SOLITARY`)** — CONFIRMED-digest conventions: "In many fantasy settings, towers are usually the homes, laboratories and/or schools of magic users. This probably goes back to the Middle Ages, when astronomers lived in towers to more conveniently observe the stars" (All The Tropes digest); the laboratory and library are "usually located up on the very topmost room" and "Nearly every wizard's abode has one, if not both" (Kobold digest); conventional contents "telescopes, orreries, hidden artifacts, secret passages, tomes of eldritch lore" (digest). The genre's own counter-note, also in the catalog: "In real life, high towers would not be the prime location to live or work unless you never descended, or if you wanted the isolation and protection… that being atop a high staircase brings" — i.e. the convention is DISPLAY + ISOLATION + OBSERVATION, and the genre knows it. REAL-WORLD ANALOGUES (CONFIRMED from the Prague paper): an alchemist's "laboratory and study room were located on top of the old tower, which together with its spiral staircase of wooden steps remain still intact"; a Bohemian tower house with "a room with high vaulted ceiling, with windows on three sides of the tower and fixed seats on the sides of each of them" used as an ORATORY, and "walking down to the basement by a stone winding staircase… three well spacious chambers plus a fourth somewhat smaller where the different laboratories were installed with corresponding tools and ovens of various sizes"; and Tycho Brahe's Uraniborg, an astronomer's house with its own alchemical laboratory (paper, citing a Rudolfine source). Typed buckets: placement `EDGE_OF_SETTLEMENT | HILLTOP | WITHIN_CASTLE | URBAN_TOWER_HOUSE`; vertical order (bottom → top) `CELLAR_LAB / STORE` → `ENTRY + KITCHEN` → `LIVING` → `LIBRARY / STUDY` → `OBSERVATORY / WORKROOM` (the top floor takes the light-and-sky function, exactly as the weavers' garret takes the light — §9); circulation `SPIRAL_STAIR` (the vice, §5); fixtures `TELESCOPE, ORRERY, LECTERN, PRESSES, WORKBENCH, FURNACE`. Structural honesty: a tower is a HEIGHT-family institution (§7) on a small footprint — law 9 says its storeys are a partition of the massing height, and a masonry tower's wall thickness (§2 ladder) eats the plan hard at small diameters (my inference: a 16-ft-external-diameter tower with 30-in walls has an 11-ft room).

**10.2 The alchemist's / enchanter's laboratory (`LABORATORY_FURNACE`)** — CONFIRMED from the Prague paper (the best-evidenced magical-institution analogue we hold): the Oberstockstall laboratory (Lower Austria) — excavated 1980 and 1993, dendro-dated 1549 by the door frame, coins to 1595 — sat "Hidden under the floor of the 'sacristy', a room next to the Oberstockstall Castle's chapel", and "This room, where 'the hole' was found, plus a second one located directly above that, accessible by a bulwark ladder are a unity conceived as a laboratory": a TWO-ROOM, VERTICALLY STACKED, LADDER-LINKED laboratory adjoining a chapel, with furnaces, crucibles, ceramic and glass vessels. The paper's general statement: "alchemy labs [are] unexpectedly found in basements, where they were hidden"; the visited laboratories "were equipped to work on alchemy, metals (metallurgy) and plants (herbal medicine). Some of them also had libraries, and a special room dedicated to praying"; the practitioners "understand their work-room as a laboratory and oratory at [once]"; the Prague cellar lab (authenticity partly reconstructed — the paper's own caution) has "three chambers with a long corridor that connects them", "In this large chamber with low ceilings, there is still a source that provided water", "clay ovens, work tables and scales", and "a large furnace with several extraction[s]". The paper also rejects the tourist "Golden Lane" as a lab site precisely because "a 'public', easily accessible place" is the wrong place for a hidden laboratory — a SITING RULE stated by the source. Genre conventions (CONFIRMED-digest, Wizard Workshop): alembics, furnaces, shelves of jars, books, a summoning circle, a cauldron, specimen collections, a workbench; located at the tower top, in a cellar, or a back room; hazard = explosion/fire. Typed buckets: placement `CELLAR | TOWER_TOP | BACK_ROOM | OUTBUILDING`; mandatory adjacencies `WATER_SOURCE` (well/cistern — as at the Kent smithy), `FLUE/VENT` (furnaces — the smithy/bathhouse furnace logic of §7), `FUEL_STORE` (the smithy's fuel lean-to rule), optional `ORATORY` and `LIBRARY`; hazard class `FIRE + FUMES` → the smithy's edge-siting logic applies when the lab is a public business (apothecary/enchanter shop) and the SECRECY logic (cellar, hidden stair, behind the chapel) when it is private. The medieval guild analogue for a "public" magical craft is the apothecary/spicer shop on a burgage frontage (PLAUSIBLE — not sourced this lane).

**10.3 The arcane library / scriptorium (`LIBRARY_CHAINED`)** — CONFIRMED-digest (chained libraries, 2026-08-23): Hereford Cathedral's chained library (early 17th-c furniture, the largest surviving); Wimborne Minster (1686, "all housed in a small stone room… accessed by a 600-year-old staircase"); Zutphen's Librije (1564, in the church of St Walburga, "rows of reading pews"); the mechanism: "A chain is attached at one end to the front cover of each book; the other end is slotted onto a rod running along the bottom of each shelf" and books "read on the lectern below." Conventions the genre inherits: books are VALUABLE and SECURED (chains, a single stair, a stone room — fire-resistant like the undercroft); the reading position is AT the press (stall system: presses at right angles to the windows with a bench between — PLAUSIBLE, the classic Bodleian/Merton stall layout, not fetched); libraries sit INSIDE another institution (church, college, tower) rather than free-standing until the early-modern period. Typed buckets: placement `UPPER_ROOM_OF_INSTITUTION | TOWER_FLOOR | OVER_CLOISTER`; fixtures `PRESSES, LECTERNS, STALL_BENCHES, CHAINS_AND_RODS, STONE_FLOOR`; light rule `WINDOWS_BETWEEN_PRESSES` (one window per bay of presses — the bay module again); security `SINGLE_STAIR_ACCESS`. The "forbidden section" of genre convention maps to the real locked press/chained rare-book subset (my inference).

**10.4 The temple of an invented faith (`TEMPLE_SPINE`)** — deity-doctrine bound: the ONLY lawful inputs are liturgical FUNCTION and craft ECONOMICS, and §7's Hamilton Thompson material supplies both: a plain rectangle with the altar at one end; a smaller cell added at the altar end; aisles by piercing the walls when population/endowment grows; towers, porches, chapels for "multiple altars"; cruciform plans for structural (tower) reasons; orientation a CULTURE CONSTANT. Genre convention (PLAUSIBLE — training, no fetched catalog this lane): the fantasy temple borrows the basilica/parish spine for "good" faiths and the crypt/undercroft for "hidden" ones — i.e. the genre's moral coding maps onto the ABOVE/BELOW-GRADE axis, which the undercity graph (law 7) already owns. Typed buckets: parti `SPINE_TWO_CELL | SPINE_AISLED | CRUCIFORM | CENTRALIZED (round/polygonal — PLAUSIBLE as the genre's "other" temple shape, historically the baptistery/chapter-house/Templar round)`; accretion events `ADD_AISLE | ADD_CHAPEL | ADD_TOWER_STAGE | ADD_PORCH`; orientation `CULTURE_AXIS_CONSTANT`; sub-grade `CRYPT_IF_GRAPH_ANCHORED`. Nothing in this bucket names a god, a rite, or a doctrine.

**10.5 The magical academy / college (`ACADEMY_COURT`)** — PLAUSIBLE (training; not fetched this lane — the collegiate quadrangle is the obvious real analogue and the genre's standard model): a COURTYARD parti (the inn's and college's gate + ranged court, §7) with hall, chapel/oratory, library over one range, chambers on staircases, and a separate kitchen/service court; magical specialization adds the §10.2 laboratory (edge-sited or cellared for its hazard) and §10.1 tower (observatory). Typed buckets: parti `COURTYARD_RING`; ranges `HALL | LIBRARY_RANGE | CHAMBER_STAIRCASES | SERVICE_COURT | LAB_ANNEX | TOWER`. To be sourced by R-INST-5 (an Oxford/Cambridge college plan is the measured-plan input).

**10.6 The teleport-circle house / summoning room (`CIRCLE_ROOM`)** — CONFIRMED-digest only for the convention (a "summoning circle" among the Wizard Workshop fixtures); no real analogue beyond the oratory (a clear-floored, vaulted, windowed room — the Bohemian tower oratory above). Typed buckets: a CLEAR-FLOOR room of one bay or more, `VAULTED | DOUBLE_HEIGHT` (spends law-9 vertical budget like a hall), `NO_COMBUSTIBLES` adjacency, placement `TOWER_FLOOR | CELLAR | DETACHED_PAVILION`. Everything beyond that is expression, not convention, and is left to the catalog's own entries.

**Consequences for the grammar (lore leg):** the genre's magical institutions are RECOMBINATIONS of four historical partis this dossier already holds — the TOWER (height family), the FURNACE LAB (the smithy/bathhouse furnace room with secrecy options), the SECURED LIBRARY (a stone upper room with presses on the bay module), and the SPINE TEMPLE (the church's liturgical-functional accretion) — plus the COURTYARD for academies. No new structural mechanism is needed; the new content is (a) a SECRECY axis (hidden stair, cellar, behind-the-chapel) that the undercity seam should own, (b) a LIGHT/SKY axis that puts the observatory at the top as the garret took the loom, and (c) hazard adjacencies borrowed intact from the smithy. Every bucket above is a closed vocabulary; the clerk composes; nothing names a deity or copies a text.
