**What this slice of the old law was FOR.** W11–W19 were the operating instructions handed to a writer at the defense, crisis and faith desks under the LICENCE test — the test that said a face is unlawful unless the card positively backs it. Under that test the bars did not need to point at anything that would be *falsified* by a breach; it was enough that the record was silent. So they fenced off the whole territory where the engine had no word of its own: what a wall is made of and where the fabric came from (W11), what you may call a body of armed men (W12, W13), how to read an engine label that happens to also be an English word (W14), which generic English nouns are safe when the row behind them may not resolve (W15), what you may say about money (W16), what a crisis does and does not entail (W17), what a stress record grants (W18), and what may be said of a faith and its standing (W19). Three of those — W14, W15, W16 — did the fencing by *prescribing vocabulary*: a table of always-safe spellings a writer could reach for without having to resolve the roster. That is the part the re-cut kills. Under NON-CONTRADICTION a word is lawful unless something denies it, and a safe-spelling list denies nothing; it was a house style wearing a truth test's clothes, and it is the measured cause of the corpus reading at 168 distinct words against the shipped 519. What survives is everything that points at a named field, a roster row, or a sentence the page prints beside the prose.

## THE STRIKES MOST LIKELY TO BE WRONG

These are the five a Fable reviewer should open first. For each, the case AGAINST the fold's decision, put as strongly as I can put it.

**1. W15-gen — striking the always-safe spelling list.** This is the biggest single strike in the slice and the argument against it is the strongest. The spelling list was not merely a house style; it was the *enforcement mechanism* for every label and alias trap that the fold kept. A writer who is required to reach for "the town's force" cannot accidentally write "the garrison" on a town with no garrison row — the safe word makes the floor-1 breach structurally unreachable. Strike the list and every surviving contradiction in W12a, W13a, W14-church, W15-sick and W15-seat now depends on the writer correctly resolving the roster at write time, which the programme has already measured that writers do not do: the first-Refute FAIL rate stood at 52 %. Worse, the fold's own evidence for the strike — `country` once every 21 words, 168 distinct words against 519 — is evidence that the list was being applied as a *requirement* where the bar's own text said "otherwise", i.e. a compliance failure by writers, not a defect in the law. The proportionate cure was to widen the safe list, or to say out loud that it is a fallback, not to abolish it and hope the roster read carries the load. If the corpus's FAIL rate rises after DEF-2, this strike is the first suspect.

**2. W12b — "never 'the garrison' from a Barracks".** The fold struck this on the ground that the catalog says "Housing for guards or small garrison" and `hasGarrison` fires on the row. But read the description again: it is a **disjunction**. The row asserts the building houses *one of two things*, and a face that picks the second states as settled what the row leaves open — which is exactly the shape of assertion floor 1 exists to catch. The flag is worse evidence than it looks: `priorityHelpers.js:46` is a name-substring matcher built to gate institution priority, not a prose licence, and the fold's own NEW FINDING #3 shows the engine keeps a *real* garrison concept entirely distinct from barracks housing — `Garrison` is a `required: true` city row described as "Professional soldiers. Noble or royal." Calling the men in a town barracks "the garrison" therefore imports a city-tier rung's meaning onto a town that has no such row, and a reader who then expects noble or royal professionals has been told something the tier roster denies. That is a contradiction of the roster, not a naming preference.

**3. W13e — "the muster roll" as a citation.** Struck as "a record-word bar; no field denies that a town that musters keeps a list." Two problems. First, the bar's condition was two-part — a `Citizen militia` AND an instantiated `Muster training` service — so on a town with neither, a muster roll is not a neutral list but a named institutional artifact of a service the roster does not carry, which is the same shape of claim as naming a watch that does not resolve (W13a, kept). Second, the strike's own guard rail is unenforceable in practice: "mention a roll, never a number on it" licenses the artifact and then asks the writer to stop one clause short of the sentence the artifact naturally produces. Every roll in prose wants a length. Licensing the object while banning its only interesting property is the least stable ruling in the slice, and the cheapest place for a floor-2 breach to enter the corpus.

**4. W14-gateduty — the self-contradicting record.** "Two surfaces, one page; a writer cannot be charged for agreeing with either" is a defensible rule of lenity, but it is too broad as written. The two surfaces are not equals: `safetyProfile.js:463-464`'s "no gates to bribe and no checkpoints to avoid" is a rendered sentence the reader sees, while the services entry is probability-gated (`p: 0.8`) and may not print on this town at all. The strike therefore licenses prose that a reader will experience as flatly false, on the authority of a record line that reader never sees. A narrower ruling was available and was not taken: suspend the bar *only where the services panel actually renders Gate duty on this town*, which keeps the teeth, costs the writer nothing real, and still does not charge the face for the engine's defect. As drafted, this strike converts a wiring bug into a standing licence.

**5. W12d — the occupation "says WHOSE" rule.** Struck as an ambiguity rule, on the reasoning that an unstamped possessor states nothing false and that floor 3 now lets an unnamed person act. But the occupation record *positively distinguishes* the two bodies it leaves on the page: `occupied.viabilityNote` keeps "Local institutions continue under oversight" while the occupation ladder tracks how firmly the occupier holds it (`contested` / `stabilized` / `vassalized`). Which body "the men at the door" belongs to is therefore not decoration — it is the rung. A face that lets the reader take the occupier's soldiers for the town's own watch conveys a firmer or looser grip than the panel prints, which W17b (kept, as a CONTRADICTION of the printed rung) says is a breach. The fold kept the rung test and struck the only rule that made the rung legible in prose. If the two are inconsistent, W12d is the half that should have survived.
