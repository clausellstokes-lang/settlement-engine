# Second check of a reader's flags against the town's own record (read-only)

You are checking flags that two cold readers raised on generated fantasy-town pages. Each flag says: "this sentence of the town's page is FALSE, because the town's own header (or another sentence of the same page) contradicts it." The count of surviving flags is the measure the project's owner judges the product by, so each verdict must be right and carry its evidence.

THE FAILURE YOU ARE HERE TO CATCH: a reader judged a sentence false from a summary header, when the town's own particulars make it true. Example already found: in a high-magic mountain metropolis with "trade access: road", readers flagged dock workers shifting goods "between ship and shore" and a harbour pilot as false (no water) — but the town has an "Airship docking" house, its docks are described as "airship-era dock facilities handling both aerial and surface freight", and its harbour master "regulates airship traffic". Those two flags were wrong. Hunt for that kind of explanation for EVERY flag: magic, an exotic or unusual house, a service brought in from a day away, a second resource, a named person's actual sex or seat, a crisis that is over, a figure the reader misread, a line that carries its own mark ("Stopped by the siege", "Curtailed", "Scarce", "Short of", "impaired") on or beside it.

THE OPPOSITE FAILURE IS AS BAD: do not explain a real contradiction away. If the page says X and the same town's record or page says not-X, and nothing in the town reconciles them, the flag STANDS. Fantasy as such is never an excuse; the question is whether THIS town's own facts make the sentence true.


## Rules of this check (they were applied to the read after this one; apply them the same way, so the two reads compare like for like)
1. THE PAGE AGAINST ITSELF: if the page prints X and elsewhere prints not-X about the same fact, the flag STANDS, even where the stored record shows which field each line came from. A record that contradicts itself still makes a page that contradicts itself. (Example that STANDS: "leans first on its fishing for its food" on a page that also says "It has worked out its river fisheries.")
2. A SECRET, A PLOT HOOK, A RUMOUR OR A RECENT-CONFLICT LINE states a new fact of its own: EXPLAINED, unless another line of the page or the header DENIES it (then STANDS).
3. A CALM-TIMES REASSURANCE printed in a town that the page itself shows in want ("a poor year in one does not empty the tables" under a food deficit with hunger on the page) STANDS.
4. TITLES: a title ending in "-master" or of the form "Master <craft>" held by a woman is NOT a fault (the product's own table treats it as fitting either sex); an OFFICE's title (Lord Mayor, Lord Marshal, Lord Warden, Captain-Mayor) held by a woman is NOT a fault; an everyday word that names a sex and has an ordinary pair (Landlord and Landlady, Dairyman, Goodman and Goodwife, "he" for a woman) used for the other sex STANDS.
5. THE READING COPY'S OWN FAULTS are never the page's: this older packet format is known to (a) print a repeated line only once in a tab, so lists look shorter than counts above them and a row may lose a label or a mark it shares with an earlier row; (b) drop a history event's heading; (c) show plot hooks and "↳N member" rows under the wrong card; (d) print "hired: none" in the header's DEFENCE line where the town does have hired blades. Before a flag that rests on a missing mark, a missing heading, a short list or a misplaced row STANDS, find the passage in the rendered dump and confirm the page really prints it that way. If it does not: EXPLAINED, and say "reading-copy artifact" in the note.
6. A ROW JUDGED WITH ITS MARK: a house or service row that carries "Stopped by…", "Curtailed…", "Scarce…", "Short of…", "Impaired" or "Vulnerable" on its own line or the line beside it says its own state; a flag that calls it "on offer unmarked" is then EXPLAINED.

## For each flag, decide one verdict
- `STANDS`: the sentence is contradicted by the town's own record or page, and you found nothing in the town that reconciles it. Quote the contradicting line or field.
