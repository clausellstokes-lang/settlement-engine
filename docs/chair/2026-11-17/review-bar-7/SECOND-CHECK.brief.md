# Second check of a reader's flags against the town's own record (read-only)

You are checking flags that two cold readers raised on generated fantasy-town pages. Each flag says: "this sentence of the town's page is FALSE, because the town's own header (or another sentence of the same page) contradicts it." The count of surviving flags is the measure the project's owner judges the product by, so each verdict must be right and carry its evidence.

THE FAILURE YOU ARE HERE TO CATCH: a reader judged a sentence false from a summary header, when the town's own particulars make it true. Example already found: in a high-magic mountain metropolis with "trade access: road", readers flagged dock workers shifting goods "between ship and shore" and a harbour pilot as false (no water) — but the town has an "Airship docking" house, its docks are described as "airship-era dock facilities handling both aerial and surface freight", and its harbour master "regulates airship traffic". Those two flags were wrong. Hunt for that kind of explanation for EVERY flag: magic, an exotic or unusual house, a service brought in from a day away, a second resource, a named person's actual sex or seat, a crisis that is over, a figure the reader misread, a line that carries its own mark ("Stopped by the siege", "Curtailed", "Scarce", "Short of", "impaired") on or beside it.

THE OPPOSITE FAILURE IS AS BAD: do not explain a real contradiction away. If the page says X and the same town's record or page says not-X, and nothing in the town reconciles them, the flag STANDS. Fantasy as such is never an excuse; the question is whether THIS town's own facts make the sentence true.

## Your inputs
- Your batch: `@@BATCH@@` — a JSON list; each item has `id` (the sentence's id, e.g. C22108), `town` (a seed such as `bar0-metropolis-calm-rand-45`), `section` (the page tab), `text` (the flagged sentence), `readers` (which of two readers flagged it: a, b or ab), `kinds`, and `whys` (each reader's reason).
- The readers' own packets: `/home/user/read7/pk/bar7-NN.txt` (twenty-two files). A town's block starts with a line `==== TOWN <seed> · "<name>" …` followed by the HEADER the readers judged against (CRISIS, GOVERNMENT, STATE, DEFENCE, HOUSES, TRADE, INCOME, FOUNDING, FOOD, LAND, QUARRELS, TENSIONS, PAST, PEOPLE), then every sentence of the page as `C<id> <text>` under `## <tab>` headings. Find a town's file with `grep -l "TOWN <seed>" /home/user/read7/pk/bar7-*.txt`. Read the header whole for each town, and read the lines AROUND each flagged id (a row's name, its description and its mark are often on neighbouring lines).
- The page as rendered, tab by tab: `/home/user/read7/render/dumps/<seed>.txt` (headings `== <tab> ==`). THE TAB IS DUMPED AS PRINTED: a row's cells are joined by ` · ` (a house's name, its category, its state; a service chip line reads `Services · <service> · <service>`, and a chip may carry its mark in brackets, e.g. `(short)`); a part of a tab that is opened by a click is printed under a line `[opened] <its label>`; nothing is de-duplicated, so a list under a heading `(N)` should hold N items: COUNT THEM before you judge a count. The dump also holds the printed export (`== PDF/<section> ==`) and the journal: the readers did not read those, but you may use them as evidence of what the page means.

Use small python scripts (gzip + json) to look up the exact field a reader's premise rests on.
- The readers' rules (what is and is not a flag): `/home/user/read7/BRIEF-REVIEW-BAR-7.md`. Read it whole first: you judge by the same rules, including its list "NOT A FLAG".

## For each flag, decide one verdict
- `STANDS`: the sentence is contradicted by the town's own record or page, and you found nothing in the town that reconciles it. Quote the contradicting line or field.
- `EXPLAINED`: something in the town makes the sentence true or plainly reasonable, or the reader's stated premise is wrong on the facts (quote the page line, header line or record field that shows it), or the rules' "NOT A FLAG" list covers it (say which rule).
- `WEAK`: there is a real mismatch but it is one loose word or a matter of degree that a careful owner would call minor, not a false statement (say exactly what is off).
- `UNSURE`: you could not settle it; say what you would need.
FIVE THINGS OF THIS READ. (1) A ROW WITH ITS MARK: the page marks a row the crisis touches (`Stopped by the siege.`, `Curtailed by ...`, `Scarce in the famine.`, `Short of <input>.` before a description; `(stopped)`, `(curtailed)`, `(scarce)`, `(short)` on a chip; `Impaired` on a card) and prints a legend that the descriptions are of ordinary times: a description that reads as an ordinary day is NOT false where its row, its chip or its card carries the mark; a service chip printed under a house whose own description carries the mark is covered by its card's mark (the card says the house is stopped; the chips name what it does); it IS a real mismatch where one tab marks a service (or a house) and another tab prints that same service under a house that carries NO mark at all, or where the page's own lines about the crisis say the thing cannot be had and the row offering it carries no mark on either tab: say which tab carries what, with the lines. (2) THE SAME FAULT ON SEVERAL LINES is judged line by line all the same (each flagged id gets its own verdict), but say in your note when a row is the same fault as another id of your batch. (3) THE SHORT MARK (wider in this read: a house's card or row opens with `Short of wool.`, `Short of iron.`, `Short of clay.`, `Short of its materials.`, or a service chip carries `(short)`): the page is saying that house has nothing to work. A flag that another line shows THAT house at work, or its good made, sold abroad or earning (an income row, a scene, a person's own line or secret, a chain's outputs, an export), STANDS where the flagged line is about that house's own trade, carries no mark itself, and NO OTHER house of the town that is not marked short makes that good: name the houses you checked and quote their cards. It is EXPLAINED where another house not marked short makes the good, where the flagged line itself opens with or carries the mark, or where the line says the good is brought in. A flag against the mark itself STANDS only where the header or the record shows the input at hand (TRADE imports that very input; LAND gives its resource as available and worked): quote the field. Check the reader's premise itself each time (e.g. if a reader says "stores 0.1 months", find that figure). Where two readers gave different reasons, check both. (4) WHAT THE PAGE NOW PRINTS BY DESIGN (the readers' brief, its paragraphs "NEW SINCE THE LAST READ, AND BY DESIGN", "THE HOUSE'S MARK ON A PERSON'S CARD", "VERY RARE SECRETS" and, where it has it, "THE RESOURCES TAB'S Nearby Resources BLOCK": READ THEM FIRST). A flag against something those paragraphs call by design is EXPLAINED (say which sentence of the brief covers it); a flag that is one of their "WHAT IS A FAULT" cases STANDS if the town's record bears it out: check the supply story in the record itself (`config.nearbyResources`, `config.nearbyResourcesDepleted`, `economicState.primaryImports`, `resourceAnalysis.gaps`, the house's card and mark in the dump). THE RESOURCES BLOCK IS PRINTED LABEL-FIRST: under "Nearby Resources" stand first the grounds a house proves (no label), then the line "Depleted" FOLLOWED BY the depleted grounds, then the line "Abundant" FOLLOWED BY the grounds still worked. Most of the first readers did not know this: a flag that read a ground as "Abundant" (or "Depleted") only because that word stood on the line AFTER it is EXPLAINED where the label-first reading agrees with the record's two lists: quote both lists. (5) KNOWN AND ALREADY BEING CURED: judge these as any other flag (a real contradiction STANDS), and OPEN YOUR NOTE WITH "IN FLIGHT:" when the flag is one of: a chain card that lists a mine under "Via" for a chain "From … (depleted)" while that mine is unmarked and at work; an income share printed plain ("Metal is smelted or worked here.") where the only smith is short of iron and a mine works; a maltster or brewer that "requires a grain surplus" at work where the food line is short; a forge scene line beside a smith marked short of iron; a history line naming "a rival lord" or "the reformers" where the town has none; a brewer who "hears everything the taverns say" in a place with no tavern; a soft "eats well" line where the food word is not Secure; an export or "Produced locally" pill naming a good whose every maker is marked short.
